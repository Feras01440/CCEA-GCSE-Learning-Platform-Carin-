Biology · Unit B1 · Section 1.4

What changes an enzyme's rate

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction in a living thing. Warm it gently and it works faster; make it too hot, or too acidic, and it stops for good. That is why dough rises in a warm kitchen and why a high fever is dangerous.

  • Name the optimum and say what happens on each side of it.
  • Explain a rising rate by energy and collisions, and a falling rate by denaturation, in the words the mark scheme rewards.
  • Read the practical's graph the right way up: the shortest time is the fastest reaction.
7 short sections8 checks1 worked answerPrescribed Practical B3

Section 1One graph, two explanations

The graph above is the shape every enzyme gives: the rate climbs, peaks, then falls away steeply. The peak is the optimum, the temperature at which the reaction runs fastest. The two sides look alike but happen for completely different reasons, and the exam asks you to explain each one separately.

The temperature at which an enzyme works fastest is called its ______ .

Section 2Below the optimum: fewer collisions

Cold molecules move slowly. The enzyme and the substrate have less energy, so they meet less often. Fewer collisions means fewer substrate molecules reaching an active site, so the rate is low. Nothing is damaged: warm the tube and the rate climbs again.

Why is an enzyme's rate low at 5 °C?

Section 3Above the optimum: denatured

Heat past the optimum and the enzyme molecule changes shape. The active site is the part that matters: once its shape has changed, the substrate is no longer complementary to it, cannot fit, and the reaction stops. The word for this is denatured.

Why cooling does not bring it backAn enzyme is a long chain folded into one exact shape, and that folding is what makes the active site. Heat shakes the chain apart; cooling does not refold it into the same shape. The change is permanent, which is why the specification says irreversible.

An enzyme is heated to 70 °C, then cooled back to 37 °C. What happens to its rate?

Section 4A full answer, written out

Both sides arrive as one question about the graph at the top. (a) Explain why the rate increases between 10 °C and 35 °C. [2] (b) Explain why the rate falls between 45 °C and 60 °C. [3] Here is the answer line by line, with what each line earns.

As the temperature rises the molecules gain energy and move faster.

Start with what the heat does to the molecules. An answer that jumps straight to collisions has missed a mark.

EARNS P1

So there are more collisions between the enzyme and the substrate, and more enzyme-substrate complexes form.

The consequence. Summer 2024's examiners named exactly this chain.

EARNS P2

Above the optimum the enzyme is denatured.

Use that word: an enzyme is a molecule, so killed and destroyed are not on the accept list.

EARNS P1 (b)

The shape of the active site changes, irreversibly.

Which part changes, and that it cannot be undone: two ideas, and the pair is rewarded.

EARNS P2 (b)

The substrate is no longer complementary to the active site, so it cannot fit and the reaction stops.

Without it the answer describes a change but not a falling rate.

Teaching methods, and the evidence behind them

Built for two learners: the one meeting the topic for the first time and the one who half knows it.

First time: instruction before testing

Idea and picture, then a fully worked example, then completion problems, then unaided practice. Guidance fades step by step (Sweller, Renkl, Atkinson). Nothing is locked behind a test; the confidence check comes after instruction, where confident errors can be corrected (Butterfield and Metcalfe).

Done it before: check, then route

"I have done this before" opens on the exam panel, runs a short confidence check, then routes to the weak skills at the faded or problem level, then a mixed set. Successive relearning to criterion, then spacing (Rawson and Dunlosky).

Self-explanation and why

Every method step carries its reason; every lesson has a "why" callout; gates ask for the reason as often as the answer. Explaining to yourself is one of the strongest study techniques on record (Chi; Dunlosky 2013).

Dual coding

Words and a picture for every idea: table then histogram, equation then graph, word equation then symbols, particle picture then observation (Mayer).

Retrieval, spaced and mixed

Prompts and misses return on a schedule (FSRS) and are shown as a promise on the close card, never a nag. Mixed sets interleave topics in CCEA layout for exam weeks (Rohrer).

Examiner language

Marks are earned in the scheme's own codes; the "In the exam" panel carries the Chief Examiner's findings for that topic in our words with the series named, so she learns what the paper rewards, not only what is true.

Method marks on screen

A working ladder marks line by line, so the 43% of marks that are method marks are seen and awarded; faded steps compare her line to the model's; a photo of working is the fallback for the last few drawing parts.

Calm accomplishment

Gates answer "Yes." or "Not quite.", never a score. The close card states what was done in exam terms and what returns when. No streaks to lose, no confetti, no shame after a missed week.
