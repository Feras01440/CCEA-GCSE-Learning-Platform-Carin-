STEM: The table gives measurements from a stained section of an artery and a vein that were the same width on the outside.
(a) Calculate the width of the lumen of the artery. [2]
(b) Explain how the artery's wall suits the job it does. [2]
  FIG: svg-gen — table
  1. wall on both sides = 2 × 1.5 = 3.0 mm   — The wall runs all the way round, so it is crossed twice on the way through. Subtracting it once is the classic slip here.  (P1)
  2. lumen = 6.0 − 3.0 = 3.0 mm   — Outside width minus the two wall thicknesses. Compare it with the vein's 5.0 mm lumen: same tube from outside, very different inside.  (P2)  WHY: ✔Because the wall is crossed once on the way in and once on the way out | ✘Because the artery wall is thicker than the vein wall | ✘Because the lumen is always half the outside width

  4. It has to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the heart, and the elastic fibres let it stretch and recoil with each beat.   — Then the reason. Structure plus function: the function is where the mark is.  (P4)
  FINAL: (a) 3.0 mm. (b) The wall is thick and contains muscle and elastic fibres, so it can withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the heart and can stretch and recoil with every beat.

### we.science.b2.b2-blood-and-vessels.02 [verified] paper 7 calc
STEM: A drawing of one red blood cell measures 42 mm across. A real red blood cell is 7 µm across.
(a) Calculate the magnification of the drawing. [3]
(b) Give two ways the cell is adapted to carry oxygen. [2]
  1. 42 mm = 42 × 1000 = 42000 µm   — Both lengths must be in the same unit before you divide, and micrometres are the smaller one, so convert the drawing. There are 1000 µm in 1 mm.  (P1)
  2. magnification = drawn size ÷ real size = 42000 ÷ 7   — Drawn over real, in that order. Turning it upside down gives a number smaller than 1, which is a warning sign in itself.  (P2)
  3. = ×6000   — A magnification has no unit: it is a ratio of two lengths. Write it with a multiplication sign in front.  (P3)
  4. It is a biconcave disc, which gives it a large surface area for absorbing oxygen.   — Adaptation plus its reason. 'Biconcave' alone usually earns the mark, but the reason makes the answer safe.  (P4)

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.b2.b2-blood-and-vessels.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Recall cell structures (B1)): Before we start, three things you have already met. Which structure is found in an animal cell but NOT in a bacterial cell?
    ✔ a nucleus — A nucleus. Keep hold of it: one blood cell is famous for giving its nucleus up.
    ✘ a cell membrane [sci.cells.bacteria-have-nucleus] — Bacteria have a membrane too. What they lack is a nucleus; their DNA lies loose in the cytoplasm.
    ✘ cytoplasm [sci.cells.bacteria-have-nucleus] — Every cell has cytoplasm.
    ✘ a cell wall [sci.cells.bacterial-wall-called-cellulose] — Bacteria do have a wall, though not a cellulose one. Animal cells are the ones without.
- p2 (Recall osmosis (previous topic)): A cell is put into pure water. Which way does water move?
    ✔ into the cell — Into the cell: pure water is the most dilute solution there is.
    ✘ out of the cell [sci.osmosis.direction-reversed] — Water moves towards the more concentrated solution, and that is the cytoplasm inside the cell.
    ✘ neither way [sci.osmosis.direction-reversed] — There is no net movement only when the two solutions match. Pure water and cytoplasm do not.
    ✘ into the cell, using energy from respiration [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — The direction is right, but osmosis is free: it needs no energy.
- p3 (Recall diffusion (B1)): What does the word 'diffusion' describe?
    ✔ Molecules spreading from where there are more of them to where there are fewer — Down the gradient. Every exchange in a capillary works this way.
    ✘ Molecules being pumped using energy [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — That is active transport. Diffusion costs nothing.
    ✘ Water moving through a membrane [sci.osmosis.direction-reversed] — That is osmosis, which is one special case of diffusion.
    ✘ Blood being pushed along a vessel [sci.osmosis.direction-reversed] — That is the heart's doing, not diffusion.

### dx.science.b2.b2-blood-and-vessels (post) [verified]
- 01 (Match a component to its function): Which component of blood converts fibrinogen to fibrin?
    ✔ platelets — Platelets. Fibrin makes the mesh that traps cells and forms a clot, and then a scab.
    ✘ red blood cells [sci.blood.component-function-swapped] — Red cells carry oxygen. Clotting is the platelets' job.
    ✘ white blood cells [sci.blood.component-function-swapped] — White cells defend against disease. Summer 2025 examiners recorded that many candidates could not link platelets to their two functions.
    ✘ plasma [sci.blood.component-function-swapped] — Plasma is the liquid that carries everything. It contains the fibrinogen, but the platelets do the converting.
- 02 (Recall red cell adaptations): Which of these is an adaptation of a red blood cell for carrying oxygen?
    ✔ It has no nucleus, so there is more room for haemoglobin — No nucleus, more haemoglobin. Summer 2024 examiners found the adaptations surprisingly poorly known.
    ✘ It has a large nucleus to control oxygen uptake [sci.blood.red-cell-adaptations-unknown] — A mature red blood cell has no nucleus at all — that is the point.
    ✘ It is a cube, which packs tightly in the vessel [sci.blood.red-cell-adaptations-unknown] — It is a biconcave disc: dimpled on both sides, which gives a large surface area.
    ✘ It contains fibrin to help it stick to the vessel wall [sci.blood.component-function-swapped] — Fibrin belongs to clotting. A red cell contains haemoglobin.
- 03 (Recall what plasma carries): Which substance does blood plasma transport?
    ✔ urea — Urea, on its way to the kidneys. Plasma also carries cells, food molecules, carbon dioxide and hormones.
    ✘ oxygen [sci.blood.plasma-carries-oxygen] — Almost all the oxygen travels inside red blood cells, bound to haemoglobin. Summer 2023 examiners saw oxygen given here and it was not credited.
    ✘ haemoglobin [sci.blood.plasma-carries-oxygen] — Haemoglobin stays inside the red blood cells; it is not dissolved in the plasma.
    ✘ fibrin [sci.blood.component-function-swapped] — Fibrin only forms when a clot is made. Plasma carries the fibrinogen it is made from.
- 04 (Relate vessel structure to function): Why does an artery have a much smaller lumen than a vein of the same outside width?
    ✔ Its wall is far thicker, to withstand high pressure — The wall takes up the space. Thick muscle and elastic fibres are what high-pressure blood needs.
    ✘ It carries less blood than a vein [sci.vessels.lumen-and-wall-swapped] — The same blood passes through both. The difference is the wall, not the amount.
    ✘ It has valves that take up room [sci.vessels.valves-in-arteries] — Arteries have no valves. Veins are the ones with valves.
    ✘ It is further from the heart [sci.vessels.lumen-and-wall-swapped] — Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and the pressure there is high, which is why the wall is thick.
- 05 (Relate capillary structure to function): Why is a capillary wall only one cell thick?
    ✔ So materials can be exchanged quickly with the tissues — Short distance, fast diffusion. Exchange is the whole job of a capillary.
    ✘ So the blood can travel faster [sci.vessels.capillary-role-unknown] — Blood actually flows slowly in capillaries, which gives more time for exchange.
    ✘ So it can withstand high pressure [sci.vessels.lumen-and-wall-swapped] — A thin wall is the opposite of what withstands pressure. That is the artery's problem.
    ✘ So valves can open and close inside it [sci.vessels.valves-in-arteries] — Capillaries have no valves. Only veins do.
- 06 (Apply osmosis to an animal cell): Higher Tier. Red blood cells are placed in pure water. What happens, and why?
    ✔ Water enters by osmosis and the cells burst — Lysis. With no cell wall there is nothing to limit the water entering.
    ✘ Water enters by osmosis and the cells become turgid [sci.osmosis.animal-cell-becomes-turgid] — Turgid is a plant-cell word: it needs a wall for the contents to press against.
    ✘ Water leaves by osmosis and the cells shrink [sci.osmosis.direction-reversed] — That happens in a concentrated salt solution. Pure water is more dilute than the cytoplasm, so water moves in.
    ✘ Nothing happens, because the cells have no nucleus [sci.osmosis.animal-cell-becomes-turgid] — The missing nucleus makes no difference to osmosis. The missing cell wall makes all the difference.

### ftm.science.b2.b2-blood-and-vessels.01 [verified] sci.quant.units-not-converted · earned P1 · ccea-cer:science:2023-summer:U7BBioH:Q4
STEM: Sinéad is asked: 'A drawing of a red blood cell is 42 mm across. The real cell is 7 µm across. Calculate the magnification. [3]'
Her working:
    1. magnification = drawn size ÷ real size
  ✘ 2. = 42 ÷ 7
    3. = ×6
  WRONG: The equation in line 1 is exactly right. Line 2 divides millimetres by micrometres, which is like dividing miles by inches: the two lengths have to be in the same unit first. 42 mm is 42000 µm.
  FIX: 42 mm = 42000 µm / magnification = 42000 ÷ 7 / = ×6000
  FEEDBACK: One mark of the three: the method line stands, and in a scheme that gives a mark for the equation it would be credited. The conversion mark and the answer mark go together. A useful habit is to write the unit beside every number as you substitute — the moment you see "mm ÷ µm" on the page, the conversion asks for itself.
