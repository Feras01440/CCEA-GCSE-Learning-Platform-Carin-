## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u3.conditional-probability.01 [verified] paper FM3 calc
STEM: The two-way table shows how 200 people travel to work, by age group.
One person is picked at random from those aged 25 or over. Calculate the probability that this person travels by car.
  FIG: svg — A two-way table of 200 people by how they travel and by age. Bus: 42 under 25 and 54 aged 25 and over, total 96. Car: 38 under 25 and 66 aged 25 and over, total 104. Column totals 80 and 120. The under-25 column is ringed with a dashed box.
  1. The condition is 'aged 25 or over'.   — Read the condition first and name the group out loud. Everything else follows from it, and reading it last is how the denominator goes wrong.  (M1)
  2. That column totals 54 + 66 = 120.   — The group is no longer all 200. Adding the column gives the new whole, and it is worth writing down before any fraction is formed.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔120, the 25-and-over total, because that is the group named | ✘200, because that is how many were surveyed | ✘104, because the question is about car travel
  3. Of those 120, 66 travel by car.   — The numerator is the single cell where the condition and the event meet. On a table it is always one cell, never a total.  (M1)
  4. $P(\text{car} \mid \text{25 and over}) = \dfrac{66}{120} = \dfrac{11}{20} = 0.55$   — Divide and simplify. A sense check: more than half of that age group travel by car, which matches the two numbers in the column.  (W1)
  FINAL: $P(\text{car} \mid \text{25 and over}) = \dfrac{11}{20}$, which is 0.55
  TWIN: Using the same table, one person is picked at random from those who travel by car. Calculate the probability that this person is aged 25 or over.  → numeric 0.6346153846153846 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=fraction/decimal

### we.fm.u3.conditional-probability.02 [verified] paper FM3 calc
STEM: A morning is wet with probability $0.4$. A delivery van is late with probability $0.25$ on a wet morning and $0.1$ on a dry one.
Given that the van was late, calculate the probability that the morning was wet.
  FIG: svg — A two-stage probability tree for a delivery van. The morning is wet with probability 0.4 and dry with probability 0.6; the van is late with probability 0.25 on a wet morning and 0.1 on a dry one
  1. $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$ with $A$ wet and $B$ late.   — Write the formula from the sheet with the letters named before any number goes in. Naming them is what keeps the condition on the bottom.  (M1)
  2. $P(\text{wet and late}) = 0.4 \times 0.25 = 0.10$   — The numerator is one branch: both things happen, so the branch probabilities multiply.  (MW1)
  3. $P(\text{late}) = 0.10 + 0.6 \times 0.1 = 0.10 + 0.06 = 0.16$   — The denominator is every branch that ends in 'late', wet or dry. Using only the wet one would leave the condition half-counted.  (M1)  WHY: ✔Both late branches, because the morning could have been wet or dry | ✘Only the wet-and-late branch, because the question is about wet mornings | ✘The probability of a wet morning, because that is the event asked for
  4. $P(\text{wet} \mid \text{late}) = \dfrac{0.10}{0.16} = \dfrac{5}{8} = 0.625$   — Divide, and check against sense: wet mornings are less common but far more likely to bring a late van, so more than half of the late mornings being wet is the right shape of answer.  (W1)
  FINAL: $P(\text{wet} \mid \text{late}) = \dfrac{5}{8}$, which is 0.625
  TWIN: Using the same tree, given that the van ran on time, calculate the probability that the morning was dry.  → numeric 0.6428571428571429 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=fraction/decimal

### we.fm.u3.conditional-probability.03 [verified] paper FM3 calc
STEM: Three pens are taken without replacement from a case of 11, of which 7 are blue.
$P(\text{all three blue}) = \dfrac{7}{33}$ and $P(\text{exactly two blue}) = \dfrac{28}{55}$.
Given that at least two of the pens are blue, calculate the probability that all three are blue.
  FIG: svg — Two nested regions inside a rectangle: the outer one is 'at least two of the three pens are blue' with probability 119/165, and the inner one, entirely inside it, is 'all three are blue' with probability 7/33.
  1. $P(\text{at least two blue}) = \dfrac{28}{55} + \dfrac{7}{33} = \dfrac{119}{165}$   — Build the condition first: at least two means exactly two or all three, and those two cases cannot both happen, so they add.  (M1)
  2. All three blue is inside at least two blue, so $P(A \cap B) = P(\text{all three blue}) = \dfrac{7}{33}$.   — Ask whether one event forces the other. It does here, so there is nothing to multiply: the joint probability is the inner one.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔$\dfrac{7}{33}$, because three blue pens already means at least two are blue | ✘$\dfrac{7}{33} \times \dfrac{119}{165}$, because both events must happen | ✘$\dfrac{7}{33} + \dfrac{119}{165}$, because either event will do
  3. $P(\text{all three} \mid \text{at least two}) = \dfrac{\dfrac{7}{33}}{\dfrac{119}{165}}$   — Substitute into the formula. The condition's probability goes underneath, as it always does.  (M1)
  4. $= \dfrac{7}{33} \times \dfrac{165}{119} = \dfrac{5}{17} = 0.29$   — Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal. A check: the answer must be larger than $\dfrac{7}{33}$, because conditioning on a smaller group makes the share bigger.  (W1)
  FINAL: $P(\text{all three blue} \mid \text{at least two blue}) = \dfrac{5}{17}$, which is 0.29
  TWIN: Using the same three pens, given that at least two are blue, calculate the probability that exactly two are blue.  → numeric 0.7058823529411765 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=fraction/decimal

### dx.fm.u3.conditional-probability.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Reading a two-way table (from GCSE Mathematics)): Before the lesson, a check on something you already know. A two-way table has a row total of 45 and one cell in that row holds 18. What is the other cell in the row?
    ✔ $27$ — Yes: $45 - 18$. Row and column totals are what make a two-way table fill itself in.
    ✘ $63$ — The two numbers have been added. A row total already includes both cells, so subtract.
    ✘ $18$ — That is the cell you were given. The rest of the row is what is left of the 45.
- p2 (Dividing decimals (from GCSE Mathematics)): Another check on earlier work. What is $\dfrac{0.12}{0.4}$?
    ✔ $0.3$ — Yes: $\dfrac{12}{40}$. Conditional probability from a tree is a division exactly like this one.
    ✘ $0.048$ — The two have been multiplied. Dividing by a number smaller than 1 makes the answer larger, not smaller.
    ✘ $3.33$ — The fraction is upside down. The 0.4 is the divisor, so it goes on the bottom.
- p3 (Adding across branches of a tree (from GCSE Mathematics)): One more from earlier work. On a probability tree, two branches end in the same outcome, with probabilities 0.1 and 0.06. What is the probability of that outcome?
    ✔ $0.16$ — Yes: separate branches add. This sum is the denominator of most conditional questions from a tree.
    ✘ $0.006$ — The two have been multiplied. Multiplying is for stages along one branch, not for separate branches.
    ✘ $0.04$ — One has been subtracted from the other. Both branches lead to the outcome, so both count.
- p4 (Reading a whole circle from its regions (from GCSE Mathematics)): A last check on earlier work. A Venn diagram shows 31 in the left circle only and 22 in the overlap. How many are in the left circle altogether?
    ✔ $53$ — Yes: $31 + 22$. A circle total is the sum of every region inside it, and it is often the denominator you need.
    ✘ $31$ — That is the 'only' region. The overlap is inside the left circle too.
    ✘ $9$ — The two have been subtracted. Both regions are inside the circle, so they add.

### dx.fm.u3.conditional-probability.post (post) [verified]
- d1 (The denominator of a conditional probability): In a survey of 150 people, 60 own a dog and 24 of those 60 also own a cat. Given that a person owns a dog, what is the probability that they own a cat?
    ✔ $\dfrac{24}{60}$ — Yes. Being told the person owns a dog shrinks the group to those 60.
    ✘ $\dfrac{24}{150}$ [fm.condprob.denominator-not-conditioned] — The numerator is right, but the condition has been ignored. Examiners report this as the commonest error in these parts.
    ✘ $\dfrac{60}{150}$ [fm.condprob.denominator-not-conditioned] — That is the probability of owning a dog at all. The question asks about cat ownership within the dog owners.
- d2 (Choosing between two plausible denominators): 88 people were asked, 28 of them said yes and all 28 were season-ticket holders. There are 100 people altogether. Given that a person is one of the 88, what is the probability they said yes?
    ✔ $\dfrac{28}{88}$ — Yes. The condition names the 88, so that is the group you are choosing from.
    ✘ $\dfrac{28}{100}$ [fm.condprob.denominator-not-conditioned] — That divides by everybody. The report on this exact question says only the best candidates used 88.
    ✘ $\dfrac{88}{100}$ [fm.condprob.denominator-not-conditioned] — That is the probability of being one of the 88, which is the condition itself rather than the answer.
- d3 (Using the formula as printed): $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$ and $P(B) = 0.5$. What is $P(A \mid B)$?
    ✔ $0.4$ — Yes: $0.2 \div 0.5$. The condition's probability goes underneath.
    ✘ $0.1$ [fm.condprob.numerator-and-denominator-not-combined] — The two have been multiplied. The formula divides the joint probability by the condition's.
    ✘ $0.2$ [fm.condprob.numerator-and-denominator-not-combined] — That is the joint probability, the numerator. The division has not been done yet.
- d4 (Combining the numerator and the denominator from a tree): A tree gives $P(\text{rain and late}) = 0.09$ and $P(\text{late}) = 0.15$. Given that the train was late, what is the probability that it rained?
    ✔ $0.6$ — Yes: $0.09 \div 0.15$. Both pieces are needed, and then the division.
    ✘ $0.09$ [fm.condprob.numerator-and-denominator-not-combined] — That is the numerator on its own. Examiners report that many candidates find one piece and stop.
    ✘ $0.15$ [fm.condprob.numerator-and-denominator-not-combined] — That is the denominator on its own, the probability of being late.
- d5 (Telling P(A given B) from P(B given A)): On a Venn diagram, 40 take French, 25 take German and 15 take both, out of 80 students. What is $P(\text{French} \mid \text{German})$?
    ✔ $\dfrac{15}{25}$ — Yes. The condition names German, so the German total is the denominator.
    ✘ $\dfrac{15}{40}$ [fm.condprob.condition-reversed] — That is $P(\text{German} \mid \text{French})$, the question the other way round. The two share a numerator and nothing else.
    ✘ $\dfrac{15}{80}$ [fm.condprob.denominator-not-conditioned] — That is the probability of taking both, out of everybody. The condition has been ignored.
- d6 (Nested events: the numerator is the inner one): A spinner is spun twice. $P(\text{two sixes}) = 0.04$ and $P(\text{at least one six}) = 0.36$. Given that at least one six was spun, what is the probability that both were sixes?
    ✔ $\dfrac{0.04}{0.36}$ — Yes. Two sixes already means at least one six, so the joint probability is simply 0.04.
    ✘ $\dfrac{0.04 \times 0.36}{0.36}$ [fm.condprob.subset-event-multiplied] — The two probabilities have been multiplied on top, as if the events were unrelated. One sits inside the other.
    ✘ $\dfrac{0.36}{0.04}$ [fm.condprob.condition-reversed] — The fraction is upside down, and the answer is above 1. The condition always goes underneath.
- d7 (Spotting the condition in the wording): Which phrase in a question tells you that the denominator will not be the whole group?
    ✔ 'given that', or 'a person is chosen from those who' — Yes. Either phrasing names the group you are now choosing from.
    ✘ 'at random' [fm.condprob.denominator-not-conditioned] — 'At random' only says the choice is fair. It says nothing about which group is being chosen from.
    ✘ 'and' [fm.condprob.numerator-and-denominator-not-combined] — 'And' points at the numerator, the outcomes where both things happen. The condition is what follows 'given'.
- d8 (Expected frequency from a conditional probability): $P(\text{bus} \mid \text{under 25}) = 0.6$ and a club has 200 under-25 members. How many would you expect to travel by bus?
    ✔ $120$ — Yes: $200 \times 0.6$. The condition names the group, and that group is what gets scaled.
    ✘ $0.6$ [fm.condprob.numerator-and-denominator-not-combined] — That is the probability. An expected number is the probability multiplied by the size of the group.
    ✘ $333$ [fm.condprob.condition-reversed] — The 200 has been divided by 0.6 instead of multiplied, which gives more members than the club has.

### q.fm.u3.conditional-probability.0008 [verified] practice · diff 5 · untiered · FM3 calc · 3 marks · (main)calculate3 · Three pens taken from a pencil case without replacement
  FIG: svg — Two nested regions inside a rectangle: the outer one is 'at least two of the three pens are blue' with probability 119/165, and the inner one, entirely inside it, is 'all three are blue' with probability 7/33.
  (main) [3] Three pens are taken without replacement from a case of 11 pens, 7 blue and 4 black. $P(\text{all three blue}) = \dfrac{7}{33}$ and $P(\text{at least two blue}) = \dfrac{119}{165}$.
Given that at least two of the pens are blue, calculate the probability that all three are blue.
      ANSWER: numeric 0.29411764705882354 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=fraction/decimal
      M1 M1: numerator recognised as $P(\text{all three blue}) = 7/33$, not a product

## RETRIEVAL PROMPTS
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.01 [verified] (formula) The conditional-probability formula, as the Unit 3 formula sheet prints it.  →  $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.02 [verified] (definition) What do the words 'given that' change in a probability question?  →  They name the group you are choosing from, so the denominator becomes that group's total instead of the whole population.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.03 [verified] (trap) Why are $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ different?  →  They share the same numerator, the outcomes where both happen, but the denominator is the condition's own total, and the two conditions have different totals.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.04 [verified] (procedure) How do you find a conditional probability from a two-way table?  →  Find the row or column the condition names, use its total as the denominator, and use the single cell where the condition and the event meet as the numerator.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.05 [verified] (procedure) How do you find a conditional probability from a tree diagram?  →  The numerator is the branch where both happen, and the denominator is every branch that satisfies the condition, added together.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.06 [verified] (trap) What goes wrong most often in a conditional-probability part, according to the examiners?  →  The numerator is usually right and the denominator is wrong: the whole population is used instead of the group the condition names.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.07 [verified] (trap) One event is inside another, such as 'three the same' inside 'two the same'. What is $P(A \cap B)$?  →  It is P(A), the inner event on its own, because every outcome of A is already in B. There is nothing to multiply.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.08 [verified] (trap) You have the numerator of a conditional probability. What must you not do next?  →  Stop there. The numerator on its own is the joint probability, and it still has to be divided by the probability of the condition.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.09 [verified] (qa) How do you find an expected number from a conditional probability?  →  Multiply the size of the group named in the condition by the conditional probability.
- rp.fm.u3.conditional-probability.10 [verified] (qa) Why can a conditional probability be larger than the joint probability it was built from?  →  Because dividing by the condition rescales it to a smaller group, and a share of a smaller group is bigger.
