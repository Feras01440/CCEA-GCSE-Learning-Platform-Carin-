## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.01 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: A café sells scones, coffees and bowls of soup.
A coffee costs 70p more than a scone. A bowl of soup costs 50% more than a coffee. An order of 2 scones, 3 coffees and 1 bowl of soup costs £12.25.
Find the price of each item.
  FIG: svg — A table pairing each kind of sentence with the equation it becomes: dearer by an amount is an addition, a percentage more is a multiplier, three times is a multiplier, together is a sum, and as many as is an equals sign.
  1. Let $x$ be the price of a scone, $y$ the price of a coffee and $z$ the price of a bowl of soup, all in pounds.   — Define the letters first, with their unit. A scheme cannot give method marks for equations whose letters have never been named, and choosing pounds now stops pence appearing later.  (M1)  WHY: ✔Because the unit fixes what every later number means | ✘Because the letters must be x, y and z | ✘Because the question says to
  2. $(1)$ $y = x + 0.7$   — "Dearer than" is an addition, and it is added to the cheaper item. The coffee is the dearer one here, so its letter stands alone.  (MW1)
  3. $(2)$ $z = 1.5y$   — A rise of 50% multiplies by $1 + \frac{50}{100} = 1.5$. A percentage is a percentage of something, so it can never be added as a number of pounds.  (MW2)  WHY: ✔Because 50% of the coffee is 0.5 times its price | ✘Because 50% means £0.50 | ✘Because 50% means £50.00
  4. $(3)$ $2x + 3y + z = 12.25$   — One equation per sentence, and every letter that belongs in it written in. An order equation takes each count times that item's own letter.  (MW3)
  5. Substituting $(1)$ and $(2)$ into $(3)$: $6.5x + 3.15 = 12.25$, so $x = 1.4$.   — Two of the three equations already give one letter in terms of another, so substitution is faster than elimination here. Pick the route the equations offer rather than the one you always use.  (M2,W1)
  6. $y = 1.4 + 0.7 = 2.1$ and $z = 1.5 \times 2.1 = 3.15$.   — Back-substitute into the simplest equations you have, which are the two comparisons.  (W2)
  7. Check in $(3)$: $2.8 + 6.3 + 3.15 = 12.25$. A scone is £1.40, a coffee £2.10 and a bowl of soup £3.15.   — Finish in the context's own words and units. Three bare numbers are not an answer to a question about a café, and prices this size are also a sense check on the whole solution.  (W3)
  FINAL: Scone £1.40, coffee £2.10, soup £3.15

How old is Aoife?  → numeric 17 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.0005} forms=decimal/fraction

### we.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.02 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: A printing firm's profits are $x$, $y$ and $z$ thousand pounds per order.
In March it completed 12 standard, 8 premium and 6 bespoke orders for a total profit of £78000.
(i) Show that $6x + 4y + 3z = 39$.
(ii) Given also that $2x + 3y + z = 18$ and $2x + y + 3z = 22$, find the profit on a standard order in pounds.
  FIG: svg — A three-row table: the March equation with the original counts and a right-hand side of 78; the same equation after every term is divided by 2; and the printed form, which matches it.
  1. The letters are in thousands, so the March total of £78000 is $78$ in the same units.   — Put both sides in the same units before anything else. Thousands on the left and pounds on the right is the slip the reports name most often on this question.  (M1)
  2. $12x + 8y + 6z = 78$   — This is the line the show-that is really asking for. Write it before you touch the printed form, because a show-that is marked on the derivation, not on the answer.  (W1)
  3. Dividing every term by $2$ gives $6x + 4y + 3z = 39$, which is the printed form.   — Compare your coefficients with the printed ones to decide the factor and its direction. The printed numbers are smaller, so the step is a division; multiplying moves away from them.  (W2)  WHY: ✔Because $12 \div 2 = 6$, and the same factor works on every term | ✘Because the right-hand side is a round number | ✘Because dividing always simplifies an equation
  4. $(3) - (2)$ clears $x$: $-2y + 2z = 4$.   — Equations $(2)$ and $(3)$ both begin with $2x$, so one subtraction clears a letter with no scaling at all. Look for that before you start multiplying equations.  (M2)
  5. Clearing $x$ from $(1)$ and $(2)$ as well and solving the pair gives $x = 2$, $y = 3$ and $z = 5$.   — Same plan as any three-unknown system: the same letter out of two different pairs, then back-substitute.  (W3)
  6. The letters are in thousands, so the profit on a standard order is $2 \times 1000 = £2000$.   — Scale back. The question asked for pounds and the letters were defined in thousands, so the last line is a multiplication by $1000$. Examiners report this step forgotten after solving that was otherwise faultless.  (W4)
  FINAL: (i) shown  (ii) £2000
  TWIN: Using the same three equations, write down the profit on a bespoke order, in pounds.  → numeric 5000 £ tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.0005} forms=decimal

### we.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.03 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: A curve has the equation $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ and passes through the points $(1, 6)$, $(2, 15)$ and $(3, 28)$.
Find the equation of the curve.
  FIG: svg — A table of the five stages of a forming question: define the letters with units, form one equation per fact, derive any printed form and then divide, solve by elimination, and interpret the answer back in the context.
  1. The unknowns are $a$, $b$ and $c$, not $x$ and $y$.   — Name what you are looking for before you substitute. Each point gives a pair of numbers to put in, and what comes out is an equation in the three coefficients.  (M1)  WHY: ✔Because $x$ and $y$ are known at each point, so only the coefficients are left | ✘Because a curve always has three unknowns | ✘Because $a$, $b$ and $c$ come first in the alphabet
  2. From $(1, 6)$: $a + b + c = 6$   — Substitute $x = 1$ and $y = 6$, and work out $x^2$ as you go so the coefficient of $a$ is a number.  (MW1)
  3. From the other two points: $4a + 2b + c = 15$ and $9a + 3b + c = 28$   — The coefficient of $a$ is $x^2$ each time, so it is $4$ and $9$ here. Writing $2a$ instead of $4a$ is the commonest slip in this kind of question.  (MW2)
  4. Every equation ends in $+ c$, so subtracting: $(2) - (1)$ gives $3a + 1b = 9$ and $(3) - (2)$ gives $5a + 1b = 13$.   — The letter to clear picks itself: $c$ has a coefficient of one in all three equations, so no scaling is needed at all.  (M2)
  5. Subtracting those two leaves $2a = 4$, so $a = 2$, then $b = 3$ and $c = 1$.   — The two new equations are an ordinary pair, and the $b$ terms are equal, so one more subtraction finishes it.  (W1)
  6. The curve is $y = 2x^2 + 3x + 1$. Check at $x = 3$: $18 + 9 + 1 = 28$.   — Answer the question that was asked, which was for the equation rather than for three numbers, and test it at a point you did not use in the last step.  (W2)
  FINAL: $y = 2x^2 + 3x + 1$
  TWIN: A curve $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ passes through $(2, 15)$. Write down the equation in $a$, $b$ and $c$ that this point gives.  → algebraic 4a + 2b + c = 15 (equivalent)

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Prerequisite: a percentage increase as a multiplier): Four quick checks on what this lesson is built from. None of them is the new work, so answer from what you already know.
A price of £2.1 rises by 50%. What is the new price?
    ✔ £3.15 — A rise of 50% multiplies by $1.5$, and $1.5 \times 2.1 = 3.15$.
    ✘ £52.1 [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — That adds 50 pounds. A percentage is always a percentage of something, so it multiplies.
    ✘ £2.6 [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — That adds 0.5 of a pound. The 50% is 50% of the £2.1, not a fixed amount.
- p2 (Prerequisite: writing a comparison in algebra): Bróna is $y$ years old. Aoife is 5 years older than Bróna. How old is Aoife, in terms of $y$?
    ✔ $y + 5$ — Older means more, so the difference is added to the younger sister's age.
    ✘ $y - 5$ [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — That makes Aoife the younger one. Decide who is older before you choose the sign.
    ✘ 5y [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — "Older than" is a difference, so it is added. Multiplying would be "5 times as old as".
- p3 (Prerequisite: converting between pounds and pence): How many pence are there in £21.3?
    ✔ 2130 — Multiply by 100. Keeping one unit throughout an equation is what makes the coefficients whole numbers.
    ✘ 213 [fm.simeq.wrong-scaling-to-reach-given] — That multiplies by ten rather than by a hundred. There are 100 pence in a pound.
    ✘ 21300 [fm.simeq.wrong-scaling-to-reach-given] — That multiplies by a thousand, which is ten times too many.
- p4 (Prerequisite: substituting a value into a squared term): The curve $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ passes through $(2, 15)$. What does the first term become when the point is substituted in?
    ✔ $4a$ — $x$ is squared before it multiplies $a$, and $2^2 = 4$.
    ✘ $2a$ [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — The square has been missed. The term is $ax^2$, so the number that multiplies $a$ is $x$ squared.
    ✘ $15a$ [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — That is the $y$-coordinate. It belongs on the other side of the equals sign.

### dx.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.post (post) [verified]
- q1 (Translate a percentage comparison): A bowl of soup costs 50% more than a coffee. Which equation says that?
    ✔ $z = 1.5y$ — A rise of 50% is a multiplier of $1.5$, and the soup is the dearer item.
    ✘ $z = y + 50$ [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — That adds 50 pounds, pricing the soup at £52.1.
    ✘ $y = 1.5z$ [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — The multiplier is right and the two items have swapped places: it is the soup that costs more.
- q2 (Check every letter appears in the equation): A candidate writes $2x + 3y = 12.25$ for an order of 2 scones, 3 coffees and 1 bowl of soup costing £12.25. What is missing?
    ✔ The soup's term, $z$ — A count of one still needs its letter written in. Examiners report omitted variables as a recurring loss on this question.
    ✘ Nothing, the equation is complete [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — The order includes a bowl of soup, so its price belongs in the bill: $2x + 3y + z = 12.25$.
    ✘ The pound signs [fm.simeq.wrong-equation-from-words] — The unit was fixed when the letters were defined, so it does not appear again. The missing piece is a letter.
- q3 (Reach a printed form by scaling the whole equation): Your equation is $12x + 8y + 6z = 78$ and the printed form is $6x + 4y + 3z = 39$. What is the step between them?
    ✔ Divide every term by $2$ — $12 \div 2 = 6$, and the same factor works on all four numbers.
    ✘ Multiply every term by $2$ [fm.simeq.wrong-scaling-to-reach-given] — The printed coefficients are smaller than yours, so multiplying moves away from them. This was the reported error on the 2024 paper.
    ✘ Divide only the left-hand side by $2$ [fm.simeq.wrong-scaling-to-reach-given] — An equation stays true only when both sides change together, so the right-hand side is divided too.
- q4 (Know which direction a show-that runs): A show-that question prints the equation you have to reach. Where does the working start?
    ✔ At the information in the question, finishing at the printed line — The derivation is what carries the marks, so it has to run forwards. The printed line is your last line, never your first.
    ✘ At the printed equation, checking that the information fits it [fm.simeq.work-backwards-from-given] — That is working backwards, and it gains nothing: examiners have said so in more than one series.
    ✘ At the answers, substituting them into the printed equation [fm.simeq.work-backwards-from-given] — The answers are not known yet at this stage, and substituting a result is verification rather than derivation.
- q5 (Scale the answer back into the context's units): The letters were defined in thousands of pounds and the solving gives $x = 2$. The question asks for the profit in pounds. What do you write?
    ✔ £2000 — Multiply by $1000$, because each unit of $x$ was one thousand pounds.
    ✘ £2 [fm.simeq.scale-factor-not-reapplied] — That is the value in the units the letters were defined in. The scaling back is a mark of its own, and it is the one most often forgotten.
    ✘ £2000000 [fm.simeq.scale-factor-not-reapplied] — The scaling has been applied twice. One multiplication by $1000$ is enough.
- q6 (Sense-check the answer against its context): A candidate solving for three ages reaches $x = 17$, $y = 22$ and $z = -2$. What should she do next?
    ✔ Go back and check the equations she formed — An age of -2 is impossible, and the elimination is usually sound in cases like this, so the fault is in the translation.
    ✘ Write the three values down and move on [fm.simeq.unrealistic-values-unchecked] — A negative age cannot be right. Examiners have noted candidates accepting meaningless values without comment.
    ✘ Change the sign of the negative one [fm.simeq.unrealistic-values-unchecked] — Changing an answer to make it look sensible leaves the equations wrong and the other two values wrong with them.
- q7 (Reverse a percentage change): A profit falls by 30% to £3500. What was it before the fall?
    ✔ £5000 — The fall leaves 70%, so divide: $3500 \div 0.7 = 5000$.
    ✘ £4550 [fm.simeq.unrealistic-values-unchecked] — Adding 30% back on is not the reverse, because the percentage is of a different amount each time. Check forwards and it does not return to £3500.
    ✘ £2450 [fm.simeq.unrealistic-values-unchecked] — That takes another 30% off. The answer has to be larger than £3500, because the profit fell to reach it.
- q8 (Know what an unsupported answer earns): What does a page with three correct values and no equations on it earn in Further Mathematics?
    ✔ Nothing, because the equations carry the method marks — The Teacher Guidance says the full development must be shown, and the reports repeat that calculator-only answers gain nothing.
    ✘ Full marks, because the values are right [fm.presentation.calculator-no-method] — Most of the marks in a forming question are for the equations and the eliminations, not for the three numbers.
    ✘ Half the marks [fm.presentation.calculator-no-method] — There is no half credit for an unsupported answer here; the instruction to show each stage is printed with the question.

### q.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.0006 [verified] practice · diff 4 · untiered · FM1 calc · 3 marks · (main)show3 · One month of a printing firm's profits reduced to the printed equation
  (main) [3] A printing firm takes standard, premium and bespoke orders.
Let $x$ be the profit on a standard order, $y$ the profit on a premium order and $z$ the profit on a bespoke order, each in thousands of pounds.

In March the firm completed 12 standard, 8 premium and 6 bespoke orders, for a total profit of £78000.

### ftm.fm.u1.form-three-simultaneous-equations.01 [verified] fm.simeq.work-backwards-from-given · earned none · ccea-cer:further-maths:2023-summer:FM1:Q11
STEM: Niamh is asked to show that a printing firm's March orders give $6x + 4y + 3z = 39$, where $x$, $y$ and $z$ are profits in thousands of pounds. The firm completed 12 standard, 8 premium and 6 bespoke orders for £78000. Her working:
  ✘ 1. 6x + 4y + 3z = 39
    2. Multiply by 2: 12x + 8y + 6z = 78
    3. That matches the 12 standard, 8 premium and 6 bespoke orders
    4. So the printed equation is right
  WRONG: Every line here is arithmetically true, and the whole thing still earns nothing, because it runs the wrong way.
Line 1 starts at the printed equation, which is the thing that was to be shown. Lines 2 and 3 then check that it fits the orders. That is verification, and a show-that asks for a derivation.
Examiners have made this point in more than one series: working backwards from the given equations gains no marks, however tidy it looks.
  FIX: The letters are in thousands, so the total is 78 / 12x + 8y + 6z = 78 / Divide every term by 2: 6x + 4y + 3z = 39 / which is the printed form
  FEEDBACK: The same four lines in the other order would score everything. Start from the orders, reach an equation of your own, and let the printed line be the last thing you write.
