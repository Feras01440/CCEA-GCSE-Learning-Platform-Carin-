## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.science.c2.c2-redox.01 [verified] paper C2 calc
STEM: Magnesium powder is heated with copper(II) oxide:
$\ce{Mg + CuO -> MgO + Cu}$
(a) Name the substance that is oxidised, and say how you know. [2]
(b) Name the substance that is reduced, and say how you know. [2]
  FIG: svg — Copper oxide and hydrogen on the left, copper and water on the right, with one curved arrow carrying the oxygen off the copper oxide and a second carrying it onto the hydrogen.
  1. Follow the oxygen. On the left it is attached to the copper; on the right it is attached to the magnesium.   — Track one atom, not the whole equation. Oxygen is the thing that moved, so oxygen is the thing to follow.
  2. The magnesium has gained oxygen, so the magnesium is oxidised.   — Name the substance first, then the reason. A definition on its own answers a different question.  (P1,P2)  WHY: ✔Because the mark is for identifying the substance, not for reciting the definition | ✘Because magnesium is more reactive than copper | ✘Because oxidation always happens to the metal on the left
  3. The copper(II) oxide has lost its oxygen, so the copper(II) oxide is reduced.   — Name the compound that lost it, not the element left behind. It is the copper oxide that is reduced; the copper is what reduction produced.  (P3,P4)
  FINAL: (a) The magnesium is oxidised, because it has gained oxygen. (b) The copper(II) oxide is reduced, because it has lost its oxygen.
  TWIN: In $\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$, name the substance that is reduced and say how you know.  → text accepted=["the iron(III) oxide is reduced because it has lost oxygen"] keyWords=[{"any":["iron oxide","iron(III) oxide","fe2o3","haematite"],"marks":1},{"any":["lost oxygen","loses oxygen","loss of oxygen","oxygen removed"],"marks":1}]

### we.science.c2.c2-redox.02 [verified] paper C2 calc
STEM: Zinc is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. The ionic equation is $\ce{Zn + Cu^{2+} -> Zn^{2+} + Cu}$.
(a) Write the half equation for the change undergone by the zinc. [3]
(b) Write the half equation for the change undergone by the copper(II) ions. [3]
Write a charge with the superscript sign — Zn²⁺ or Zn2⁺ — and an electron as e⁻.
  FIG: svg — A four-step table for building the half equation for iron becoming an iron three plus ion: write the atom and the ion, count the charge on each side, add three electrons, then check the charges match.
  1. Zinc starts as an atom and finishes as a 2+ ion: Zn → Zn^2+.   — Take one species at a time and write only what happens to it. A half equation has one element on each side, never the whole reaction.  (P1)
  2. Charges: 0 on the left, +2 on the right. Two electrons are missing, and they were lost, so they go on the right: Zn → Zn^2+ + 2e^-.   — Electrons go on the side that makes the two totals equal. Lost electrons appear with the products; gained electrons appear with the reactants.  (P2,P3)  WHY: ✔Because the two sides of any equation must carry the same total charge | ✘Because electrons are always written on the right | ✘Because zinc is a metal, and metals always end up positive
  3. Copper starts as a 2+ ion and finishes as an atom: Cu^2+ → Cu.   — Same method, opposite direction. The ion is on the left this time because that is where it starts.  (P4)
  4. Charges: +2 on the left, 0 on the right. Two electrons must be added on the left: Cu^2+ + 2e^- → Cu.   — Gained electrons go in with the reactants. Check the totals: +2 − 2 = 0 on the left, 0 on the right.  (P5,P6)
  FINAL: (a) Zn → Zn^2+ + 2e^- (oxidation). (b) Cu^2+ + 2e^- → Cu (reduction).
  TWIN: Write the half equation for a magnesium atom becoming a magnesium ion. Write a charge with the superscript sign — Zn²⁺ or Zn2⁺ — and an electron as e⁻.  → equation {"kind":"equation","kindOf":"half","balancedLatex":"Mg -> Mg^{2+} + 2e^-","stateSymbolsRequired":false,"acceptMultiples":true}

### we.science.c2.c2-redox.03 [verified] paper C2 calc
STEM: In the blast furnace, $\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$.
(a) Name the reducing agent and explain your choice. [2]
(b) Name the oxidising agent and explain your choice. [2]
  FIG: svg — Two labelled boxes joined by an arrow carrying two electrons: the reducing agent gives electrons away and is oxidised, the oxidising agent takes them and is reduced.
  1. Work out who was reduced first: the iron(III) oxide lost its oxygen, so it was reduced.   — You cannot name an agent until you know what happened to each substance. Do the reduction and oxidation first, the job titles second.
  2. Something must have taken that oxygen: the carbon monoxide, which became carbon dioxide. So the carbon monoxide was oxidised.   — Oxygen is conserved, so a loss on one side is always a gain on the other. This is the check that stops you naming the same substance twice.
  3. The reducing agent is the one that did the reducing: carbon monoxide. It reduced the iron(III) oxide by taking its oxygen, and was oxidised itself.   — Read the name as a job title, not as a description of what happened to it. The reducing agent is always the substance that is itself oxidised.  (P1,P2)  WHY: ✔Because an agent is named for what it does to the other substance | ✘Because the reducing agent is always the one that is reduced | ✘Because the reducing agent is always a gas
  4. The oxidising agent is the iron(III) oxide: it oxidised the carbon monoxide by giving up its oxygen, and was reduced itself.   — The two answers are always the two substances, one each way round. If you name the same one twice, something has gone wrong.  (P3,P4)
  FINAL: (a) Carbon monoxide is the reducing agent: it removes the oxygen from the iron(III) oxide, reducing it, and is itself oxidised to carbon dioxide. (b) Iron(III) oxide is the oxidising agent: it gives its oxygen to the carbon monoxide, oxidising it, and is itself reduced to iron.
  TWIN: In $\ce{2Al + Fe2O3 -> 2Fe + Al2O3}$, name the reducing agent and explain your choice.  → text accepted=["aluminium, because it takes the oxygen from the iron(III) oxide and is oxidised itself"] keyWords=[{"any":["aluminium"],"marks":1,"reject":["iron oxide"]},{"any":["takes the oxygen","removes the oxygen","gains oxygen","is oxidised"],"marks":1}]

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.c2.c2-redox.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Prerequisite from C1 §1.2 — charge on a simple ion; you are not expected to have met redox yet): A magnesium atom loses two electrons. What is the charge on the ion it becomes?
    ✔ 2+ — Losing two negative charges leaves the ion two positive. That single idea is the whole of the Higher half of this lesson.
    ✘ 2− [sci.formulae.ionic-charges-partial] — Losing negative charge makes an ion more positive, not more negative. Section 3 of the lesson leans on this, so it is worth fixing now.
    ✘ no charge [sci.formulae.ionic-charges-partial] — An atom is neutral because its protons and electrons balance. Take electrons away and the protons win.
- p2 (Prerequisite from C1 §1.5 — reading a formula): In $\ce{Fe2O3}$, which element is joined to the oxygen?
    ✔ iron — Fe is iron, and Fe₂O₃ is iron(III) oxide. Most of this lesson follows the oxygen on and off that compound.
    ✘ fluorine [sci.formulae.subscript-case-errors] — Fluorine is F on its own; Fe is iron. Symbols are case-sensitive and the second letter matters.
    ✘ hydrogen [sci.formulae.subscript-case-errors] — There is no hydrogen in this formula. Fe is iron and O is oxygen.
- p3 (Prerequisite from C1 §1.1 — the three sub-atomic particles): Which particle in an atom carries a negative charge?
    ✔ the electron — Electrons are the negative ones, and they are the currency the whole of this topic is traded in.
    ✘ the proton [sci.definitions.atomic-number-includes-electrons] — Protons are positive. Electrons are the negative particles, and they are what moves in a redox reaction.
    ✘ the neutron [sci.definitions.atomic-number-includes-electrons] — Neutrons carry no charge at all. The negative particle is the electron.

### dx.science.c2.c2-redox.post (post) [verified]
- 01 (Identify the oxidised species from an equation): In $\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}$, which substance is oxidised?
    ✔ the hydrogen — The hydrogen gains oxygen and becomes water, so it is oxidised.
    ✘ the copper(II) oxide [sci.redox.which-substance-oxidised] — The copper(II) oxide loses its oxygen, which is reduction. Follow the oxygen: whoever ends up with it is the one oxidised.
    ✘ the copper [sci.redox.which-substance-oxidised] — The copper is a product, and it has less oxygen than it started with, not more.
    ✘ the water [sci.redox.which-substance-oxidised] — The water is the product of the oxidation, not the substance that was oxidised. Name the starting substance.
- 02 (Recall the three definitions): Which of these is a definition of reduction that CCEA accepts?
    ✔ gain of electrons — Any one of gain of electrons, gain of hydrogen or loss of oxygen earns the mark.
    ✘ loss of electrons [sci.redox.oxidation-given-for-reduction] — That is oxidation. OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. Summer 2024 examiners recorded candidates giving the oxidation definition when reduction was asked.
    ✘ gain of oxygen [sci.redox.oxidation-given-for-reduction] — Gaining oxygen is oxidation. Reduction loses oxygen and gains hydrogen or electrons.
    ✘ loss of hydrogen [sci.redox.oxidation-given-for-reduction] — Losing hydrogen is oxidation. Read the table row by row: whatever oxidation does, reduction does the opposite.
- 03 (Define redox completely): What does the word redox mean?
    ✔ oxidation and reduction happening in the same reaction — Both halves, and the words 'in the same reaction' — that second half is a mark of its own.
    ✘ oxidation and reduction [sci.redox.redox-definition-incomplete] — Summer 2024 examiners recorded most candidates getting this far and no further. Add 'in the same reaction' or 'at the same time'.
    ✘ a reaction that gives out heat [sci.redox.redox-definition-incomplete] — That is an exothermic reaction, which is a different idea. Redox is about electrons or oxygen changing hands.
    ✘ a reaction that can go both ways [sci.redox.redox-definition-incomplete] — That is a reversible reaction. Redox means reduction and oxidation together.
- 04 (Put the electrons on the correct side): Which is the correct half equation for an iron atom becoming an iron(III) ion?
    ✔ Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ — Three electrons are lost, so they leave with the products and the charges cancel: 0 on the left, +3 and −3 on the right.
    ✘ Fe + 3e⁻ → Fe³⁺ [sci.equations.electrons-wrong-side] — The electrons are on the wrong side. That would make the left −3 and the right +3. Summer 2025 examiners named this as a main fault on this very question.
    ✘ Fe → Fe³⁺ + 2e⁻ [sci.equations.electrons-wrong-side] — The number of electrons must match the charge: an iron(III) ion is 3+, so three electrons are lost.
    ✘ Fe³⁺ → Fe + 3e⁻ [sci.equations.half-equation-reversed] — That is written backwards, and the electrons are on the wrong side for it too. The question starts from the atom.
- 05 (Use the molecule, not the atom, in a half equation): Bromide ions are oxidised at the anode during electrolysis. Which half equation is correct?
    ✔ 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ — Bromine leaves as a molecule, so two bromide ions are needed and two electrons are released.
    ✘ Br⁻ → Br + e⁻ [sci.equations.half-equation-atoms-not-molecules] — Bromine is diatomic: the product is Br₂, not a lone Br atom. Summer 2024 examiners recorded exactly this fault with oxygen at the anode.
    ✘ Br⁻ + e⁻ → Br₂ [sci.equations.electrons-wrong-side] — Oxidation loses electrons, so they belong on the right; and the bromines do not balance.
    ✘ 2Br⁻ + 2e⁻ → Br₂ [sci.equations.electrons-wrong-side] — The bromines balance, but the electrons are on the wrong side for an oxidation.
- 06 (Name the agent rather than the process): Magnesium is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution: $\ce{Mg + Cu^{2+} -> Mg^{2+} + Cu}$. Which species is the reducing agent?
    ✔ the magnesium — It gives its electrons away, reducing the copper(II) ions, and is oxidised itself.
    ✘ the copper(II) ions [sci.redox.agents-swapped] — They take electrons, so they oxidise the magnesium: they are the oxidising agent. An agent is named for what it does to the other substance.
    ✘ the sulfate ions [sci.redox.agents-swapped] — The sulfate ions are spectators: they are unchanged on both sides and take no part.
    ✘ the copper [sci.redox.agents-swapped] — The copper is a product. The agents are the two substances you start with.
- 07 (Apply redox to an industrial process): In the blast furnace, $\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$. What is happening to the carbon monoxide?
    ✔ it is being oxidised, because it gains oxygen — It picks up the oxygen the iron(III) oxide lost, becoming carbon dioxide, so it is oxidised.
    ✘ it is being reduced, because it gains oxygen [sci.redox.oxidation-given-for-reduction] — Gaining oxygen is oxidation. The substance being reduced here is the iron(III) oxide, which loses oxygen.
    ✘ nothing: it is a catalyst [sci.redox.reducing-agent-role-unknown] — A catalyst comes out unchanged. This carbon monoxide is changed into carbon dioxide, and that change is the point of it.
    ✘ it is being decomposed by the heat [sci.redox.reduction-reaction-not-recognised] — Summer 2024 examiners recorded answers such as combustion and the Haber process here. Read the equation: carbon monoxide plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide.

## FIND THE MISTAKE
### ftm.science.c2.c2-redox.01 [verified] sci.equations.electrons-wrong-side · earned P1 · ccea-cer:science:2025-summer:C2H:Q2(b)(v)
STEM: Ciara is asked: 'Write the half equation for the change from an iron atom to an iron(III) ion. [3]'
Her answer:
  ✘ 1. Fe + 3e- → Fe3+
    2. Iron loses three electrons.
    3. So the iron is oxidised.
  WRONG: Lines 2 and 3 are exactly right, and they contradict line 1. If the iron loses three electrons, those electrons leave with the products, so they belong on the right-hand side. As written, the left side carries −3 and the right side +3, which cannot be an equation. Summer 2025 examiners named electrons on the incorrect side as one of the two commonest faults on this very question.
  FIX: Fe → Fe3+ + 3e-
  FEEDBACK: One of the three marks: the reactant and product with an arrow are right. The electron mark goes because they are on the wrong side, and because the balancing mark depends on the electron mark, that one goes with it. Use the charge check every time: 0 on the left, +3 and −3 on the right, which is 0.

### ftm.science.c2.c2-redox.02 [verified] sci.redox.which-substance-oxidised · earned P2 · ccea-cer:science:2023-summer:C2H:Q2(c)
STEM: Ruairí is asked: 'In $\ce{Mg + CuO -> MgO + Cu}$, name the substance that is reduced and explain your answer. [2]'
His answer:
  ✘ 1. The magnesium is reduced.
    2. The copper(II) oxide has lost its oxygen.
    3. Loss of oxygen is reduction.
  WRONG: The answer contradicts its own evidence. Lines 2 and 3 are both right, and together they point straight at the copper(II) oxide: it is the substance that lost oxygen, and losing oxygen is reduction. Line 1 names the other substance. The magnesium gained that oxygen, so the magnesium is oxidised. Summer 2023's report describes exactly this — candidates who could give the general definitions but could not state which substance had undergone each process.
  FIX: The copper(II) oxide is reduced.
  FEEDBACK: One of the two marks: the reason is right, and it is doing the work for you. Read your own last two lines back before you name the substance — whichever one lost the oxygen is the one that was reduced. A quick habit: underline the command word first, and if it says Name, make the first word of your answer a name.

### ftm.science.c2.c2-redox.03 [verified] sci.equations.ionic-spectator-ions-included · earned P1 · ccea-cer:science:2024-summer:C2H:Q2
STEM: Tomás is asked: 'Zinc is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. Write the ionic equation. [2]'
