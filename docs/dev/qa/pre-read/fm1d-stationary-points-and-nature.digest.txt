## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u1.stationary-points-and-nature.01 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: A curve is defined by the equation $y = 2x^{3} - 9x^{2} + 12x - 3$
Using calculus, find the coordinates of every turning point on this curve, and say whether each one is a maximum point or a minimum point.
  FIG: svg — A cubic curve with a peak on the left and a dip on the right, each marked with a short flat dashed tangent.
  1. Differentiate: $\frac{dy}{dx} = 6x^{2} - 18x + 12$.   — The question says using calculus, so this line is compulsory as well as useful. It is the first mark and nothing can go wrong in it yet.  (MW1)
  2. Set it to zero: $6x^{2} - 18x + 12 = 0$, and divide through by 6 to get $x^{2} - 3x + 2 = 0$.   — Taking the common factor out first keeps the numbers small, which is where the arithmetic slips come from in this topic.  (M1)  WHY: ✔Because a stationary point is where the gradient is zero | ✘Because the curve crosses the x-axis there | ✘Because the second derivative is zero
  3. Factorise: $(x - 1)(x - 2) = 0$, so $x = 1$ and $x = 2$.   — A cubic gives a quadratic here, so two answers are expected. One answer on a cubic is a sign that something has gone wrong.  (W1)
  4. Heights from the curve: $y = 2$ at $x = 1$ and $y = 1$ at $x = 2$.   — Back into the original equation, never into the derivative, which would only hand back zero. Write the two substitutions on separate lines.  (W2)
  5. Second derivative: $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = 12x - 18$. At $x = 1$ it is $-6$; at $x = 2$ it is $6$.   — One substitution each. The number itself does not matter; only its sign is being read.  (MW2)
  6. $-6$ is negative, so $(1, 2)$ is a maximum; $6$ is positive, so $(2, 1)$ is a minimum.   — One of each, which is what a cubic always gives. If both had come out the same, the place to look would be step 3 or step 4.  (W3)
  FINAL: Maximum at $(1, 2)$ and minimum at $(2, 1)$
  TWIN: Find the coordinates of every turning point on $y = 2x^{3} - 3x^{2} - 12x + 4$.  → algebraic (-1, 11), (2, -16) (equivalent)

### we.fm.u1.stationary-points-and-nature.02 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: A curve is defined by the equation $y = 2x + \frac{8}{x^{1}}$
Using calculus, find the coordinates of every turning point on this curve, and say whether each one is a maximum point or a minimum point.
  FIG: svg — A curve in two separate branches, the left one with a high point below the axis and the right one with a low point above it.
  1. Rewrite and differentiate: $y = 2x + 8x^{-1}$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2 - \frac{8}{x^{2}}$.   — The fraction has to be written as a negative index before the power rule can reach it, and multiplying 8 by -1 makes that term negative.  (MW1)
  2. Set it to zero: $2 = \frac{8}{x^{2}}$, so $2x^{2} = 8$ and $x^{2} = 4$.   — Clearing the fraction first avoids the commonest slip here, which is solving as though the 8 were on the top line.  (M1)
  3. So $x = 2$ and $x = -2$.   — A square root gives two answers. Losing the negative one loses half the question, and this curve has a stationary point on each branch.  (W1)  WHY: ✔Because both 2 and -2 square to 4 | ✘Because x cannot be negative | ✘Because the curve is a cubic
  4. Heights from the curve: $y = 8$ at $x = 2$ and $y = -8$ at $x = -2$.   — Substitute into the original equation. Both terms change sign when x does, so the two heights are opposites here.  (W2)
  5. Second derivative: $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = \frac{16}{x^{3}}$, which is $2$ at $x = 2$ and $-2$ at $x = -2$.   — A negative x cubed is negative, so the sign flips between the two branches without any extra work.  (MW2)
  6. So there is a minimum at $(2, 8)$ and a maximum at $(-2, -8)$.   — The maximum is below the minimum, which looks wrong and is not: the two points are on separate branches with a break between them.  (W3)
  FINAL: Minimum at $(2, 8)$ and maximum at $(-2, -8)$
  TWIN: Find the coordinates of every turning point on $y = 3x + \frac{12}{x^{1}}$.  → algebraic (-2, -12), (2, 12) (equivalent)

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u1.stationary-points-and-nature.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Solve a quadratic by factorising): Three quick checks on what this lesson is built from. None of them is the new method, so answer from what you already know.
Solve $x^{2} - 3x + 2 = 0$.
    ✔ $x = 1$ and $x = 2$ — $(x - 1)(x - 2) = 0$. A cubic's derivative is a quadratic, so this is the equation you will be solving.
    ✘ $x = -1$ and $x = -2$ [fm.calc.coefficient-not-multiplied] — The signs in the brackets are the opposite of the signs in the answers: $(x - 1)(x - 2)$ gives $x = 1$ and $x = 2$.
    ✘ $x = 3$ and $x = 2$ [fm.calc.coefficient-not-multiplied] — The two numbers must add to $3$ and multiply to $2$, which is $1$ and $2$.
- p2 (Differentiate a cubic (the earlier topic)): Differentiate $y = 2x^{3} - 9x^{2} + 12x - 3$.
    ✔ $6x^{2} - 18x + 12$ — Multiply by the power, step the power down, and the constant goes to zero.
    ✘ $6x^{2} - 18x + 9$ [fm.calc.constant-term-kept] — The $-3$ differentiates to zero, so it does not appear in the gradient rule.
    ✘ $6x^{3} - 18x^{2} + 12x$ [fm.calc.power-not-reduced] — The coefficients are right. Each power also steps down by one.
- p3 (Read the sign of a number): Is $-6$ greater than zero or less than zero?
    ✔ Less than zero — Negative numbers are below zero. The whole second-derivative test is this one reading.
    ✘ Greater than zero [fm.calc.second-derivative-sign-misread] — The minus sign puts it below zero. Six is greater than zero; minus six is not.
    ✘ Neither, because it is a whole number [fm.calc.second-derivative-sign-misread] — Every number except zero is on one side or the other, and $-6$ is on the negative side.

### dx.fm.u1.stationary-points-and-nature.post (post) [verified]
- q1 (State the defining equation): What equation do you solve to find the stationary points of a curve?
    ✔ $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ — Stationary means neither rising nor falling, which is a gradient of exactly zero.
    ✘ $y = 0$ [fm.calc.differentiate-when-intercepts-asked] — That finds where the curve meets the $x$-axis, which is a different question.
    ✘ $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = 0$ [fm.calc.second-derivative-sign-misread] — The second derivative decides the nature of a point you have already found; it does not find it.
- q2 (Find a quadratic's turning point): The curve $y = x^{2} - 8x + 3$ is stationary at one point. What are its coordinates?
    ✔ $(4, -13)$ — $2x - 8 = 0$ gives $x = 4$, and the curve gives $y = -13$.
    ✘ $(4, 0)$ [fm.calc.stationary-y-from-derivative] — The $x$ is right. The height was taken from $\frac{dy}{dx}$, which is zero there by definition; it comes from the curve.
    ✘ $(-4, 51)$ [fm.calc.coefficient-not-multiplied] — The sign slipped when $2x - 8 = 0$ was solved: adding 8 to both sides gives $x = 4$.
- q3 (Read the second-derivative test): At a stationary point $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}} = -6$. What kind of point is it?
    ✔ A maximum — Negative means the curve bends downwards, so the point is the top of a rise.
    ✘ A minimum [fm.calc.second-derivative-sign-misread] — The two are the other way round: negative bends downwards and gives a maximum.
    ✘ The test cannot say [fm.calc.second-derivative-sign-misread] — The test only falls silent when the second derivative is exactly zero, and $-6$ is not.
- q4 (Identify which expression gives the height): Where does the $y$-coordinate of a turning point come from?
    ✔ From the equation of the curve — The point is on the curve, so substituting its $x$ into the curve gives its height.
    ✘ From $\frac{dy}{dx}$ [fm.calc.stationary-y-from-derivative] — That gives zero every time, because being stationary is what defines the point.
    ✘ From $\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}$ [fm.calc.stationary-y-from-derivative] — The second derivative tells you the nature of the point, not where it is.
- q5 (Find both turning points of a cubic): The curve $y = x^{3} + 3x^{2} - 9x + 2$ has two stationary points. What are they?
    ✔ $(-3, 29), (1, -3)$ — $3x^{2} + 6x - 9 = 0$ factorises to $3(x + 3)(x - 1) = 0$, and each $x$ goes back into the curve.
    ✘ $(-3, 0), (1, 0)$ [fm.calc.stationary-y-from-derivative] — Both values of $x$ are right. The heights were read from $\frac{dy}{dx}$, which is zero at every stationary point.
    ✘ $(1, -3)$ [fm.calc.coordinates-given-as-x-only] — That is one of the two points. A cubic has two stationary points, and both are wanted.
- q6 (Use the shape of a cubic as a check): A candidate finds both stationary points of a cubic and reports that both are minima. What should they do?
    ✔ Go back and look for an error — A cubic's second derivative is linear, so it changes sign exactly once: one maximum and one minimum, always.
    ✘ Write both down as minima [fm.calc.sketch-contradicts-results] — A cubic cannot have two minima. The examiners say this pattern should itself signal an error.
    ✘ Choose the smaller one and call it the minimum [fm.calc.sketch-contradicts-results] — Guessing between them leaves the error in place. The arithmetic in the earlier lines is where to look.
