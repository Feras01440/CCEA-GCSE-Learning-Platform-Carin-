## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.01 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A survey drone flies along a straight service tunnel. Its displacement from the entrance is shown on the graph.

(i) Calculate the velocity of the drone during the first 12 seconds.
(ii) Describe the motion of the drone between $t = 12$ and $t = 20$.
(iii) Calculate the velocity of the drone during the last stage of the flight.
  FIG: svg — A displacement/time graph: a straight line from (0, 0) to (12, 42), a horizontal line to (20, 42), then a straight line falling to (35, -18).
  1. Rise $= 42 - 0 = 42$ m, run $= 12 - 0 = 12$ s   — Read both from the axes, not by counting squares. On a displacement/time graph the rise is a change in position and the run is the time it took, so their quotient is a velocity.  (MW1)
  2. Velocity $= \dfrac{42}{12} = 3.5$ m/s   — The gradient of a displacement/time graph is the velocity. It is positive here, so the drone is moving away from the entrance.  (W1)  WHY: ✔Because the gradient of a displacement/time graph is the velocity | ✘Because the area under a displacement/time graph is the velocity | ✘Because the height of the graph is the velocity
  3. The graph is horizontal from $t = 12$ to $t = 20$.   — A horizontal section means the displacement is not changing, so the drone is hovering: at rest, though still 42 m from the entrance.  (MW1)
  4. Rise $= -18 - 42 = -60$ m, run $= 35 - 20 = 15$ s   — Use the stage's own start time, not the clock reading at the end of it. The rise is negative because the drone finishes on the other side of the entrance.  (MW1)
  5. Velocity $= \dfrac{-60}{15} = -4$ m/s   — The minus sign is part of the answer: it says the drone is travelling back towards the entrance and past it. A bare 4 would be the speed, and the question asked for the velocity.  (W1)
  FINAL: (i) $3.5$ m/s  (ii) at rest, 42 m from the entrance  (iii) $-4$ m/s
  TWIN: A body moves in a straight line. Its displacement/time graph is a single straight line through $(8, 50)$ and $(26, -4)$. Calculate the velocity of the body.  → numeric -3 m/s tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=decimal/fraction

### we.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.02 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A model boat moves along a straight channel. Its displacement from a jetty is shown on the graph.

(i) Calculate the total distance travelled by the boat in the 45 seconds.
(ii) Write down the displacement of the boat from the jetty after 45 seconds.
  FIG: svg — A displacement/time graph: a straight line from (0, 0) to (15, 45), a horizontal line to (25, 45), then a straight line falling to (45, -25).
  1. Outward leg: $0$ m to $45$ m, so $45$ m travelled.   — Distance counts every metre the boat covers, whichever way it is pointing, so take each leg separately and add their sizes.  (MW1)
  2. Return leg: $45$ m to $-25$ m, so $70$ m travelled.   — The displacement falls by 70 m. Its size is the distance covered on that leg; the sign belongs to the displacement, not to the distance.  (MW1)
  3. Total distance $= 45 + 70 = 115$ m   — The horizontal middle stage adds nothing: the boat is at rest there.  (W1)
  4. Displacement after 45 s $= -25$ m   — Read it straight off the vertical axis. The boat has finished 25 m on the far side of the jetty, so the displacement is negative even though 115 m were covered.  (MW1)
  FINAL: (i) $115$ m  (ii) $-25$ m
  TWIN: A body travels 18 m forwards along a straight line and then 30 m backwards along the same line. Write down its displacement from the starting point.  → numeric -12 m tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=decimal/fraction

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.pre (pre) [verified]
- 01 (Prerequisite: gradient of a straight line through two points): From an earlier topic: a straight line passes through $(2, 5)$ and $(6, 17)$. What is its gradient?
    ✔ 3 — Rise $= 17 - 5 = 12$ and run $= 6 - 2 = 4$, so the gradient is $12 \div 4 = 3$.
    ✘ 4 [fm.kin.gradient-not-taken-from-graph] — That is the run on its own. The gradient divides the rise by the run.
    ✘ 12 [fm.kin.gradient-not-taken-from-graph] — That is the rise on its own. It still has to be divided by the 4 the line travels across.
    ✘ 0.33 [fm.kin.gradient-and-area-swapped] — The fraction is upside down: run over rise instead of rise over run.
- 02 (Prerequisite: speed from a distance/time graph): From an earlier topic: a distance/time graph for a walk is a straight line from $(0, 0)$ to $(50, 400)$, with distance in metres and time in seconds. What is the walking speed?
    ✔ 8 m/s — 400 m in 50 s, so $400 \div 50 = 8$ m/s. The gradient of a distance/time graph is a speed.
    ✘ 0.125 m/s [fm.kin.gradient-and-area-swapped] — That divides the time by the distance. Speed is distance divided by time.
    ✘ 400 m/s [fm.kin.gradient-not-taken-from-graph] — That is the distance covered. A speed needs the time underneath it.
    ✘ 20000 m/s [fm.kin.gradient-and-area-swapped] — Multiplying gives an area, which has no meaning on these axes. Divide instead.
- 03 (Prerequisite: size of a negative reading on a number line): From an earlier topic: a point Q sits at $-24$ on a number line whose origin is O. How far is Q from O?
    ✔ 24 — The minus sign says which side of O the point is on. The distance is the size, 24.
    ✘ -24 [fm.kin.speed-vs-velocity] — A distance is a length, so it cannot be negative. The sign belongs to the position, not to the distance.
    ✘ 0 [fm.kin.graph-taken-to-axis] — Q is 24 away from O, not on top of it.
    ✘ 48 [fm.kin.speed-vs-velocity] — That is the distance from $-24$ to $+24$, which is twice as far as O.

### dx.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.post (post) [verified]
- 01 (Name what the gradient of a displacement/time graph gives): A displacement/time graph has a straight sloping section. What does the gradient of that section give?
    ✔ The velocity — Change in displacement divided by the time it took: that is a velocity, and it carries a sign.
    ✘ The acceleration [fm.kin.gradient-and-area-swapped] — Acceleration is the gradient of a velocity/time graph. A displacement/time graph is one step earlier than that.
    ✘ The distance travelled [fm.kin.gradient-not-taken-from-graph] — Distance is read from the height of the graph, not from its slope. The slope tells you how quickly that height is changing.
    ✘ The speed, so it is never negative [fm.kin.speed-vs-velocity] — The gradient can be negative, and that minus sign is the direction. Speed is the size of the velocity, which is why it never carries one.
- 02 (Read a horizontal section): Part of a displacement/time graph is a horizontal line for 15 seconds. What is happening?
    ✔ The body is at rest for 15 seconds — The displacement is not changing, so the body is not moving. It is parked somewhere, not necessarily at the start.
    ✘ The body is moving at a steady velocity for 15 seconds [fm.kin.gradient-and-area-swapped] — A steady velocity would be a sloping straight line. Flat means the position stays the same.
    ✘ The body is back at its starting point [fm.kin.graph-taken-to-axis] — That would be a section lying along the time axis. A horizontal line at any height means the body has stopped at that displacement.
    ✘ The body is slowing down [fm.kin.negative-gradient-as-slowing] — Slowing down would show as a curve flattening. A straight horizontal line has already reached a velocity of zero and stays there.
- 03 (Interpret a negative gradient): A section of a displacement/time graph slopes downwards. What does that tell you?
    ✔ The body is travelling back towards, and possibly past, the starting point — A falling displacement means the position is decreasing, so the body has turned round.
    ✘ The body is slowing down [fm.kin.negative-gradient-as-slowing] — Slowing down is about how the velocity changes, which a straight line cannot show. A constant negative gradient is a steady velocity in the other direction.
    ✘ The body is below ground level [fm.kin.gradient-not-taken-from-graph] — The vertical axis measures position along one straight line, not height. Negative simply means the other side of the starting point.
    ✘ The body has a negative speed [fm.kin.speed-vs-velocity] — Speed is a size and is never negative. The velocity is negative; its size is the speed.
- 04 (Use the stage's own time, not the clock reading): A body is at 60 m when $t = 20$ s and at 12 m when $t = 36$ s. What is its velocity over that stage?
    ✔ -3 m/s — Rise is 12 - 60 = -48 m and run is 36 - 20 = 16 s, so the velocity is -48 divided by 16.
    ✘ -1.33 m/s [fm.kin.stage-time-not-used] — That divides by the clock reading of 36 s instead of the 16 s the stage actually took. Subtract the start time first.
    ✘ 3 m/s [fm.kin.speed-vs-velocity] — The size is right, so the method mark is safe. The displacement is falling, so the velocity is negative.
    ✘ -48 m/s [fm.kin.gradient-not-taken-from-graph] — That is the change in displacement, not the velocity. Divide it by the 16 s the stage took.
- 05 (Separate distance from displacement): A body goes 40 m forwards along a straight line and then 65 m backwards. What is its displacement from the start?
    ✔ -25 m — Displacement adds the signed legs: 40 - 65 = -25 m, which is 25 m on the other side of the start.
    ✘ 105 m [fm.kin.speed-vs-velocity] — That is the total distance travelled. Displacement asks only where the body has ended up.
    ✘ 25 m [fm.kin.negative-gradient-as-slowing] — The size is right and the working is worth a method mark. The body finished behind the start, so the displacement is negative.
    ✘ 0 m [fm.kin.graph-taken-to-axis] — That would need the two legs to be equal. Here the return leg is 25 m longer than the outward one.

## FIND THE MISTAKE
### ftm.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.01 [verified] fm.kin.stage-time-not-used · earned MW1 · ccea-cer:further-maths:2023-summer:FM2:Q6
STEM: Órlaith is finding the velocity of the coastguard launch on its last stage. The launch is 100 m from the pier when $t = 40$ s and 20 m beyond the pier when $t = 60$ s. Her working:
    1. Change in displacement = -20 - 100 = -120 m
  ✘ 2. Time = 60 s
    3. Velocity = -120 ÷ 60 = -2 m/s
  WRONG: Line 2 uses the clock reading at the end of the stage. The stage did not last 60 seconds: it began at $t = 40$ s, so it lasted $60 - 40 = 20$ s. Dividing by the whole time since the launch left the pier spreads the last 120 m over the resting and outward stages as well.
  FIX: Change in displacement = -20 - 100 = -120 m / Time for the stage = 60 - 40 = 20 s / Velocity = -120 ÷ 20 = -6 m/s
  FEEDBACK: The first line is exactly right, minus sign and all, and that is the method mark (M) safely earned. The working mark (W) needs the run measured across the stage itself. A good habit is to write the two joints down as coordinates first, $(40, 100)$ and $(60, -20)$, because then both subtractions are in front of you.

- rp.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.04 [verified] (procedure) How do you find the velocity of one stage of a displacement/time graph?  →  Take the two joints of that stage, subtract the displacements for the rise, subtract the times for the run, then divide the rise by the run.
- rp.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.05 [verified] (trap) A section of a displacement/time graph slopes down. Is the body decelerating?  →  No. A straight section has a constant velocity, so nothing is speeding up or slowing down. The negative gradient means the body is travelling back towards the start.
- rp.fm.u2.displacement-time-graphs.06 [verified] (qa) A body goes out and comes back to where it began. What are its distance and its displacement?  →  The distance is everything it covered, out and back. The displacement is zero.
