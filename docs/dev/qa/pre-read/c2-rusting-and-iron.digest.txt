### we.science.c2.c2-rusting-and-iron.01 [verified] paper C2 calc
STEM: The diagram shows four stoppered tubes, each holding an iron nail, left for a week.
(a) Give the chemical name of rust. [2]
(b) Explain the purpose of the anhydrous calcium chloride in tube B. [1]
(c) Explain why the nail in tube C does not rust. [2]
  FIG: svg — Four stoppered boiling tubes, each holding an iron nail: tube A in tap water, tube B in dry air above anhydrous calcium chloride, tube C in boiled water under a layer of oil, and tube D in salty water.
  1. Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide.   — Two words, two marks: hydrated, and iron(III) oxide. Leaving out hydrated, or writing iron oxide without the (III), each costs one.  (P1,P2)  WHY: ✔Because 'hydrated' and the oxidation state are separate marking points | ✘Because iron only forms one oxide | ✘Because rust contains no water at all
  2. The anhydrous calcium chloride is a drying agent: it removes the water (water vapour) from the air in the tube.   — Say what it takes away, not what it keeps out. 'To prevent water getting in' is recorded as earning nothing, because the bung already does that.  (P3)
  3. Tube C: the water was boiled first, which drives out the dissolved oxygen.   — Start with why the oxygen has gone, then say what keeps it away. Two separate ideas, two marks.  (P4)
  4. The oil layer then stops oxygen from dissolving back into the water, so the nail has no oxygen and cannot rust.   — Use the word dissolving. 'To keep oxygen out' is not detailed enough for the mark.  (P5)
  FINAL: (a) Hydrated iron(III) oxide. (b) It is a drying agent: it removes water from the air in the tube. (c) The water was boiled to drive out the dissolved oxygen, and the layer of oil stops oxygen dissolving back in, so there is no oxygen and the nail cannot rust.
  TWIN: In the same experiment, explain why the nail in tube A, standing in ordinary tap water with air above it, does rust.  → text accepted=["both water and oxygen are present, and rusting needs both"] keyWords=[{"any":["water","moisture"],"marks":1},{"any":["oxygen","air"],"marks":1}]

### we.science.c2.c2-rusting-and-iron.02 [verified] paper C2 calc
STEM: A blast furnace is charged with haematite, coke and limestone.
(a) Write the balanced symbol equation for the reduction of iron(III) oxide by carbon monoxide. [3]
(b) Calculate the maximum mass of iron that could be obtained from 320 tonnes of iron(III) oxide. The relative formula mass of iron(III) oxide is 160 and the relative atomic mass of iron is 56. Give your answer to the nearest tonne. [3]
  FIG: svg — A section through a blast furnace with the charge entering at the top, hot air blasted in near the base, the reactions listed down the middle, and tap holes for molten slag above molten iron.
  1. Reactants Fe2O3 and CO; products Fe and CO2.   — Write every formula before you balance. Carbon monoxide is CO and carbon dioxide is CO2 — one oxygen apart, and the difference is the whole reaction.  (P1)
  2. Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2. Two irons, so 2Fe; three oxygens to move, so 3CO and 3CO2.   — Balance the iron first, then count the oxygens: 3 in the ore plus 3 in the carbon monoxide makes 6, and 3 carbon dioxides also hold 6.  (P2,P3)
  3. From the equation, one Fe2O3 gives two Fe, so 160 tonnes of the ore would give 2 × 56 = 112 tonnes of iron.   — Take the ratio from the balanced equation, not from the formula. Missing the 2 in front of Fe halves the answer.  (P4)
  4. Mass of iron = 320 × 112 ÷ 160.   — Write the line out before you use the calculator; a written substitution is what earns the method mark if the arithmetic slips.  (P5)
  5. = 224 tonnes.   — Sense-check it: iron is most, but not all, of the ore, so the answer must be smaller than 320 and bigger than half of it.  (P6)
  FINAL: (a) Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2. (b) 224 tonnes of iron.

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.c2.c2-rusting-and-iron.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Prerequisite from §2.1 — you are not expected to have met rust prevention yet): Which is higher in the reactivity series, zinc or iron?
    ✔ zinc — Zinc is above iron, and that one fact is what makes galvanising and sacrificial protection work.
    ✘ iron [sci.metals.reactivity-order-not-recalled] — The order runs magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, copper, so zinc comes first. Section 4 of this lesson depends on it.
    ✘ neither: they are equal [sci.metals.reactivity-order-not-recalled] — They sit at different heights, and the gap between them is what protects a galvanised bucket.
- p2 (Prerequisite from the previous topic — oxidation and reduction with oxygen): What does reduction mean, in terms of oxygen?
    ✔ loss of oxygen — Loss of oxygen. The whole blast furnace is one long example of it.
    ✘ gain of oxygen [sci.redox.oxidation-given-for-reduction] — Gaining oxygen is oxidation. The redox topic sets this out, and section 6 here assumes it.
    ✘ losing mass [sci.redox.oxidation-given-for-reduction] — Reduction is not about mass; it is about losing oxygen, gaining hydrogen or gaining electrons.
- p3 (Prerequisite from C2 §2.9 — the composition of air): Which gas in the air makes up about a fifth of it and is needed for burning?
    ✔ oxygen — Oxygen, about 21%. It is one of the two things rusting needs.
    ✘ nitrogen [sci.gases.nitrogen-unreactive-triple-bond] — Nitrogen is the bigger share at about 78%, but it is very unreactive. The gas that matters here is oxygen.
    ✘ carbon dioxide [sci.gases.nitrogen-unreactive-triple-bond] — Carbon dioxide is a very small part of the air and takes no part in rusting.

### dx.science.c2.c2-rusting-and-iron.post (post) [verified]
- 01 (Name rust in full): What is the chemical name of rust?
    ✔ hydrated iron(III) oxide — Both marking words are there: hydrated, and the oxidation state of the iron.
    ✘ iron(III) oxide [sci.redox.rust-name-incomplete] — That is half the answer. Rust has water built into it, so the name starts with hydrated — the word Summer 2025 examiners record as commonly omitted.
    ✘ hydrated iron oxide [sci.redox.rust-name-incomplete] — Also half. Iron forms two oxides, so the name has to say which: iron(III).
    ✘ iron hydroxide [sci.redox.rust-name-incomplete] — A hydroxide is a different compound. Rust is an oxide with water built into the solid, which is what hydrated means.
- 02 (State the purpose of the drying agent): In the conditions experiment, what is the anhydrous calcium chloride for?
    ✔ it removes the water from the air in the tube — It is a drying agent: it absorbs water that is already in the tube.
    ✘ it stops water getting into the tube [sci.redox.drying-agent-purpose] — The bung already does that. Examiners record this answer as earning nothing: the chemical removes water, it does not block it.
    ✘ it removes the oxygen from the air [sci.redox.drying-agent-purpose] — Removing oxygen is the other tube's job, done by boiling the water and adding oil.
    ✘ it stops the nail rusting [sci.redox.drying-agent-purpose] — That is the result, not the purpose. A general statement about preventing rust is not accepted.
- 03 (State the purpose of the oil layer): The water in one tube is boiled and then covered with a layer of oil. What is the oil for?
    ✔ it stops oxygen dissolving back into the water — The word dissolving is what earns it. Boiling removed the oxygen; the oil keeps it out of solution.
    ✘ it keeps oxygen out [sci.redox.oil-layer-purpose] — Examiners record this as too vague for the mark: say that it stops oxygen dissolving in the water.
    ✘ it stops the water evaporating [sci.redox.oil-layer-purpose] — The tube is stoppered, so evaporation is not the issue. The oil is there because of oxygen.
    ✘ it kills bacteria in the water [sci.redox.oil-layer-purpose] — Rusting is not biological. Examiners list sterilising as one of the wrong answers seen here.
- 04 (Explain galvanising): Which of these is a complete explanation of galvanising?
    ✔ the iron is coated with zinc — The metal has to be named. That is the fact Summer 2025 examiners record as missing.
    ✘ the iron is coated with metal [sci.rust.barrier-not-qualified] — Which metal? The answer is zinc, and without it there is no mark.
    ✘ the iron is painted [sci.redox.galvanising-zinc-omitted] — Painting is a different barrier method. Galvanising is specifically a zinc coating.
    ✘ the iron is dipped in acid to clean it [sci.redox.galvanising-zinc-omitted] — Cleaning is a preparation step, not the protection. The protection is the zinc layer.
- 05 (Explain sacrificial protection): A block of magnesium is bolted to a steel pipeline. Why does the pipeline not rust?
    ✔ magnesium is more reactive, so it corrodes instead of the iron — More reactive means it gives up its electrons first, so it is the one that goes.
    ✘ the magnesium rusts in place of the iron [sci.rust.zinc-rusts-instead] — Only iron rusts; other metals corrode. Summer 2024 examiners recorded 'zinc rusting in place of iron' as not credited, and the same applies to magnesium.
    ✘ magnesium is less reactive, so it protects the iron [sci.rust.zinc-rusts-instead] — It is the other way round. A less reactive metal, such as tin, makes a scratched object rust faster.
    ✘ the magnesium seals the whole pipeline [sci.rust.barrier-not-qualified] — One bolted-on block cannot cover a pipeline. This is sacrificial protection, not a barrier.
- 06 (Name the raw materials): Which substance is blasted into the base of a blast furnace?
    ✔ hot air — Hot air, and the word hot matters: Summer 2023 examiners record very few candidates getting it.
    ✘ cold water [sci.blast.hot-air-not-named] — Water would cool the furnace. What goes in at the base is hot air, to burn the coke.
    ✘ carbon monoxide [sci.blast.hot-air-not-named] — Carbon monoxide is made inside the furnace from carbon dioxide and coke; it is not pumped in.
    ✘ limestone [sci.blast.hot-air-not-named] — Limestone is one of the three solids tipped in at the top, not blasted in at the base.
- 07 (Identify slag and molten iron): Two liquids collect at the bottom of a blast furnace. Which is on top, and why?
    ✔ the slag, because it is less dense than the molten iron — Slag floats, so it is tapped off through the higher hole and the iron through the lower one.
    ✘ the molten iron, because it is less dense than the slag [sci.blast.slag-and-iron-swapped] — Iron is much denser, so it sinks. Summer 2023 examiners record candidates labelling these two the wrong way round.
    ✘ the slag, because it is hotter [sci.blast.slag-and-iron-swapped] — Slag does float, but density is the reason, not temperature.
    ✘ they mix, so neither is on top [sci.blast.slag-and-iron-swapped] — They separate into two layers, which is exactly why the furnace has two tap holes.

      SOLUTION: Set up three stoppered boiling tubes, each holding a clean iron nail, and leave them side by side for a week. The first holds the nail in ordinary tap water with air above it; this is the control, and its nail rusts, which shows what a normal result looks like. The second holds the nail in dry air above anhydrous calcium chloride, a drying agent that removes the water from the air; its nail does not rust. The third holds the nail in water that has been boiled to drive out the dissolved oxygen, with a layer of oil on top to stop oxygen dissolving back in; its nail does not rust either. Because a nail rusts only when both water and oxygen are present, rusting needs both, and the orange solid that forms is hydrated iron(III) oxide. The hull can be protected in two ways. A barrier method, such as painting it or galvanising it with a layer of zinc, keeps the water and the oxygen off the steel. Sacrificial protection bolts blocks of a more reactive metal, zinc or magnesium, onto the hull; because they are higher in the reactivity series they corrode in place of the iron, and they are replaced when they are eaten away.
