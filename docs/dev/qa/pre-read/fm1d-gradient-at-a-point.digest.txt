## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.01 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: A curve is defined by the equation $y = x^{3} - 4x^{2} + 2x + 5$
Find the gradient of this curve at the point where $x = 3$, and write down the coordinates of that point.
  FIG: svg — The curve with its tangent at the point (3, 2), and a gradient triangle showing a rise of 5 for a run of 1.
  1. Differentiate: $\frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^{2} - 8x + 2$.   — Write the derivative before anything else. It is the first mark on the scheme, and it is available whether or not the rest of the question goes well.  (MW1)
  2. Substitute $x = 3$ into the derivative: $3(3)^{2} - 8(3) + 2$.   — Brackets round the 3 keep the square attached to the 3 and not to the coefficient. The examiners ask for this habit because it is where the arithmetic slips start.  (M1)  WHY: ✔Because the gradient comes from the derivative | ✘Because 3 is the y-coordinate | ✘Because the curve passes through 3
  3. Work it out: $27 - 24 + 2 = 5$. The gradient is $5$.   — One number, and it is positive, which agrees with the picture: the curve is climbing at this point.  (W1)
  4. Now substitute $x = 3$ into the **curve**: $27 - 36 + 6 + 5 = 2$.   — The height of the point has nothing to do with the derivative. Two substitutions, two expressions, two lines of working.  (M2)
  5. So the point is $(3, 2)$ and the gradient there is $5$.   — Answer both halves of the question explicitly. A bare number leaves the examiner guessing which of the two you meant.  (W2)
  FINAL: The gradient is $5$, at the point $(3, 2)$
  TWIN: Find the gradient of the curve $y = x^{3} - 2x^{2} - 5x + 1$ at the point where $x = -1$.  → numeric 2 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=decimal/fraction

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Read the gradient of a straight line from its equation): Three quick checks on what this lesson is built from. None of them is the new method, so answer from what you already know.
What is the gradient of the straight line $y = 4x - 9$?
    ✔ $4$ — For a straight line the gradient is the coefficient of $x$, and it is the same everywhere along the line. A curve is exactly the case where that stops being true.
    ✘ $-9$ [fm.calc.gradient-read-as-coefficient] — That is the intercept, where the line crosses the $y$-axis. The gradient is the number multiplying $x$.
    ✘ $-\frac{9}{4}$ [fm.calc.gradient-read-as-coefficient] — That is where the line crosses the $x$-axis. The gradient is the coefficient of $x$.
- p2 (Substitute a negative value with brackets): Work out $3(-2)^{2} + 5(-2)$.
    ✔ $2$ — $3 \times 4 = 12$ and $5 \times -2 = -10$, so $12 - 10 = 2$. Squaring a negative makes it positive; multiplying does not.
    ✘ $-22$ [fm.calc.gradient-from-y-not-derivative] — The square came out negative. $(-2)^{2}$ is $4$, because the bracket squares the whole of $-2$.
    ✘ $22$ [fm.calc.gradient-from-y-not-derivative] — The second term is $5 \times -2 = -10$, so it is taken away rather than added.
- p3 (Differentiate a quadratic (the previous topic)): Differentiate $y = 5x^{2} - 4x + 1$.
    ✔ $10x - 4$ — Multiply by the power, step the power down, and send the constant to zero.
    ✘ $10x - 3$ [fm.calc.constant-term-kept] — The $+ 1$ differentiates to zero, so it does not appear in the gradient rule.
    ✘ $5x - 4$ [fm.calc.coefficient-not-multiplied] — The powers stepped down correctly. Each coefficient is multiplied by the power it came from as well.

### dx.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.post (post) [verified]
- q1 (Substitute into the derivative): A curve has $y = x^{2} + 3x$. What is the gradient at the point where $x = 2$?
    ✔ $7$ — $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2x + 3$, and at $x = 2$ that is $7$.
    ✘ $10$ [fm.calc.gradient-from-y-not-derivative] — That is the height of the curve at $x = 2$, not its steepness. The gradient comes from $\frac{dy}{dx}$.
    ✘ $3$ [fm.calc.gradient-read-as-coefficient] — That is the coefficient of $x$ in the curve's equation. Reading a gradient off a coefficient only works for a straight line.
- q2 (Substitute a negative value into the derivative): A curve has $\frac{dy}{dx} = 10x - 4$. What is the gradient at the point where $x = -1$?
    ✔ $-14$ — $10(-1) - 4 = -14$. The sign travels through the substitution.
    ✘ $6$ [fm.calc.negative-power-differentiation] — The minus sign was dropped on the way in. Substituting $-1$ gives $-10$, not $10$.
    ✘ $-4$ [fm.calc.gradient-read-as-coefficient] — That is the coefficient of $x$ in the curve. The gradient of a curve is never read off a coefficient.
- q3 (Set the derivative equal to a stated gradient): A curve has $\frac{dy}{dx} = 4x - 7$. Where is the gradient equal to $9$?
    ✔ At $x = 4$ — Solve $4x - 7 = 9$: $4x = 16$, so $x = 4$.
    ✘ At $x = 1.75$ [fm.calc.default-to-derivative-zero] — That is the solution of $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$, which finds the flat point. The question gave a gradient of 9.
    ✘ At $x = 9$ [fm.calc.default-to-derivative-zero] — The 9 is the value of the gradient, not the value of $x$. It goes on the right-hand side of the equation you solve.
- q4 (Find a point from a stated gradient): A curve has $y = 2x^{2} - 7x + 3$ and its gradient is $1$ at one point. What are the coordinates of that point?
    ✔ $(2, -3)$ — $4x - 7 = 1$ gives $x = 2$, and substituting that into the curve gives $y = -3$.
    ✘ $(2, 1)$ [fm.calc.stationary-y-from-derivative] — The $x$ is right. The second coordinate is the height of the curve there, not the gradient you were given.
    ✘ $(\frac{7}{4}, -\frac{25}{8})$ [fm.calc.default-to-derivative-zero] — That is the point where the gradient is zero. The question named a gradient of 1, so that is what $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is set equal to.

## FIND THE MISTAKE
### ftm.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.01 [verified] fm.calc.default-to-derivative-zero · earned MW1 · ccea-cer:further-maths:2025-summer:FM1:Q13
STEM: Cara was asked: the curve $y = 2x^{2} - 7x + 4$ has gradient $9$ at the point $P$; find the coordinates of $P$. Her working:
    1. y = 2x^2 - 7x + 4
    2. dy/dx = 4x - 7
  ✘ 3. 4x - 7 = 0
    4. x = 1.75
    5. y = -2.125, so P = (1.75, -2.125)
  WRONG: Line 2 is right, and lines 4 and 5 are careful, accurate work.
Line 3 is where it goes: the derivative was set equal to zero out of habit, and the question had already said the gradient is $9$. So the equation should read $4x - 7 = 9$.
Everything after line 3 then answers a question nobody asked, which is why nothing in the arithmetic looks wrong.
  FIX: 4x - 7 = 9 / 4x = 16 / x = 4 / y = 2(4)^2 - 7(4) + 4 = 8 / P = (4, 8)
  FEEDBACK: The differentiation mark is yours. Before writing the equation, read the sentence once more and say what $\frac{dy}{dx}$ has been told to equal: zero only when the question says flat, stationary or turning point. The examiners report this exact substitution of habit for reading, and it costs two marks every time.

## RETRIEVAL PROMPTS
- rp.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.01 [verified] (procedure) How do you find the gradient of a curve at the point where $x = a$?  →  Differentiate to get $\frac{dy}{dx}$, then substitute $a$ into that derivative.
- rp.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.02 [verified] (trap) Where does the $y$-coordinate of the point come from?  →  From the equation of the curve, never from $\frac{dy}{dx}$.
- rp.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.03 [verified] (trap) A question says the gradient of the curve at $P$ is 5. What equation do you solve?  →  $\frac{dy}{dx} = 5$. Zero belongs there only when the question asks for a flat or stationary point.
- rp.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.04 [verified] (qa) Why can a curve not have one gradient the way a straight line does?  →  Its steepness changes from point to point, so the gradient is a function of $x$ rather than a single number.
- rp.fm.u1.gradient-at-a-point.05 [verified] (trap) What should you write when substituting a negative value into $\frac{dy}{dx}$?  →  Put the value in brackets, for example $4(-2) - 7$, so the sign and any power stay attached to it.
