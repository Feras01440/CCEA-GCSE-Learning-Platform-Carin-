## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u2.vertical-motion-under-gravity.01 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A ball is thrown straight up from level ground with a speed of 24 m/s.

(i) Calculate the greatest height the ball reaches.
(ii) Calculate the time it takes to come back to the ground.
(iii) Calculate the speed at which it lands.

Take $g = 10$ m/s².
  FIG: svg — A ball thrown straight up from level ground at 24 metres per second, rising to a greatest height of 28.8 metres where its velocity is zero, then falling back. An arrow on the left marks upwards as the positive direction.
  1. Up is positive, so $u = 24$, $a = -10$ m/s².   — Fix the positive direction before any numbers go into a formula. Gravity pulls downwards for the whole flight, on the way up and on the way down, so the acceleration is negative the whole time.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔Because up is positive and gravity acts downwards, so $a = -10$ throughout | ✘Because the ball is rising, so $a = +10$ until it stops | ✘Because the acceleration changes sign at the greatest height
  2. At the greatest height $v = 0$, so $0 = 24^2 + 2(-10)s$ gives $s = 28.8$ m.   — The question gives a speed and asks for a height, with no time mentioned, so $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ is the formula that holds those three letters and nothing else.  (MW1,W1)
  3. Back at the ground $s = 0$, so $0 = 24t - 5t^2$, giving $t = 0$ or $t = 4.8$ s.   — Landing means the displacement from the start is zero again, not that the distance is zero. The root $t = 0$ is the throw itself, so the flight time is the other one.  (MW1,W1)
  4. $v = 24 + (-10) \times 4.8 = -24$ m/s, so the speed is $24$ m/s.   — The minus sign says the ball is travelling downwards, which is what you expect. Speed is the size of that velocity, so the answer on the line is positive.  (MW1)
  FINAL: (i) $28.8$ m  (ii) $4.8$ s  (iii) $24$ m/s
  TWIN: A ball is thrown straight up from level ground with a speed of 18 m/s. Calculate the greatest height it reaches. Take $g = 10$ m/s².  → numeric 16.2 m tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=decimal/fraction

### we.fm.u2.vertical-motion-under-gravity.02 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A stone is thrown vertically upwards at 15 m/s from the top of a wall, 20 m above the ground.

(i) Calculate how far the stone rises above the top of the wall.
(ii) Calculate the time it takes to reach the ground.
(iii) Calculate the speed with which it strikes the ground.

Take $g = 10$ m/s².
  FIG: svg — A stone thrown upwards at 15 metres per second from the top of a wall 20 metres above the ground. It rises 11.25 metres to its greatest height, 31.25 metres above the ground, then falls the whole 31.25 metres to the ground.
  1. Up is positive from the top of the wall, so $u = 15$, $a = -10$, and the ground is at $s = -20$ m.   — Measure every displacement from the point of release, because that is where the clock starts and where $u$ belongs. The ground is below that point, so its displacement is negative.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔Because the ground is 20 m below the point of release, and down is the negative direction | ✘Because the ground is always at $s = 0$ | ✘Because the stone falls 20 m, so $s = +20$
  2. At the greatest height $v = 0$: $0 = 15^2 - 2(10)s$, so $s = 11.25$ m.   — Part (i) asks how far it rises above the wall, so the answer is measured from the release point, not from the ground.  (MW1,W1)
  3. $-20 = 15t - 5t^2$, so $5t^2 - 15t - 20 = 0$ and $t^2 - 3t - 4 = 0$.   — One equation carries the whole flight, up and down together, because the acceleration never changes. Put the landing displacement in and solve the quadratic.  (MW1,W1)
  4. $(t - 4)(t + 1) = 0$, so $t = 4$ s. The negative root is discarded.   — A negative time would be before the stone was thrown, so it is not part of this flight. Say so on the page rather than rubbing it out.  (W1)
  5. $v^2 = 15^2 + 2(-10)(-20) = 625$, so the speed is $25$ m/s.   — Two minus signs multiply to a plus, which is why the landing speed of $25$ m/s is larger than the $15$ m/s it was thrown at: the stone finishes below where it started.  (MW1)
  FINAL: (i) $11.25$ m  (ii) $4$ s  (iii) $25$ m/s
  TWIN: A ball is thrown vertically upwards at 20 m/s from the top of a tower 15 m above the ground. Calculate the speed with which it strikes the ground. Take $g = 10$ m/s², and round to 2 decimal places.  → numeric 26.46 m/s tol={"type":"dp","places":2} forms=decimal/fraction

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u2.vertical-motion-under-gravity.pre (pre) [verified]
- 01 (Prerequisite: solving a quadratic by factorising): From an earlier topic: solve $t^2 - 4t - 5 = 0$.
    ✔ $t = 5$ or $t = -1$ — $(t - 5)(t + 1) = 0$, so the roots are $5$ and $-1$.
    ✘ $t = -5$ or $t = 1$ — Both signs are the other way round. Check by multiplying out: $(t + 5)(t - 1) = t^2 + 4t - 5$.
    ✘ $t = 5$ or $t = 1$ — The two numbers must multiply to $-5$, so one of them is negative.
    ✘ $t = 4$ or $t = -5$ — That reads the coefficients straight off. Factorise first: the pair must add to $-4$ and multiply to $-5$.
- 02 (Prerequisite: rearranging a formula): From an earlier topic: rearrange $v = u + at$ to make $t$ the subject.
    ✔ $t = \dfrac{v - u}{a}$ — Take $u$ from both sides, then divide both sides by $a$.
    ✘ $t = \dfrac{u - v}{a}$ — The subtraction is the wrong way round. Moving $u$ to the left leaves $v - u$ on top.
    ✘ $t = a(v - u)$ — $a$ is multiplying $t$, so it is removed by dividing, not by multiplying.
    ✘ $t = v - u - a$ — $at$ is a product, so $a$ cannot be moved by subtracting it.
- 03 (Prerequisite: squares and square roots): From an earlier topic: find $\sqrt{625}$.
    ✔ $25$ — $25 \times 25 = 625$.
    ✘ $312.5$ — That halves 625. A square root asks which number multiplies by itself to give 625.
    ✘ $62.5$ — That divides by 10. Try squaring a few whole numbers: $20^2 = 400$ and $30^2 = 900$, so the root is between them.
    ✘ $35$ — $35^2 = 1225$, which is too large.

### dx.fm.u2.vertical-motion-under-gravity.post (post) [verified]
- 04 (The velocity at the greatest height): A ball is thrown straight up. What is its velocity at the instant it is at its greatest height?
    ✔ $0$ m/s — It has stopped rising and has not started falling, so for that instant the velocity is zero. That is what makes the height findable.
    ✘ The speed it was thrown at [fm.kin.velocity-not-zero-at-top] — That is its speed at the start and again when it returns to the throwing point, not at the top.
    ✘ $-10$ m/s [fm.kin.gravity-sign] — $-10$ is the acceleration, in m/s². The velocity at the top is zero while the acceleration is not.
    ✘ Half the speed it was thrown at [fm.kin.velocity-not-zero-at-top] — The velocity falls steadily to zero, so half the launch speed happens part of the way up, not at the top.
- 05 (The sign of the acceleration): Upwards is taken as positive. A stone thrown upwards is now falling back down. What is its acceleration?
    ✔ $-10$ m/s² — Gravity acts downwards for the whole flight, so with up positive the acceleration stays negative, rising or falling.
    ✘ $+10$ m/s² [fm.kin.gravity-sign] — The stone is speeding up, but it is moving in the negative direction, so the acceleration is still negative. Changing the sign halfway breaks the one convention the question set.
    ✘ $0$ m/s² [fm.kin.gravity-sign] — The velocity is changing every second, so the acceleration is not zero. Only the velocity is zero, and only at the top.
    ✘ It depends on how fast the stone was thrown [fm.kin.gravity-sign] — The launch speed sets $u$, not $a$. Gravity gives every body the same acceleration here.
- 06 (Choosing the equation for a greatest height): A body is thrown up at $u$ m/s from level ground. Which formula reaches its greatest height in one step?
    ✔ $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ — It holds $u$, $a$, $s$ and $v$, and $v = 0$ at the top. No time is needed.
    ✘ $v = u + at$ [fm.kin.wrong-equation-chosen] — That gives the time to the top, not the height. It would work as a first step, but then a second formula is still needed.
    ✘ $s = \tfrac{1}{2}(u + v)t$ [fm.kin.wrong-equation-chosen] — The time is not known yet, so this one has two unknowns in it.
    ✘ $F = ma$ [fm.kin.wrong-equation-chosen] — That is Newton's Second Law, which relates a force to an acceleration. It carries no displacement.
- 07 (Landing below the point of projection): A stone is thrown upwards at 15 m/s from the top of a wall 20 m high. Taking up as positive, what is its displacement when it reaches the ground?
    ✔ $-20$ m — Displacement is measured from the point of release, and the ground is below it, so the sign is negative.
    ✘ $+20$ m [fm.kin.height-from-wrong-level] — The stone finishes 20 m below where it left the hand. A positive $s$ would put it above the wall.
    ✘ $-31.25$ m [fm.kin.height-from-wrong-level] — $31.25$ m is the distance from the greatest height down to the ground, not the displacement from the point of release.
    ✘ $0$ m [fm.kin.height-from-wrong-level] — Zero displacement would mean the stone came back to the top of the wall, which is not where the question stops it.
- 08 (Two times at one height): Solving for the time a ball is at a given height gives $t = 1$ and $t = 4$. The question asks when it passes that height on the way down. Which time is it?
    ✔ $t = 4$ s — The ball passes the level once going up and once coming down, so the later root is the descent.
    ✘ $t = 1$ s [fm.kin.first-root-taken] — That is the first pass, on the way up. Both roots are real flights of the same ball; the wording picks which one.
    ✘ $t = 5$ s [fm.kin.first-root-taken] — Adding the roots does not answer anything here. Each root is a time in its own right.
    ✘ $t = 3$ s [fm.kin.first-root-taken] — The gap between the roots is the time spent above that level, not the time of the second pass.
