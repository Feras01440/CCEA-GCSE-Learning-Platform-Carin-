## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u1.area-under-curve.01 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: Find the area enclosed by the curve $y = x^{2} + 1$, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 0$ and $x = 3$.
  FIG: svg — The curve y equals x squared plus 1 with the region between it, the x-axis and the vertical lines x equals 0 and x equals 3 shaded.
  1. Write the integral: $\int_{0}^{3} \left(x^{2} + 1\right) dx$.   — The left ordinate is the bottom limit and the right one is the top limit. This line is the first mark and it costs no arithmetic, so write it even if the rest defeats you.  (MW1)
  2. Integrate, simplifying each term as you write it: $\left[\frac{1}{3}x^{3} + x\right]_{0}^{3}$.   — Simplifying now rather than after the limits go in is what keeps the arithmetic small; the examiners name unsimplified terms as a source of slips.  (M2)  WHY: ✔Because the area is the integral of the curve's own equation | ✘Because the limits have to be differentiated first | ✘Because the constant of integration is needed here
  3. Top limit first: at $x = 3$ the bracket is $12$. Then the bottom: at $x = 0$ it is $0$.   — Putting each substitution in its own bracket is what stops a sign going astray when the bottom value is negative.  (M3)
  4. Subtract, top minus bottom: $12 - \left(0\right) = 12$.   — The value is positive, which matches the picture: the whole region sits above the axis. A sanity check like that takes a second and catches a reversed subtraction.  (W1)
  FINAL: $12$
  TWIN: Find the area enclosed by the curve $y = x^{2} + 2x$, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 0$ and $x = 3$.  → numeric 18 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.0005} forms=decimal/fraction

### we.fm.u1.area-under-curve.02 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: Find the area enclosed by the curve $y = x^{2} - 4x$, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 0$ and $x = 4$.
  FIG: svg — The curve y equals x squared minus 4x between x equals 0 and x equals 4, lying below the x-axis, with the region shaded.
  1. Write the integral: $\int_{0}^{4} \left(x^{2} - 4x\right) dx$.   — The set-up is the same whichever side of the axis the region is on. Nothing about the limits changes.  (MW1)
  2. Integrate: $\left[\frac{1}{3}x^{3} - 2x^{2}\right]_{0}^{4}$.   — Raise each index by one and divide by the new index. The examiners see both halves of that rule dropped, so say it to yourself as you write.  (M2)
  3. At $x = 4$ the bracket is $-\frac{32}{3}$; at $x = 0$ it is $0$. Subtracting gives $-\frac{32}{3}$.   — A negative result is information, not a mistake. It says every strip in this region hangs below the axis.  (M3)  WHY: ✔Because the region lies below the x-axis, so every strip has a negative height | ✘Because the limits were written the wrong way round | ✘Because the curve was differentiated instead of integrated
  4. The region lies below the $x$-axis, so the area is $\frac{32}{3}$.   — Write that sentence. It shows the examiner the sign was read rather than quietly dropped, and it is where the last mark lives.  (W1)
  FINAL: $\frac{32}{3}$
  TWIN: Find the area enclosed by the curve $y = x^{2} - 9$, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 0$ and $x = 3$.  → numeric 18 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.0005} forms=decimal/fraction

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u1.area-under-curve.pre (pre) [draft]
- p1 (Integrate a single power term): Three quick checks on what this lesson is built from. None of them is the new idea, so answer from what you already know.
What is $\int \left(3x^{2}\right) dx$?
    ✔ $x^{3} + c$ — Raise the index to $3$ and divide the coefficient by $3$, which leaves $x^{3}$.
    ✘ $x^{2} + c$ — The coefficient was divided but the index stayed at $2$. Both halves of the rule apply.
    ✘ $3x^{3} + c$ — The index went up but the coefficient was never divided by the new index.
- p2 (Evaluate a definite integral): What is the value of $\int_{1}^{2} \left(3x^{2}\right) dx$?
    ✔ $7$ — $8 - \left(1\right) = 7$.
    ✘ $8$ — Only the top limit has been substituted. The bottom one still has to be taken away.
    ✘ $-7$ — The subtraction is the wrong way round. It is the top limit take away the bottom.
- p3 (Find the x-intercepts of a quadratic): Where does the curve $y = x^{2} - 4$ cross the $x$-axis?
    ✔ $x = 2$ and $x = -2$ — Setting $y = 0$ gives $x^{2} = 4$, so $x = 2$ or $x = -2$.
    ✘ $x = 4$ and $x = -4$ — That is the value of $x^{2}$, not of $x$. Take the square root of $4$.
    ✘ $x = 2$ only — A square root has two values, so the curve meets the axis twice.

### dx.fm.u1.area-under-curve.post (post) [draft]
- d1 (Set up the integral with the right limits): Which integral gives the area between the curve $y = x^{2} + 2x$, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 0$ and $x = 3$?
    ✔ $\int_{0}^{3} \left(x^{2} + 2x\right) dx$ — The curve's own equation goes inside, with the left ordinate at the bottom and the right one at the top.
    ✘ $\int_{3}^{0} \left(x^{2} + 2x\right) dx$ [fm.int.limits-reversed] — The limits are the wrong way round, which changes the sign of everything that follows.
    ✘ $\int_{0}^{3} \left(\frac{1}{3}x^{3} + x^{2}\right) dx$ [fm.int.limits-into-integrand] — That is the integrated expression, already integrated once. What goes inside the integral is the curve itself.
- d2 (Report a below-axis region): A region lies below the $x$-axis and its integral works out as $-18$. What is the area?
    ✔ $18$ — The minus sign says the region is below the axis; the area is its size.
    ✘ $-18$ [fm.int.negative-area-left] — An area cannot be negative. The integral tells you where the region is, and the answer line wants how big it is.
    ✘ $0$ [fm.int.negative-area-left] — The region has a real size. A negative integral is not an empty region.
- d3 (Split a crossing region at the root): The curve $y = x^{2} - 4$ crosses the $x$-axis at $x = 2$. What is the total area between the curve, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 0$ and $x = 4$?
    ✔ $16$ — Split at the root, take the size of each piece, then add: $\frac{16}{3} + \frac{32}{3}$.
    ✘ $\frac{16}{3}$ [fm.int.region-not-split-at-root] — That is one integral straight across the root, so the piece below the axis cancelled part of the piece above it.
    ✘ $\frac{32}{3}$ [fm.int.region-not-split-at-root] — That is only the piece above the axis. The piece below it still has an area to add.
- d4 (Find the limits when none are printed): A question asks for the area enclosed by the curve $y = -x^{2} + 6x$ and the $x$-axis, and prints no ordinates. What are the limits?
    ✔ $x = 0$ and $x = 6$, where the curve meets the $x$-axis — Setting $y = 0$ gives the two intercepts, and those are the limits.
    ✘ $x = 0$ and $x = 1$, because no other values are given [fm.int.limits-into-integrand] — Limits are never invented. Solve $y = 0$ to find where the enclosed region starts and stops.
    ✘ $y = 0$ and $y = 9$, the smallest and largest heights [fm.int.limits-into-integrand] — The limits of $\int y \, dx$ are values of $x$, not of $y$.
- d5 (Apply both halves of the integration rule): Integrating $y = x^{2} + 2x$ gives which expression?
    ✔ $\frac{1}{3}x^{3} + x^{2}$ — Each index rises by one and each coefficient is divided by its new index.
    ✘ $x^{3} + 2x^{2}$ [fm.int.coefficient-not-divided] — The indices are right and no coefficient was divided by the new index.
    ✘ $\frac{1}{3}x^{2} + x$ [fm.int.power-not-raised] — The coefficients were divided and the indices never moved.

  (main) [3] The diagram shows the line $y = 2x + 1$.
Find the area of the shaded region, which is enclosed by the line, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = 1$ and $x = 4$.
      ANSWER: numeric 18 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.0005} forms=decimal/fraction
      MW1 MW1: $\int_{1}^{4} \left(2x + 1\right) dx$, with both limits
      M1 M2: $\left[x^{2} + x\right]_{1}^{4}$, each term simplified
      W1 W1: $18$ ft
      SOLUTION: Area $= \int_{1}^{4} \left(2x + 1\right) dx = \left[x^{2} + x\right]_{1}^{4}$. At $x = 4$ the bracket is $20$; at $x = 1$ it is $2$. Subtracting: $20 - \left(2\right) = 18$.
      ERR [fm.int.limits-reversed] {"kind":"numeric","value":-18} → The integrating and both substitutions are right, so the method marks stand. The subtraction is the wrong way round: it is the top limit take away the bottom, giving $18$. (2)
      HINTS: Write the integral with $1$ at the bottom and $4$ at the top. || Integrate, then substitute the top limit and the bottom one.

### q.fm.u1.area-under-curve.0011 [verified] exam-style · diff 4 · untiered · FM1 calc · 6 marks · (a)find2|(b)find4 · Intercepts then area, the shape a Unit 1 curve question takes in its closing parts

## RETRIEVAL PROMPTS
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.01 [draft] (formula) Write the integral for the area between a curve, the $x$-axis and the ordinates $x = a$ and $x = b$.  →  Area $= \int_{a}^{b} y \, dx$, with the curve's own equation in place of $y$, $a$ at the bottom and $b$ at the top.
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.02 [draft] (procedure) Give the four steps of an area question in order.  →  Write the integral with both limits; integrate and simplify each term; substitute the top limit and then the bottom one, each in its own bracket; subtract top minus bottom and report the size.
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.03 [draft] (qa) What does a negative definite integral tell you about a region?  →  That the region lies below the $x$-axis. The area is the size of the value, so drop the minus sign and say why.
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.04 [draft] (trap) A question asks for the area enclosed by a curve and the $x$-axis and prints no ordinates. Where do the limits come from?  →  From the curve itself: set $y = 0$ and solve, and the two $x$-intercepts are the limits.
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.05 [draft] (procedure) What do you do when the curve crosses the $x$-axis between the two ordinates?  →  Split the integral at the root, work out each piece separately, take the size of each, then add them. One integral straight across would let the two pieces cancel.
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.06 [draft] (trap) Which line of an area question earns the first mark, even when the rest goes wrong?  →  The integral written out with both limits and the curve's equation inside it.
- rp.fm.u1.area-under-curve.07 [draft] (trap) Why is the constant of integration never needed in an area question?  →  Because the integral is definite: the constant appears in both substitutions and cancels in the subtraction.
