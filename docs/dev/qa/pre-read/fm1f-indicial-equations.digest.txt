## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.fm.u1.indicial-equations.01 [verified] paper FM1 calc
STEM: Solve the equation $5^{3x - 2} = 40$, giving your answer correct to two decimal places.
  FIG: svg — Five lines of working solving 5 to the power 3x minus 2 equals 40, each with a short note beside it.
  1. Take logarithms of both sides: $\log 5^{3x - 2} = \log 40$.   — Both sides, always. Taking logs of one side alone changes the equation, and this line is the first mark whatever happens next.  (M1)
  2. The power law brings the whole index down, in a bracket: $(3x - 2) \log 5 = \log 40$.   — The bracket is not decoration. Without it the logarithm multiplies only the constant, and the equation stops being the one that was set.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔Because $\log a^{n} = n \log a$ applies to the whole index | ✘Because $\log 5$ can be cancelled from both sides | ✘Because the index can be divided out
  3. Divide both sides by $\log 5$: $3x - 2 = \frac{\log 40}{\log 5}$.   — Leave it as a quotient of logarithms. Writing $\log 40 - \log 5$ here is the slip the reports name, and it changes the answer.  (M2)
  4. Now evaluate, once: $\frac{\log 40}{\log 5} = 2.29203$, so $3x = 4.29203$ and $x = 1.430677$.   — One calculator step for the whole expression. Rounding the logarithms on the way in moves the second decimal place, which is where the last mark sits.  (M3)
  5. To two decimal places, $x = 1.43$.   — Check it: $5^{3(1.43) - 2} = 39.87$, which is the right-hand side to the accuracy carried.  (W1)
  FINAL: $x = 1.43$
  TWIN: Solve the equation $7^{3x - 1} = 200$, giving your answer correct to two decimal places.  → numeric 1.24 tol={"type":"dp","places":2} forms=decimal/fraction

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u1.indicial-equations.pre (pre) [draft]
- p1 (The power law): Three quick checks on what this lesson is built from. None of them is the new method, so answer from what you already know.
What is $\log a^{n}$ written another way?
    ✔ $n \log a$ — The power law brings the index down in front. It is the move this whole lesson rests on.
    ✘ $(\log a)^{n}$ — The index belongs to $a$ inside the logarithm, not to the logarithm itself.
    ✘ $\log n + \log a$ — That would be $\log(na)$. An index is not a factor inside the logarithm.
- p2 (A linear equation with the unknown on both sides): Solve $5x - 2 = 3x + 8$.
    ✔ $x = 5$ — $2x = 10$, so $x = 5$. The last half of every indicial equation is exactly this.
    ✘ $x = 3$ — The constant moved without changing sign. From $5x - 3x = 8 + 2$, $2x = 10$.
    ✘ $x = 1.25$ — The $x$ terms were added rather than subtracted: $5x - 3x = 2x$, not $8x$.
- p3 (Recognising a shared base): Write $4$ as a power of $2$.
    ✔ $2^{2}$ — $2^{2} = 4$. Spotting a shared base can finish a question without logarithms at all.
    ✘ $2^{4}$ — $2^{4} = 16$, which is too big. The index is the number of twos multiplied.
    ✘ $2^{1}$ — $2^{1} = 2$. One more factor of $2$ is needed.

### dx.fm.u1.indicial-equations.post (post) [draft]
- d1 (Bringing the index down with a bracket): Taking logarithms of $5^{x + 2} = 90$ gives which line?
    ✔ $(x + 2) \log 5 = \log 90$ — The whole index comes down together, inside a bracket.
    ✘ $x + 2 \log 5 = \log 90$ [fm.logs.brackets-omitted] — Without the bracket only the $2$ is multiplied by the logarithm, so the equation has changed.
    ✘ $\log(x + 2) + \log 5 = \log 90$ [fm.logs.sum-inside-the-log] — An index does not become a separate logarithm. The power law multiplies, it does not add.
- d2 (Isolating x from a product of logarithms): From $x \log 2 = \log 40$, what is $x$?
    ✔ $\frac{\log 40}{\log 2}$ — Divide both sides by $\log 2$, exactly as you would by any other number.
    ✘ $\log 40 - \log 2$ [fm.logs.quotient-as-divided-logs] — Subtracting logarithms is what $\log \frac{40}{2}$ means, which is a different number. Here you divide the two logarithms.
    ✘ $\log \frac{40}{2}$ [fm.logs.quotient-as-divided-logs] — That is the logarithm of a quotient. What the equation needs is a quotient of logarithms.
- d3 (Collecting the x terms): In $4x \log 2 + \log 2 = x \log 7 + 3 \log 7$, what happens when the $x$ term on the right is moved to the left?
    ✔ It becomes $-x \log 7$ on the left — A term crossing the equals sign changes sign, which is what lets $x$ be factorised out.
    ✘ It stays as $+x \log 7$ on the left [fm.logs.terms-not-collected] — The sign has to change. Leaving it positive adds the two $x$ terms instead of subtracting them.
    ✘ It cancels with $\log 2$ [fm.logs.terms-not-collected] — They are different numbers and one carries an $x$, so nothing cancels.
- d4 (Keeping accuracy to the last step): Solving $3^{x} = 11$, a student rounds $\log 11$ and $\log 3$ to two decimal places before dividing. What happens?
    ✔ The answer comes out as $2.17$ instead of $2.18$ — Rounding on the way in moves the second decimal place, and that is the place the mark is given for.
    ✘ Nothing changes, because rounding twice cancels out [fm.logs.rounded-too-early] — Two roundings do not cancel; here they move the answer by a hundredth.
    ✘ The answer is more accurate, because the numbers are simpler [fm.logs.rounded-too-early] — Simpler numbers are not more accurate ones. Keep the logarithms in the calculator until the last step.

### q.fm.u1.indicial-equations.0004 [verified] practice · diff 3 · untiered · FM1 calc · 3 marks · (main)solve3 · Both sides written as powers of the same base, where indices beat logarithms
  (main) [3] By writing both sides as powers of $2$, solve the equation
$4^{x} = 2^{x + 3}$
      ANSWER: numeric 3 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.0005} forms=decimal/fraction

      M1 M2: $2x = x + 3$

### q.fm.u1.indicial-equations.0005 [verified] practice · diff 3 · untiered · FM1 calc · 5 marks · (main)solve5 · Different bases on both sides, so the x terms have to be gathered
  (main) [5] Solve the equation
$5^{2x} = 3^{x + 4}$
Give your answer correct to two decimal places.
      ANSWER: numeric 2.07 tol={"type":"dp","places":2} forms=decimal/fraction
      M1 M1: taking logarithms of both sides
      MW1 MW1: $(2x) \log 5 = (x + 4) \log 3$, with brackets
      M1 M2: expanding and collecting the terms in $x$ on one side
      M1 M3: factorising $x$ out and dividing
      W1 W1: $2.07$ ft
      SOLUTION: Taking logs: $2x \log 5 = (x + 4) \log 3$. Expanding: $2x \log 5 = x \log 3 + 4 \log 3$. Collecting: $x(2 \log 5 - \log 3) = 4 \log 3$. So $x = 2.072596$, which is $2.07$ to two decimal places.
      ERR [fm.logs.terms-not-collected] {"kind":"numeric","value":1.02} → The $x$ term crossed the equals sign without changing sign, so the two $x$ terms were added instead of subtracted. With the sign changed, $x(2 \log 5 - \log 3) = 4 \log 3$ gives $2.07$. (2)
      HINTS: Logs of both sides, with brackets. || Every $\log$ is a number, so collect the $x$ terms as usual.

## FIND THE MISTAKE
### ftm.fm.u1.indicial-equations.01 [draft] fm.logs.brackets-omitted · earned M1 · ccea-cer:further-maths:2018-summer:FM1:Q6
STEM: Cara was asked to solve 7^3x - 1 = 200 correct to two decimal places. Her working:
    1. log(7^3x - 1) = log 200
  ✘ 2. 3x - 1 log 7 = log 200
    3. 3x = log 200 + log 7
    4. 3x = 3.146128
    5. x = 1.05
  WRONG: Line 1 is right: logarithms of both sides is always the first move.
Line 2 brings the index down without a bracket, so $\log 7$ multiplies only the $1$ and no longer multiplies the $3x$. Everything after that solves a different equation, which is why the answer is out by more than a rounding.
The line should read $(3x - 1) \log 7 = \log 200$, and then $x = 1.24$.
  FIX: (3x - 1) log 7 = log 200 / 3x - 1 = log 200 / log 7 = 2.722797 / x = 1.24
  FEEDBACK: Taking logarithms of both sides is the right first move and it earns its mark. The bracket is what the rest depends on: the index is one expression, so all of it multiplies the logarithm. Write the bracket as you bring the index down, every time, and the rest is a linear equation you can already do.

## RETRIEVAL PROMPTS
- rp.fm.u1.indicial-equations.01 [draft] (procedure) Give the first two lines of solving an equation with the unknown in the index.  →  Take logarithms of both sides, then use the power law to bring each whole index down in front of its logarithm, inside a bracket.
- rp.fm.u1.indicial-equations.02 [draft] (trap) Why must the index be written in a bracket when it comes down?  →  Because the whole index multiplies the logarithm. Without the bracket only the constant is multiplied, and the equation being solved is no longer the one that was set.
- rp.fm.u1.indicial-equations.03 [draft] (procedure) With logarithms on both sides, how is $x$ found?  →  Expand both brackets, move the $x$ terms to one side and the numbers to the other, factorise $x$ out, then divide.
- rp.fm.u1.indicial-equations.04 [draft] (trap) From $x \log 2 = \log 40$, what is the next line, and what is it not?  →  It is $x = \frac{\log 40}{\log 2}$, a quotient of the two logarithms. It is not $\log 40 - \log 2$, which is a different number.
- rp.fm.u1.indicial-equations.05 [draft] (trap) When should the calculator be used in an indicial equation, and to what accuracy is the answer given?  →  Only at the last step, on the whole expression at once. Give two decimal places unless the question says otherwise.
- rp.fm.u1.indicial-equations.06 [draft] (qa) When can an indicial equation be solved without logarithms at all?  →  When both sides can be written as powers of the same base. Then the indices are equal and the equation is linear at once.
