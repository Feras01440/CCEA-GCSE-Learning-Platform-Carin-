### we.fm.u3.tree-diagrams-probability.01 [verified] paper FM3 calc
STEM: A bag holds 8 counters: 5 red and 3 white. Two counters are taken out one after the other, without replacement.
Calculate the probability that the two counters are of different colours.
  FIG: svg — A two-stage probability tree for two counters taken without replacement from 8 counters, 5 red and 3 white, with second-stage denominators of 7.
  1. First stage: $P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{5}{8}$, $P(\text{white}) = \dfrac{3}{8}$.   — Nothing has been taken yet, so both denominators are 8. Writing the first stage down before the second keeps the two apart.  (M1)
  2. Second stage after red: $\dfrac{4}{7}$ red, $\dfrac{3}{7}$ white. After white: $\dfrac{5}{7}$ red, $\dfrac{2}{7}$ white.   — One counter has gone, so every second denominator is 7. Only the colour already taken loses one from its numerator.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔7, because one counter has been taken and not put back | ✘8, because the bag is the same bag | ✘6, because two counters are being taken altogether
  3. Red then white: $\dfrac{5}{8} \times \dfrac{3}{7} = \dfrac{15}{56}$. White then red: $\dfrac{3}{8} \times \dfrac{5}{7} = \dfrac{15}{56}$.   — Multiply along each branch. Two different colours can come out in either order, so both branches belong to the event.  (M1)
  4. $P(\text{different colours}) = \dfrac{15}{56} + \dfrac{15}{56} = \dfrac{15}{28}$   — Add across the branches. The check is \dfrac{15}{28} together with the same-colour answer \dfrac{13}{28}, which come to 1.  (W1)
  FINAL: $P(\text{different colours}) = \dfrac{15}{28}$
  TWIN: Using the same bag of 8 counters, calculate the probability that both counters taken are white.  → numeric 0.10714285714285714 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=fraction/decimal

### we.fm.u3.tree-diagrams-probability.02 [verified] paper FM3 calc
STEM: Two seeds are planted. Each grows, independently of the other, with probability $0.8$.
Calculate the probability that exactly one of the two seeds grows.
  FIG: svg — A two-stage probability tree for two seeds, each growing with probability 0.8 and failing with probability 0.2.
  1. $P(\text{grows}) = 0.8$, so $P(\text{fails}) = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2$.   — Find the second branch of each pair by subtracting from 1. The two branches leaving a point always total 1, which is the quickest check on a tree.  (MW1)
  2. The seeds are independent, so the second stage carries the same two probabilities as the first.   — Independent means the first outcome changes nothing. That is what makes this different from a without-replacement tree, where the second stage has to be recalculated.  (M1)
  3. Grows then fails: $0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$. Fails then grows: $0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.16$.   — Exactly one is two different paths, not one. Writing both down before adding is what stops the answer being halved.  (M1)
  4. $P(\text{exactly one}) = 0.16 + 0.16 = 0.32$   — Add the two branches. A check: the four paths come to 0.64 + 0.32 + 0.04 = 1.  (W1)
  FINAL: $P(\text{exactly one grows}) = 0.32$
  TWIN: With the same two seeds, calculate the probability that at least one of them grows.  → numeric 0.96 tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.005} forms=decimal/fraction

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u3.tree-diagrams-probability.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Multiplying two fractions (from GCSE Mathematics)): Before the lesson, a check on something you already know. What is $\dfrac{2}{5} \times \dfrac{3}{4}$, in its simplest form?
    ✔ $\dfrac{3}{10}$ — Yes: $\dfrac{6}{20}$, which cancels by 2. Every branch of a tree is a product like this.
    ✘ $\dfrac{5}{9}$ — The numerators and the denominators have been added separately. Multiplying fractions multiplies top by top and bottom by bottom.
    ✘ $\dfrac{8}{15}$ — The two fractions have been crossed over. Multiply straight across: $2 \times 3$ over $5 \times 4$.
- p2 (Adding fractions with different denominators (from GCSE Mathematics)): Another check on earlier work. What is $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3}$?
    ✔ $\dfrac{1}{2}$ — Yes: $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{3}{6}$. Adding branch results needs a common denominator.
    ✘ $\dfrac{2}{9}$ — The numerators and denominators have been added separately, which is not how fractions add. Rewrite both over 6 first.
    ✘ $\dfrac{1}{18}$ — That is the product, not the sum. Adding needs a common denominator.
- p3 (Tracking a total after one item is removed (from GCSE Mathematics)): One more from earlier work, before anything new. A bag holds 7 red and 3 green beads. One bead is taken and kept. How many beads are left in the bag?
    ✔ $9$ — Yes: one of the 10 has gone. Without replacement, that 9 becomes the next denominator.
    ✘ $10$ — The bead was kept, not put back, so the bag is one smaller.
    ✘ $8$ — Only one bead has been taken, so the total drops by one, not two.

### dx.fm.u3.tree-diagrams-probability.post (post) [verified]
- d1 (The second-stage denominator without replacement): A bag holds 4 lemon and 6 lime sweets. One is eaten, then another is taken. What is the probability that the second is lemon, given the first was lemon?
    ✔ $\dfrac{3}{9}$ — Yes: one lemon has gone, so 3 lemon are left among 9 sweets.
    ✘ $\dfrac{4}{9}$ [fm.prob.replacement-denominator-not-reduced] — The total dropped but the lemon count did not. The sweet eaten was lemon, so the numerator falls too.
    ✘ $\dfrac{4}{10}$ [fm.prob.replacement-denominator-not-reduced] — That is the probability for the first sweet, or for a sweet put back. One sweet has gone, so the denominator is 9.
- d2 (Counting both orders): On a tree, $P(\text{A then B}) = 0.3$ and $P(\text{B then A}) = 0.3$. What is the probability of one A and one B, in either order?
    ✔ $0.6$ — Yes: the two branches are separate ways for the same event, so they add.
    ✘ $0.3$ [fm.prob.both-orders-not-counted] — Only one order has been counted, which halves the answer. Examiners report this exact slip.
    ✘ $0.09$ [fm.prob.multiply-instead-of-add-mutually-exclusive] — The two branch results have been multiplied. Multiplying would ask for both orders at once, which cannot happen.
- d3 (Adding across branches rather than multiplying): On a two-stage tree, one path gives $P = \dfrac{1}{6}$ and another gives $P = \dfrac{1}{3}$. The event happens on either path. What is its probability?
    ✔ $\dfrac{1}{2}$ — Yes: two separate branches add. $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.
    ✘ $\dfrac{1}{18}$ [fm.prob.multiply-instead-of-add-mutually-exclusive] — Multiplying asks for both paths to happen together, and a pair of outcomes only travels one path.
    ✘ $\dfrac{1}{6}$ [fm.prob.both-orders-not-counted] — Only one of the two paths has been counted. Both satisfy the event, so both are added.
- d4 (Using the complement for 'at least one'): $P(\text{no sixes in three rolls}) = 0.579$. What is $P(\text{at least one six in three rolls})$, to 3 decimal places?
    ✔ $0.421$ — Yes: $1 - 0.579$. 'At least one' is everything except 'none', which is one line rather than seven branches.
    ✘ $0.579$ [fm.prob.complement-not-used] — That is the probability of no sixes at all, which is the opposite event. Take it from 1.
    ✘ $1.579$ [fm.prob.complement-not-used] — The complement has been added to 1 rather than subtracted from it, and a probability is never above 1.
- d5 (Telling 'along a branch' from 'across branches'): Two events happen one after the other and both must occur. What do you do with the two branch probabilities?
    ✔ Multiply them — Yes: along a branch every stage has to happen, so the probabilities multiply.
    ✘ Add them [fm.prob.add-instead-of-multiply] — Adding is for separate branches that cannot both happen. Examiners report this swap on straightforward openers.
    ✘ Subtract the smaller from the larger [fm.prob.add-instead-of-multiply] — Subtracting has no meaning here. Along a branch the rule is to multiply.
- d6 (Denominators on a three-stage without-replacement tree): Three counters are taken from a bag of 8 without replacement. What are the three denominators on the top path of the tree?
    ✔ $8$, then $7$, then $6$ — Yes: one counter leaves the bag at each draw.
    ✘ $8$, then $8$, then $8$ [fm.prob.replacement-denominator-not-reduced] — That is the with-replacement tree. Here the counters are kept, so the bag shrinks.
    ✘ $8$, then $7$, then $7$ [fm.prob.replacement-denominator-not-reduced] — The third draw follows two removals, so the denominator has dropped twice.

STEM: Conor was asked for the probability that two pens taken from a case of 11 (7 blue, 4 black), without replacement, are the same colour. His working:
    1. P(blue, blue) = 7/11 x 6/10 = 21/55
    2. P(black, black) = 4/11 x 3/10 = 6/55
  ✘ 3. P(same colour) = 21/55 x 6/55 = 126/3025
  WRONG: Lines 1 and 2 are both right, denominators and all. Line 3 multiplies them. Multiplying asks for both branches to happen at once, and a single pair of pens travels one branch only. Two separate branches add.
  FIX: P(blue, blue) = 7/11 x 6/10 = 21/55 / P(black, black) = 4/11 x 3/10 = 6/55 / P(same colour) = 21/55 + 6/55 = 27/55
  FEEDBACK: Both branch products are correct, so the method mark and the working mark for them stand. The final mark is lost at line 3. A quick sense check would have caught it: 126/3025 is far smaller than either branch on its own, and adding two chances can never make the result smaller. The answer is \dfrac{27}{55}.
