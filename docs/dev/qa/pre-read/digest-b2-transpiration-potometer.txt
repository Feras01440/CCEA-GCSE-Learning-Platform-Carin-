## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.science.b2.b2-transpiration-potometer.01 [verified] paper 7 calc
STEM: In run A the bubble started at the 10 mm mark and finished at the 42 mm mark after 20 minutes.
(a) Calculate the rate at which the shoot took up water, in mm per minute. [2]
(b) Explain why the bubble moved towards the shoot. [3]
  FIG: svg — A bubble potometer with a leafy shoot, rubber bung, reservoir and tap, and a capillary tube with a millimetre scale; the air bubble sits at the 10 mm mark.
  1. distance moved = 42 − 10 = 32 mm   — Subtract the starting reading. The bubble began at a mark, not at zero, and forgetting that is the commonest slip in this calculation.  (P1)
  2. rate = 32 ÷ 20 = 1.6 mm per minute   — A rate is an amount divided by the time it took. The unit comes straight out of the division: millimetres, divided by minutes.  (P2)  WHY: ✔Because a rate is always an amount per unit of time | ✘Because the numbers divide exactly | ✘Because the bubble moves at a steady speed
  3. Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and the vapour diffuses out through the stomata.   — Start at the leaf. Transpiration is the loss; everything else follows from it.  (P3)
  4. That water is replaced by water pulled up from the shoot's cut end.   — This is the link the 2025 reports say candidates missed. The bubble does not move on its own: it follows the water column.  (P4)
  5. So water is drawn along the capillary tube and the bubble moves towards the shoot.   — Finish on the bubble, because that is what the question asked about.  (P5)
  FINAL: (a) 1.6 mm per minute. (b) Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata; that water is replaced by water pulled up from the cut end of the shoot, so water moves along the capillary tube and the bubble follows it towards the shoot.
  TWIN: In run C the bubble moved from 10 mm to 50 mm in 20 minutes. Calculate the rate of water uptake in mm per minute.  → numeric 2 mm per minute tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.05} forms=decimal

### we.science.b2.b2-transpiration-potometer.02 [verified] paper 7 calc
STEM: Runs A and B used the same shoot for the same 20 minutes; the only difference was a fan.
(a) Calculate the percentage increase in the rate of water uptake when the fan was switched on. [3]
(b) Explain the increase. [2]
  FIG: svg — A bar chart of the rate of water uptake in four runs: still air, fan on, warm still air, and the shoot inside a sealed bag.
  1. rate in A = 32 ÷ 20 = 1.6 mm per minute; rate in B = 48 ÷ 20 = 2.4 mm per minute   — Turn both runs into rates before comparing them. Comparing raw distances only works because the times happen to match; rates always work.  (P1)
  2. increase = 2.4 − 1.6 = 0.8 mm per minute   — Find the increase first, as a plain difference.  (P2)
  3. percentage increase = 0.8 ÷ 1.6 × 100 = 50.0%   — Divide by the ORIGINAL rate, the still-air one. Dividing by the new rate is the single most common error in a percentage-change question.  (P3)
  4. Moving air blows the water vapour away from the leaf surface.   — Say what the wind physically does before you say what it does to the rate.  (P4)
  5. That keeps the air outside drier than the air spaces inside, so the vapour diffuses out faster and more water is taken up.   — Finish with the diffusion gradient. 'It dries the leaf' is not enough for the second mark.  (P5)
  FINAL: (a) 50.0%. (b) The fan blows water vapour away from the leaf surface, so the air outside stays drier than the air spaces inside; the difference in water concentration is larger, the vapour diffuses out faster, and more water is taken up.
  TWIN: Runs A and C differ only in temperature. Calculate the percentage increase in the rate of water uptake from run A to run C.  → numeric 25 % tol={"type":"absolute","value":0.05} forms=decimal/percent

### we.science.b2.b2-transpiration-potometer.03 [verified] paper B2 calc
STEM: Define transpiration, and explain why it slows down when the air around the leaf becomes humid. [5]
  FIG: svg — A leaf in section: water travels up the xylem, evaporates from the mesophyll cells, diffuses through the air spaces and leaves through a stoma.
  1. Transpiration is the evaporation of water from the mesophyll cells…   — The definition has three stages and CCEA wants all of them. Stage one is evaporation, inside the leaf, from the cell surfaces.  (P1)
  2. …followed by diffusion of the water vapour through the air spaces…   — Stage two. 'Air spaces' is a separate mark from 'stomata', and the 2025 reports say it is the one candidates leave out.  (P2)
  3. …and out through the stomata.   — Stage three, the exit. Note the direction: water leaves here. It never enters the leaf this way.  (P3)
  4. Humid air already holds a lot of water vapour.   — Describe the outside air first.  (P4)
  5. So the difference in water vapour concentration between the air spaces and the air outside is smaller, and the vapour diffuses out more slowly.   — The mark is for the smaller difference, not for the word humid. Every factor in this topic works through that same difference.  (P5)
  FINAL: Transpiration is the evaporation of water from the mesophyll cells, followed by diffusion of the water vapour through the air spaces and out through the stomata. In humid air there is already a lot of water vapour outside, so the difference in water vapour concentration between the air spaces and the air outside is smaller and the vapour diffuses out more slowly.
  TWIN: State the two structures inside a leaf that water vapour passes through on its way out.  → text accepted=["the air spaces and the stomata"] keyWords=[{"any":["air spaces","airspaces","intercellular spaces"],"marks":1},{"any":["stomata","stoma","stomates"],"marks":1}]

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.b2.b2-transpiration-potometer.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Recall leaf structure (B1)): Before we start, three things you have already met. Which layer of a leaf carries out most gas exchange?
    ✔ the spongy mesophyll — Its loose cells and large air spaces are the route in and out — and the same air spaces carry water vapour away.
    ✘ the palisade mesophyll [sci.leaf.gas-exchange-layer-unknown] — The palisade layer is packed tight for light absorption. Gas exchange needs the spaces of the spongy layer.
    ✘ the waxy cuticle [sci.leaf.gas-exchange-layer-unknown] — The cuticle is waterproof, which is the opposite of a gas-exchange surface.
    ✘ the upper epidermis [sci.leaf.gas-exchange-layer-unknown] — A protective transparent layer with almost no stomata in it.
- p2 (Recall the stomata (B1)): What are the pores in the lower surface of a leaf called?
    ✔ stomata — Stomata — the pores themselves. You will need this word in almost every answer in this topic.
    ✘ guard cells [sci.leaf.guard-cells-for-stomata] — The guard cells open and close the pore. The pore is the stoma.
    ✘ air spaces [sci.transpiration.exit-route-vague] — The air spaces are inside the leaf, between the spongy cells. The pores in the surface are the stomata.
    ✘ xylem vessels [sci.transpiration.exit-route-vague] — Xylem vessels are the tubes that bring water up. They do not open onto the leaf surface.
- p3 (Recall osmosis (previous topic)): Water moves by osmosis from…
    ✔ a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution — Water goes where there is less of it. That is what pulls water out of the soil and into a root.
    ✘ a concentrated solution to a more dilute solution [sci.osmosis.direction-reversed] — That is the way the sugar would go if it could fit through. Water moves the other way.
    ✘ wherever the plant needs it most [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — Osmosis has no plan: it is diffusion, and it only ever runs down a difference in concentration.
    ✘ the air into the leaf through the stomata [sci.transpiration.water-enters-through-stomata] — Water leaves a leaf through the stomata as vapour. It enters the plant at the roots.

### dx.science.b2.b2-transpiration-potometer (post) [verified]
- 01 (Name the exit route): Water vapour leaves a leaf through…
    ✔ the air spaces, then the stomata — Both structures, in that order. The scheme gives a mark for each, and the air spaces are the one most candidates leave out.
    ✘ the stomata only [sci.transpiration.exit-route-vague] — Half the answer. The vapour has to cross the air spaces inside the leaf first, and that is a mark of its own.
    ✘ the xylem vessels [sci.transpiration.exit-route-vague] — The xylem brings water up to the leaf; it does not carry vapour out of it.
    ✘ the waxy cuticle [sci.transpiration.exit-route-vague] — The cuticle is waterproof, which is exactly what stops water escaping through the upper surface.
- 02 (State the direction of a factor's effect): As light intensity increases, the rate of transpiration…
    ✔ increases, because the stomata open wider — Light opens the stomata for photosynthesis, and open stomata let more water vapour out.
    ✘ decreases, because the stomata close [sci.transpiration.factor-trend-reversed] — The stomata close in the dark, not in the light. Summer 2025 examiners reported candidates giving this relationship the wrong way round.
    ✘ stays the same, because light is not a factor [sci.transpiration.factor-trend-reversed] — Light intensity is one of the five factors named in the specification.
    ✘ increases, because the plant makes more water in photosynthesis [sci.transpiration.bubble-confused-with-oxygen] — Photosynthesis does make water, but the amount is tiny and irrelevant here. The link is through the stomata opening.
- 03 (Explain the movement of the bubble): Which way does the air bubble in a bubble potometer move, and why?
    ✔ Towards the shoot, because water is taken up to replace what is lost by transpiration — The bubble follows the water column. That is all it is measuring.
    ✘ Away from the shoot, because water is pushed out of the leaves [sci.transpiration.water-enters-through-stomata] — Water leaves the leaf as vapour through the stomata, so water is drawn IN at the cut end and the bubble follows it towards the shoot.
    ✘ Away from the shoot, because oxygen from photosynthesis pushes it along [sci.transpiration.bubble-confused-with-oxygen] — The tube is full of water, not gas from the leaf. Summer 2025 examiners saw exactly this answer and it earned nothing.
    ✘ Towards the shoot, because water enters the leaf through the stomata [sci.transpiration.water-enters-through-stomata] — The direction is right but the reason is not: water leaves through the stomata as vapour. It enters at the cut end of the stem.
- 04 (Calculate a rate from two readings): In run A the bubble moved from 10 mm to 42 mm in 20 minutes. What was the rate of water uptake?
    ✔ 1.6 mm per minute — Distance 32 mm divided by 20 minutes.
    ✘ 2.10 mm per minute [sci.rates.start-value-not-subtracted] — The final reading has been divided by the time without subtracting the start. The bubble began at a mark, not at zero.
    ✘ 32 mm per minute [sci.rates.time-not-divided] — That is the distance the bubble moved, not a rate. A rate needs dividing by the time.
    ✘ 0.625 mm per minute [sci.rates.rate-inverted] — Time has been divided by distance. Rate is the amount first, the time second: distance ÷ time.
- 05 (Recall the technique): How do you reset the air bubble to the start of the scale between runs?
    ✔ Open the tap on the reservoir so water pushes the bubble back — The reservoir is there for exactly this. Some set-ups use a syringe instead of a tap, and pushing the plunger does the same job.
    ✘ Blow down the capillary tube [sci.transpiration.potometer-reset-unknown] — That would add air and break the water column. Use the reservoir tap or the syringe.
    ✘ Lift the shoot out and put it back [sci.transpiration.potometer-reset-unknown] — Breaking the seal lets air in, and the airtight seal is what makes the measurement mean anything.
    ✘ Wait until it drifts back on its own [sci.transpiration.potometer-reset-unknown] — It will not: the shoot goes on taking water up, so the bubble only travels one way.
- 06 (Pick the factor that lowers the rate): Which change would make the rate of transpiration fall?
    ✔ Sealing the shoot inside a clear plastic bag — The bag traps water vapour, so the air around the leaves becomes humid and the vapour diffuses out more slowly.
    ✘ Switching on a fan [sci.transpiration.factor-trend-reversed] — Moving air carries vapour away, keeping the outside air drier, so the rate rises.
    ✘ Moving the plant to a warmer room [sci.transpiration.factor-trend-reversed] — Warmth gives the water molecules more energy, so evaporation and diffusion are faster and the rate rises.
    ✘ Adding two more leaves to the shoot [sci.transpiration.factor-trend-reversed] — More leaf surface area means more stomata, so the rate rises.
- 07 (Recall the uses of water): Which of these is NOT one of the uses of water the specification lists for a plant?
    ✔ Breaking down glucose to release energy — That is respiration, and it uses oxygen, not water. The four listed uses are support, transport, transpiration and photosynthesis.
    ✘ Keeping the cells turgid so the plant is supported [sci.transpiration.uses-of-water-incomplete] — Support is one of the four uses: turgid cells hold a soft plant upright.
    ✘ Carrying dissolved minerals around the plant [sci.transpiration.uses-of-water-incomplete] — Transport is one of the four uses.
    ✘ Supplying a raw material for photosynthesis [sci.transpiration.uses-of-water-incomplete] — Photosynthesis is one of the four uses: water is one of the two reactants.
