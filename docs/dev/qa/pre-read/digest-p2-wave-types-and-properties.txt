### we.science.p2.p2-wave-types-and-properties.01 [verified] paper P2 calc
STEM: A loudspeaker produces a note of frequency 440 Hz. In the air of the room the wavelength of the note is 75 cm.
Calculate the speed of sound in the air of the room. [4]
  1. v = f λ   — Write the equation before a single number goes down. P2 papers print no equations, and the equation line is normally a mark of its own.  (P1)
  2. λ = 75 ÷ 100 = 0.75 m   — Convert to the unit the equation expects. Metres per second comes out only if the wavelength goes in as metres.  (P2)  WHY: ✔Because v = f λ gives m/s only when λ is in metres | ✘Because centimetres are too small to use in physics | ✘Because the frequency is already in hertz
  3. v = 440 × 0.75   — Substitute, keeping the equation's own order so the marker can follow it.  (P3)
  4. v = 330 m/s   — Write the unit on the answer line: it carries its own mark. A check: sound in air is about 330 m/s, so the answer is believable.  (P4)
  FINAL: v = 330 m/s
  TWIN: A wave on a stretched spring has a frequency of 8 Hz and a wavelength of 40 cm.
Calculate the speed of the wave in m/s. [4]  → numeric 3.2 m/s tol={"type":"exact"} forms=decimal

### we.science.p2.p2-wave-types-and-properties.02 [verified] paper P2 calc
STEM: A crest of a sea wave passes a harbour post every 5 s. The distance between one crest and the next is 12 m.
Calculate the speed of the wave. [5]
  1. time for one complete wave, T = 5 s   — Name what you have been given. The time for one whole wave to pass a point is the period, not the frequency.  (P1)
  2. f = 1 ÷ T   — The wave equation needs a frequency, so convert the period into one. Write that equation down too.  (P2)  WHY: ✔Because 5 s is a period, and v = f λ needs a frequency in hertz | ✘Because 5 s can be written as 5 Hz | ✘Because every wave question starts with f = 1 ÷ T
  3. f = 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2 Hz   — Do the division. The answer is below 1 Hz, exactly as expected for a wave that takes several seconds.  (P3)
  4. v = f λ   — Now the wave equation, written out before the numbers.  (P4)
  5. v = 0.2 × 12 = 2.4 m/s   — Substitute and finish with the unit. A sea wave at a few metres per second is believable.  (P5)
  FINAL: v = 2.4 m/s
  TWIN: One complete wave takes 0.25 s to pass a point.
Calculate the frequency of the wave. [2]  → numeric 4 Hz tol={"type":"exact"} forms=decimal

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.p2.p2-wave-types-and-properties.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Prerequisite: speed, distance and time): Before we start, from P1: which equation gives an average speed?
    ✔ speed = distance ÷ time — That one. The wave equation you are about to meet is a cousin of it.
    ✘ speed = distance × time — Multiplying gives a bigger number the longer you travel, which cannot be a speed. It is a division.
    ✘ speed = time ÷ distance — That is upside down. Distance goes on top.
- p2 (Prerequisite: reading a graph axis): A graph has time along the bottom and displacement up the side. Which axis do you read a time from?
    ✔ the axis along the bottom — Along the bottom. Two wave graphs in this topic differ only in what that axis shows.
    ✘ the axis up the side — That one shows the displacement. Times are read along the bottom.
- p3 (Prerequisite: minutes and seconds): How many seconds are there in one minute?
    ✔ 60 — 60. A frequency in hertz counts waves per second, so a rate given per minute has to be divided by 60.
    ✘ 100 — Time is not decimal: there are 60 seconds in a minute.
    ✘ 24 — 24 is hours in a day. A minute is 60 seconds.

### dx.science.p2.p2-wave-types-and-properties.post (post) [verified]
- 01 (Particle motion in a transverse wave): In a transverse wave, how do the particles move?
    ✔ they vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave travels — That is the wording the scheme wants: vibrate, at right angles, to the direction of travel.
    ✘ they travel at right angles to the direction the wave travels [sci.waves.particles-move-not-vibrate] — The angle is right but the verb is not. Examiners look for vibrate or oscillate; travel suggests the particles go somewhere and they do not.
    ✘ they vibrate along the direction the wave travels [sci.waves.sound-called-transverse] — That is a longitudinal wave. In a transverse wave the vibration is across the direction of travel.
    ✘ they are carried along with the wave [sci.waves.energy-not-matter] — A wave transfers energy, not matter. Each particle stays in the same place and vibrates about it.
- 02 (Sorting examples into the two families): Which of these is a longitudinal wave?
    ✔ ultrasound — Ultrasound is sound at a frequency above 20 000 Hz, and sound is longitudinal.
    ✘ ultraviolet [sci.em.ultrasound-for-ultraviolet] — Ultraviolet is electromagnetic, so it is transverse. Examiners have reported these two words being swapped in both directions.
    ✘ a water wave [sci.waves.sound-called-transverse] — Water waves are transverse: the water bobs up and down across the direction the wave travels.
    ✘ a microwave [sci.waves.sound-called-transverse] — Microwaves are electromagnetic, so they are transverse. Only sound and ultrasound are longitudinal on this specification.
- 03 (Amplitude from a graph): A wave graph shows crests 6 cm above the middle line and troughs 6 cm below it. What is the amplitude?
    ✔ 6 cm — Amplitude is the maximum displacement from the rest position, so it is the height of one crest.
    ✘ 12 cm [sci.waves.amplitude-peak-to-peak] — That is the whole distance from trough to crest. Halve it. Summer 2024 examiners named this slip on a P2 Higher graph.
    ✘ 3 cm [sci.waves.amplitude-peak-to-peak] — That halves the amplitude instead of halving the peak-to-peak height. Read straight from the middle line to a crest.
    ✘ you cannot tell without the wavelength [sci.waves.amplitude-peak-to-peak] — Amplitude and wavelength are independent: one is read up the displacement axis, the other along the horizontal axis.
- 04 (Frequency from a period): One complete wave passes a post every 4 s. What is the frequency?
    ✔ 0.25 Hz — f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 4. Fewer than one wave a second gives a frequency below 1 Hz.
    ✘ 4 Hz [sci.waves.period-vs-frequency] — That is the period with a different unit stuck on it. Summer 2025 examiners recorded a period of two seconds written as two hertz.
    ✘ 0.5 Hz [sci.waves.wavelength-half-read] — That uses half a cycle as the whole period. One complete wave takes 4 s, so f = 1 ÷ 4.
    ✘ 0.025 Hz [sci.waves.prefix-not-converted] — A power of ten has slipped. 1 ÷ 4 is 0.25, and no conversion is needed because the period is already in seconds.
- 05 (The wave equation with a conversion): A wave has a frequency of 5 Hz and a wavelength of 40 cm. What is its speed in m/s?
    ✔ 2 m/s — 40 cm is 0.4 m, and 5 × 0.4 = 2 m/s. The conversion comes before the substitution.
    ✘ 200 m/s [sci.waves.prefix-not-converted] — The centimetres were left as centimetres. 5 × 40 gives 200 cm/s, which is 2 m/s.
    ✘ 0.08 m/s [sci.waves.equation-rearranged-wrongly] — That divides the wavelength by the frequency. In v = f λ the two are multiplied.
    ✘ 12.5 m/s [sci.waves.equation-rearranged-wrongly] — That divides the frequency by the wavelength. Multiply instead: v = f λ.
- 06 (Telling the two wave graphs apart): Which quantity can be read from a displacement-time graph but not from a displacement-distance graph?
    ✔ the period — A period is a time, so only the graph with time along the bottom can show it.
    ✘ the amplitude [sci.waves.wavelength-half-read] — Both graphs show the amplitude, because both have displacement up the side. Summer 2023 examiners reported candidates unaware of this.
    ✘ the wavelength [sci.waves.wavelength-half-read] — The other way round: a wavelength is a distance, so it is read from the displacement-distance graph.
    ✘ the speed [sci.waves.equation-rearranged-wrongly] — Neither graph shows the speed directly. You calculate it from v = f λ once you have two of the quantities.

### ftm.science.p2.p2-wave-types-and-properties.01 [verified] sci.waves.prefix-not-converted · earned P1 · ccea-cer:science:2025-summer:P2H:Q3

  ✘ 2. v = 15 × 20

  FEEDBACK: One mark of four: the equation line stands, because it is correct physics. What goes is the conversion mark and the two that depend on it. A wave on a bench travelling at 300 m/s would be faster than sound, which is the sanity check worth making before you write the answer down.
