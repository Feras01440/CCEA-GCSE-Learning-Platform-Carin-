### we.fm.u2.equilibrium-of-forces.01 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A block of mass $6$ kg rests on a rough horizontal table. A rope pulls the block with a force of $24$ N at $50°$ to the table, and the block does not move.

(i) Calculate the normal reaction of the table on the block.
(ii) Calculate the force due to friction. Round to 2 decimal places.
  FIG: svg — A block of mass 6 kg on a rough table. R points straight up, 60 N straight down, a 24 N pull acts up and to the right at 50 degrees to the table, and F acts to the left along the table.
  1. Weight $= 6 \times 10 = 60$ N, acting vertically downwards.   — The mass has to become a force before it can go into an equation, and g = 10 on this paper.  (MW1)
  2. Resolving vertically: $R + 24\sin 50° = 60$.   — Name the direction in writing, then list every force with a part in it. Three forces have a vertical part here: the reaction, the rope's lift and the weight.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔Because a missing term makes the equation wrong even when the resolving is right | ✘Because the rope is the only force that needs resolving | ✘Because the reaction always equals the weight
  3. $R = 60 - 24\sin 50° = 41.61$ N.   — The rope lifts part of the weight, so the table supports less than the whole of it.  (MW1,W1)
  4. Resolving horizontally: $F = 24\cos 50° = 15.43$ N.   — The block does not move, so the horizontal forces balance too: friction matches the rope's pull along the table.  (MW1,W1)
  FINAL: (i) $41.61$ N  (ii) $15.43$ N
  TWIN: A block of weight $80$ N rests on a table while a rope pulls it with a force of $50$ N at $30°$ to the table. Calculate the normal reaction. Round to 2 decimal places.

### we.fm.u2.equilibrium-of-forces.02 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A box of mass $14$ kg is held at rest on a smooth slope inclined at $27°$ to the horizontal by a rope lying along the slope.

(i) Calculate the tension in the rope.
(ii) Calculate the normal reaction of the slope on the box. Round to 2 decimal places.
  FIG: svg — A box of mass 14 kg held at rest on a smooth slope inclined at 27 degrees. R leaves the box at right angles to the slope, 140 N points straight down, and T points up the slope along a rope.
  1. Weight $= 14 \times 10 = 140$ N, vertically downwards.   — Start by turning the mass into a force, as every equation that follows needs newtons.  (MW1)
  2. Resolving along the slope: $T = 140\sin 27° = 63.56$ N.   — Along the slope and at right angles to it is the pair worth choosing, because the tension and the reaction already lie along them and only the weight has to be split.  (MW1,W1)
  3. Resolving at right angles to the slope: $R = 140\cos 27° = 124.74$ N.   — The tension has no part at right angles to the slope, so this equation has just two terms in it.  (MW1,W1)  WHY: ✔Because the rope lies along the slope, so it is entirely in the other direction | ✘Because the tension is too small to matter here | ✘Because the reaction always balances the tension
  4. Check: $124.74$ N is less than the weight of $140$ N, as it must be on a slope.   — A reaction larger than the weight would mean the cosine of the angle was greater than one, so this check catches a swapped ratio.  (W1)
  FINAL: (i) $63.56$ N  (ii) $124.74$ N

### we.fm.u2.equilibrium-of-forces.03 [verified] paper FM2 calc
STEM: A particle at $O$ is held in equilibrium by three forces: $20$ N acting horizontally to the right, $15$ N acting vertically downwards, and a force of $P$ N acting at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, to the left.

(i) Calculate the value of $P$.
(ii) Calculate the value of $\theta$. Round to 2 decimal places.
  FIG: svg — A particle at O held in equilibrium by three forces: 20 N horizontally to the right, 15 N vertically downwards, and a force of P newtons acting up and to the left at an angle to the horizontal that has to be found.
  1. Resolving horizontally: $P\cos\theta = 20$.   — The third force is the only one with an unknown direction, so write its two components as $P\cos\theta$ and $P\sin\theta$ and let the equations find them.  (MW1)
  2. Resolving vertically: $P\sin\theta = 15$.   — The second equation comes from the other direction. Two equations, two unknowns.  (MW1)
  3. Squaring and adding: $P^2 = 20^2 + 15^2 = 625$, so $P = 25$ N.   — Squaring and adding removes the angle, because $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. It is Pythagoras wearing a different hat.  (MW1,W1)
  4. Dividing: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{15}{20}$, so $\theta = 36.87°$.   — Dividing the two equations removes $P$ instead, leaving the angle on its own.  (MW1,W1)
  FINAL: (i) $P = 25$ N  (ii) $\theta = 36.87°$
  TWIN: A particle is held in equilibrium by a force of $12$ N to the right, a force of $5$ N upwards and a third force. Calculate the magnitude of the third force.

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u2.equilibrium-of-forces.pre (pre) [verified]
- 01 (Prerequisite: weight from mass): From an earlier topic: taking $g = 10$ m/s², what is the weight of a body of mass $20$ kg?
    ✔ $200$ N — $20 \times 10 = 200$ N.
    ✘ $20$ N — That is the mass with newtons written after it.
    ✘ $2$ N — That divides by $g$ instead of multiplying.
    ✘ $196$ N — That uses $g = 9.8$, which this unit does not.
- 02 (Prerequisite: resolving along a line): From an earlier topic: a force $F$ acts at an angle $\theta$ to a line. What is its component along that line?
    ✔ $F\cos\theta$ — The line is beside the angle, so it takes the cosine.
    ✘ $F\sin\theta$ — That is the component at right angles to the line.
    ✘ $F\tan\theta$ — Tangent compares the two components with each other.
    ✘ $F$ — The whole force only lies along the line when the angle is zero.
- 03 (Prerequisite: the weight on a slope): From an earlier topic: a body of weight $W$ rests on a slope inclined at $\alpha$. What is the component of $W$ acting down the slope?
    ✔ $W\sin\alpha$ — Down the slope is opposite the slope's angle, so it takes the sine.
    ✘ $W\cos\alpha$ — That is the component pressing into the slope.
    ✘ $W$ — The whole weight acts down the slope only when the slope is vertical.
    ✘ $W\tan\alpha$ — That can be larger than the weight itself, which no component ever is.
- 04 (Prerequisite: solving a linear equation): From an earlier topic: solve $R + 18 = 90$.
    ✔ $R = 72$ — Taking 18 from both sides leaves $R = 72$.
    ✘ $R = 108$ — That adds 18 instead of subtracting it.
    ✘ $R = 90$ — That ignores the 18 altogether.
    ✘ $R = 5$ — That divides rather than subtracts.

### dx.fm.u2.equilibrium-of-forces.post (post) [verified]
- 01 (What equilibrium means): A particle is in equilibrium under four forces. What is the resultant of the four forces?
    ✔ zero — Nothing is left over, which is exactly why the particle does not accelerate.
    ✘ the largest of the four [fm.forces.term-missing-from-equation] — Every force takes part in the balance, so none of them survives on its own.
    ✘ the total of the four sizes [fm.forces.components-added-as-scalars] — That totals the sizes and ignores the directions, which is what makes them cancel.
    ✘ it cannot be found without the mass [fm.forces.equilibrium-assumed-when-accelerating] — Equilibrium fixes the resultant at zero whatever the mass is.
- 02 (Constant velocity is equilibrium): A crate is dragged across a floor at a constant velocity of $3$ m/s. What is true of the forces on it?
    ✔ they balance, because the acceleration is zero — Constant velocity means no acceleration, so $F = ma$ gives a resultant of zero.
    ✘ they do not balance, because the crate is moving [fm.forces.equilibrium-assumed-when-accelerating] — Motion at a steady speed needs nothing left over; it is changing speed that does.
    ✘ the driving force must be larger than the friction [fm.forces.equilibrium-assumed-when-accelerating] — A larger driving force would speed the crate up, so the velocity would not be constant.
    ✘ there is not enough information to say [fm.forces.equation-not-formed] — Constant velocity is all the information needed: it sets the resultant to zero.
- 03 (The reaction when a rope lifts): A block of weight $70$ N rests on a table while a rope pulls it with a vertical component of $20$ N upwards. Which equation gives the normal reaction?
    ✔ $R + 20 = 70$ — The table and the rope together hold the weight up, so their totals match it.
    ✘ $R = 70$ [fm.forces.reaction-equals-weight-unresolved] — That leaves the rope out. The reports name this two-term shortcut as very common.
    ✘ $R = 20$ [fm.forces.term-missing-from-equation] — That leaves the weight out and keeps only the rope.
    ✘ $R = 70 + 20$ [fm.forces.opposing-component-sign-dropped] — The rope pulls upwards, the same way as the reaction, so it takes work away from the table rather than adding to it.
- 04 (The reaction on a slope): A box of weight $150$ N rests on a smooth slope inclined at $30°$ to the horizontal. What is the normal reaction?
    ✔ $150\cos 30°$ — Only the part of the weight pressing into the surface has to be balanced.
    ✘ $150$ N [fm.forces.reaction-equals-weight-unresolved] — The reaction equals the weight only on a horizontal surface, where nothing else is vertical.
    ✘ $150\sin 30°$ [fm.forces.sin-cos-swapped] — That is the component down the slope, which friction or a rope has to balance instead.
    ✘ $150\tan 30°$ [fm.forces.sin-cos-swapped] — Tangent compares the two components and cannot give either from the weight.
- 05 (A component is not an equation): A candidate resolving a force in an equilibrium question writes $25\sin 40°$ and stops. How is that marked?
    ✔ a method mark for one correct resolved component, and no more — The schemes allow the first mark for a correct component; the marks after it are for the equation it belongs in.
    ✘ full marks, because the resolving is right [fm.forces.equation-not-formed] — The question asks for a value, and a value needs the complete equation.
    ✘ no marks at all [fm.forces.equation-not-formed] — A correct component is worth something, which is why it is always worth writing down.
    ✘ a method mark only if the answer that follows is right [fm.forces.equation-not-formed] — A method mark stands on its own, whatever comes after it.
- 06 (Two-term equations): Three forces have vertical parts: a reaction $R$ upwards, a pull of $17\sin 33°$ upwards and a weight of $58$ N downwards. Which vertical equation is complete?
    ✔ $R + 17\sin 33° = 58$ — All three vertical parts appear, and the two upward ones together match the weight.
    ✘ $R = 17\sin 33°$ [fm.forces.term-missing-from-equation] — The weight has dropped out. This exact two-term shape is the one the reports single out.
    ✘ $R = 58$ [fm.forces.reaction-equals-weight-unresolved] — The pull has dropped out, so the table is being asked to hold the whole weight.
    ✘ $R = 58 + 17\sin 33°$ [fm.forces.opposing-component-sign-dropped] — The pull acts upwards, so it helps the reaction rather than adding to what it must support.
- 07 (Friction on a rough slope): A crate of weight $90$ N rests on a rough slope inclined at $25°$, with nothing attached to it. What is the force due to friction?
    ✔ $90\sin 25°$, acting up the slope — Friction is the only force along the slope, so it matches the component of the weight pulling the crate down it.
    ✘ $90\sin 25°$, acting down the slope [fm.forces.friction-direction] — The size is right, but friction opposes the way the crate would slide, which is downwards.
    ✘ $90\cos 25°$, acting up the slope [fm.forces.sin-cos-swapped] — That is the component pressing into the slope, which the normal reaction balances.
    ✘ $90$ N, acting up the slope [fm.forces.force-left-unresolved] — That is the whole weight. Only the part along the slope has to be held.
- 08 (Choosing the two directions on a slope): Which pair of directions makes an equilibrium problem on a slope shortest to solve?
    ✔ along the slope and at right angles to it — The reaction and any rope along the slope then need no resolving, leaving only the weight to split.
    ✘ horizontal and vertical [fm.forces.angle-from-wrong-line] — That works, but then both the reaction and the tension have to be resolved instead of one force.
    ✘ along the slope and vertical [fm.forces.angle-from-wrong-line] — Those two are not at right angles, so the two equations would not be independent.
    ✘ any two directions at all [fm.forces.angle-from-wrong-line] — They have to be perpendicular, or one equation repeats part of the other.

### ftm.fm.u2.equilibrium-of-forces.01 [verified] fm.forces.term-missing-from-equation · earned M1 · ccea-cer:further-maths:2019-summer:FM2:Q3
STEM: Eoin is finding the normal reaction on a block of weight $58$ N that is held still on a table by a rope pulling at $33°$ to the table with a force of $17$ N. His working:
    1. the block does not move, so the vertical forces balance
  ✘ 2. resolving vertically: $R = 17\sin 33°$
    3. $R = 9.26$ N
  WRONG: Line 2 has only two terms where three belong. Three forces have a vertical part: the reaction upwards, the rope's $17\sin 33°$ upwards, and the weight of $58$ N downwards. Leaving the weight out makes the equation say the rope alone is being balanced.
  FIX: the block does not move, so the vertical forces balance / resolving vertically: $R + 17\sin 33° = 58$ / $R = 58 - 9.26 = 48.74$ N
  FEEDBACK: Naming the direction and saying the forces balance is the method, and it earns the method mark (M). Before writing an equation, list every force that has a part in that direction: three here, so three terms. The reports call the two-term equation the commonest shape of all in this question.
