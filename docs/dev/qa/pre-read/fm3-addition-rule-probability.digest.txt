### we.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.01 [verified] paper FM3 calc
STEM: A leisure centre has 50 members. 31 of them swim, 24 use the gym and 12 do both.
One member is chosen at random. Calculate the probability that this member swims or uses the gym.
  FIG: svg — A two-circle Venn diagram for 50 members showing 19 who swim only, 12 who do both, 12 who use the gym only and 7 outside both circles.
  1. $S$ is 'swims', $G$ is 'uses the gym'. $P(S) = \dfrac{31}{50}$, $P(G) = \dfrac{24}{50}$, $P(S \cap G) = \dfrac{12}{50}$.   — Name the events and write all three probabilities over the same total. The rule on the sheet is written in letters, so the letters have to mean something before it can be used.  (M1)
  2. $P(S \cup G) = \dfrac{31}{50} + \dfrac{24}{50} - \dfrac{12}{50}$   — Substitute into $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. The last term is there because the 12 members who do both were counted in each of the first two.  (MW1)  WHY: ✔Subtract $\dfrac{12}{50}$ once, because those members were counted twice | ✘Subtract $\dfrac{12}{50}$ twice, because they appear in two circles | ✘Add $\dfrac{12}{50}$, because they belong to the group as well
  3. $= \dfrac{31 + 24 - 12}{50} = \dfrac{43}{50}$   — The denominators already match, so only the numerators need combining. Keep the unreduced fraction until the end; the scheme accepts it either way.  (W1)
  4. $P(S \cup G) = \dfrac{43}{50} = 0.86$   — Simplify or convert, and check the answer is between 0 and 1. 0.86 is under 1, which the un-subtracted version 1.10 would not have been.  (W1)
  FINAL: $P(S \cup G) = \dfrac{43}{50}$, which is 0.86

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.pre (pre) [verified]
- p1 (Probability from equally likely outcomes (from GCSE Mathematics)): Before the lesson, a check on something you already know. A bag holds 5 red and 3 green beads. What is the probability that one bead taken at random is red?
    ✔ $\dfrac{5}{8}$ — Yes: 5 red out of 8 beads altogether. Every probability in this topic is built from a count like this.
    ✘ $\dfrac{5}{3}$ — That compares red with green rather than with the whole bag. The denominator is always the total number of outcomes, which is 8.
    ✘ $\dfrac{3}{8}$ — That is the probability of green. The question asked for red, so the numerator is 5.
- p2 (The complement of an event (from GCSE Mathematics)): Another check on earlier work. If $P(A) = 0.35$, what is the probability that $A$ does not happen?
    ✔ $0.65$ — Yes: the two possibilities total 1, so the complement is $1 - 0.35$. You will use this to turn 'at least one' into 'neither'.
    ✘ $-0.35$ — A probability is never negative. Take 0.35 away from 1 instead of from 0.
    ✘ $0.35$ — That is the probability that $A$ does happen. The complement is what is left of the whole: $1 - 0.35$.
- p3 (Writing a count as a fraction of a total and simplifying (from GCSE Mathematics)): One more from earlier work, before anything new. In a group of 30 people, 12 wear glasses. What fraction of the group wear glasses, in its simplest form?
    ✔ $\dfrac{2}{5}$ — Yes: $\dfrac{12}{30}$ cancels by 6. Either form earns the mark in the exam, and the unsimplified one is often easier to keep working with.
    ✘ $\dfrac{12}{18}$ — 18 is the number who do not wear glasses. The denominator of a probability is the whole group, which is 30.
    ✘ $\dfrac{5}{2}$ — That is the fraction upside down, and it is larger than 1. A probability sits between 0 and 1.

### dx.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.post (post) [verified]
- d1 (Counting a union from two totals and an overlap): In a class of 25, 14 play a team sport, 11 play an instrument and 6 do both. How many play at least one of the two?
    ✔ $19$ — Yes: $14 + 11 - 6 = 19$. The 6 in both were counted in each total, so they come off once.
    ✘ $25$ [fm.prob.overlap-not-subtracted] — That is $14 + 11$, with the 6 who do both counted twice. Taking them off once leaves 19.
    ✘ $31$ [fm.prob.overlap-not-subtracted] — The 6 have been added rather than subtracted, so they are now counted three times. The rule subtracts the overlap: 19.
- d2 (The addition rule when the overlap is empty): $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.3$ and the two events are mutually exclusive. What is $P(A \cup B)$?
    ✔ $0.9$ — Yes. Mutually exclusive means $P(A \cap B) = 0$, so the rule's last term is zero and the two probabilities simply add.
    ✘ $0.18$ [fm.prob.or-multiplied-instead-of-added] — That is $0.6 \times 0.3$, which answers 'both'. Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen, so multiplying is never the route here.
    ✘ $0.3$ [fm.prob.overlap-not-subtracted] — $P(B)$ has been subtracted as if it were the overlap. When the events are mutually exclusive there is no overlap to take off, so the answer is $0.6 + 0.3$.
- d3 (Recognising when two events can happen together): A card is drawn from an ordinary pack. Which pair of events is NOT mutually exclusive?
    ✔ The card is a heart; the card is a queen — Yes: the queen of hearts is both, so the overlap is not empty and the addition rule needs its last term.
    ✘ The card is a heart; the card is a spade [fm.prob.overlap-not-subtracted] — No card is in two suits at once, so these two cannot happen together and the overlap is empty.
    ✘ The card is a king; the card is a seven [fm.prob.overlap-not-subtracted] — A card carries one value, so it cannot be a king and a seven. That overlap is empty too.
- d4 (Telling an 'and' question from an 'or' question): A coin is tossed and a dice is rolled. Which calculation gives the probability of a head AND a six?
    ✔ $\dfrac{1}{2} \times \dfrac{1}{6}$ — Yes: the two are independent, so 'and' multiplies. That gives $\dfrac{1}{12}$.
    ✘ $\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{6}$ [fm.prob.add-instead-of-multiply] — Adding answers 'a head or a six'. Examiners report this exact swap every year: decide 'and' or 'or' in writing before calculating.
    ✘ $\dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{6} - \dfrac{1}{12}$ [fm.prob.add-instead-of-multiply] — That is the addition rule, which gives 'a head or a six'. For both together the two probabilities multiply.

## FIND THE MISTAKE
### ftm.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.01 [verified] fm.prob.overlap-not-subtracted · earned M1 · ccea-cer:further-maths:2019-summer:FM3:Q7
STEM: Niamh was asked for the probability that a student chosen from a form group of 40 brings a packed lunch or has a bus pass. 22 bring a packed lunch, 15 have a bus pass and 8 do both. Her working:
    1. P(packed lunch) = 22/40, P(bus pass) = 15/40
  ✘ 2. P(packed or bus) = 22/40 + 15/40 = 37/40
    3. = 0.925
  WRONG: Line 2 adds the two probabilities and stops. The 8 students who do both are inside each total, so they have been counted twice. The rule on the formula sheet has a third term for exactly this: subtract $P(\text{both}) = 8/40$ once.
  FIX: P(packed lunch) = 22/40, P(bus pass) = 15/40, P(both) = 8/40 / P(packed or bus) = 22/40 + 15/40 - 8/40 = 29/40 / = 29/40 = 0.725
  FEEDBACK: The two probabilities are right, so the method mark for identifying them is safe. The working mark goes at line 2, where the overlap stays counted twice. A quick check would have caught it: 0.925 is close to 1 for a group where 11 students do neither. The answer is \dfrac{29}{40}.

## RETRIEVAL PROMPTS
- rp.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.01 [verified] (formula) The addition rule for two events, as the Unit 3 formula sheet prints it.  →  $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
- rp.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.02 [verified] (definition) What does 'or' mean in a probability question, and what does it include?  →  At least one of the two events happens, and that includes the case where both happen.
- rp.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.03 [verified] (trap) Why does the addition rule subtract the overlap, and what happens if you leave it in?  →  Anyone in both events is counted once in P(A) and once in P(B), so the overlap is counted twice. Leaving it in makes the answer too large, and it can come out above 1.
- rp.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.04 [verified] (definition) What does it mean for two events to be mutually exclusive, and what does the addition rule become?  →  They cannot both happen, so the intersection is empty and P(A and B) = 0. The rule becomes P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
- rp.fm.u3.addition-rule-probability.05 [verified] (trap) How do you decide whether to add or to multiply two probabilities?  →  Or means at least one, so add and take the overlap off once. And means both together, so for independent events multiply.
