### we.science.b1.b1-minerals-eutrophication.01 [verified] paper B1 calc
STEM: Describe and explain the process by which a root hair cell takes up nitrate ions from the soil. [4]
  FIG: svg — Two compartments separated by a membrane: few mineral ions in the soil water, many in the cytoplasm, with an arrow crossing against the gradient labelled active transport.
  1. Nitrate ions are taken up by active transport (active uptake).   — Name the process first. Summer 2024's examiners said only the better answers linked mineral absorption to active uptake at all.  (P1)
  2. They move against the concentration gradient — from the low concentration in the soil water to the higher concentration inside the cell.   — State the direction in both forms: the phrase 'against the concentration gradient' and the low-to-high description. Summer 2025's report names the gradient as where most errors were.  (P2)
  3. This requires energy.   — A separate marking point from where the energy comes from. Say both.  (P3)
  4. The energy is released by respiration in the root hair cell.   — Finish with respiration. 'It uses ATP' is not on this specification; 'energy from respiration' is the scheme's wording.  (P4)
  FINAL: Nitrate ions are taken up by active transport. They are moved against the concentration gradient, from the lower concentration in the soil water into the higher concentration inside the cell. This requires energy, and that energy is released by respiration in the root hair cell.
  TWIN: A root hair cell also takes in water. Name the process, and state how it differs from the uptake of nitrate ions.  → text accepted=["water enters by osmosis, which moves down the concentration gradient and needs no energy, whereas nitrate ions are moved against the gradient by active transport using energy from respiration"] keyWords=[{"any":["osmosis"],"marks":1,"reject":["active transport moves the water"]},{"any":["no energy","needs no energy","without energy"],"marks":1},{"any":["against the gradient","active transport"],"marks":1}]

### we.science.b1.b1-minerals-eutrophication.02 [verified] paper B1 calc
STEM: Slurry from a farmyard is washed into a lake after heavy rain. Explain how this can lead to the death of the fish in the lake. [5]
  FIG: svg — Seven numbered boxes joined by arrows giving the stages of eutrophication in order.
  1. The slurry adds nitrates (and phosphates) to the lake water.   — Name the ion. Summer 2024's report says answers giving 'nutrients' earned nothing for this point.  (P1)
  2. The nitrate makes the algae and water plants grow and reproduce rapidly, forming an algal bloom.   — Say what the nitrate does, not just that it is there.  (P2)
  3. The bloom shades the plants below it, and the nitrate is used up, so the plants and algae die.   — Two causes, one consequence. Shading and nitrate depletion are both in the specification's own bullet.  (P3)
  4. Aerobic decomposing bacteria feed on the dead material and multiply, respiring as they do so.   — This is the first of the three stages the Summer 2025 report found blank. Name the bacteria as aerobic.  (P4)
  5. Their respiration uses up the dissolved oxygen, so the fish cannot respire and they die.   — Finish on oxygen and then on the fish. An answer that stops at 'the bacteria decompose them' loses the two marks the question was really set for.  (P5)
  FINAL: The slurry adds nitrates to the lake. The extra nitrate makes the algae and water plants grow and reproduce rapidly, forming a bloom at the surface. The bloom shades the plants beneath it and the nitrate is soon used up, so the plants and algae die. Aerobic decomposing bacteria feed on the dead material and multiply, and their aerobic respiration uses up the dissolved oxygen in the water. With too little dissolved oxygen the fish cannot respire, and they die.
  TWIN: Untreated sewage is released into a slow-moving river. Explain why the number of invertebrate species falls just downstream of the outflow.  → text accepted=["the sewage adds nitrate so algae grow rapidly, the bloom shades the plants and the nitrate runs out so they die, aerobic bacteria decompose them and multiply, their respiration uses up the dissolved oxygen and the invertebrates cannot respire so fewer species survive"] keyWords=[{"any":["nitrate","phosphate"],"marks":1,"reject":["nutrients"]},{"any":["algae","bloom"],"marks":1},{"any":["bacteria","decompose"],"marks":1},{"any":["oxygen"],"marks":1}]

### we.science.b1.b1-minerals-eutrophication.03 [verified] paper B1 calc
STEM: The graph shows nitrate uptake by barley roots at different oxygen concentrations.
Describe the relationship, and explain what it shows about how nitrate is absorbed. [4]
  FIG: svg — A line graph of nitrate uptake by roots against oxygen concentration, rising steeply then levelling off.
  1. As the oxygen concentration increases the nitrate uptake increases, from 0.4 units at zero oxygen to 7 units.   — Describe with numbers taken off the graph. A trend quoted without values is the commonest way to lose the description mark.  (P1)
  2. Above about 8 units of oxygen the uptake levels off and stays at its maximum.   — The second half of the description. The plateau is always worth its own mark.  (P2)
  3. Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, which releases energy.   — Now explain. Oxygen is not part of the nitrate ion, so its only possible role is supplying energy.  (P3)
  4. Nitrate must therefore be absorbed by active transport, which needs that energy; with no oxygen there is almost no uptake.   — Name the process and use the zero-oxygen reading as the evidence. Diffusion would not depend on oxygen at all.  (P4)
  FINAL: As the oxygen concentration increases, nitrate uptake increases sharply, from 0.4 units with no oxygen up to 7 units, after which it levels off at its maximum. Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, which releases energy, so this shows that nitrate is absorbed by active transport rather than by diffusion: with no oxygen there is almost no energy and almost no uptake.
  TWIN: Suggest why a barley plant growing in a waterlogged field takes up very little nitrate, even when the soil is rich in it.  → text accepted=["a waterlogged soil has no air spaces so there is very little oxygen, the roots cannot respire aerobically and release enough energy, and active transport of nitrate therefore almost stops"] keyWords=[{"any":["little oxygen","no oxygen","waterlogged"],"marks":1},{"any":["respire","respiration"],"marks":1},{"any":["active transport","energy"],"marks":1}]

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.b1.b1-minerals-eutrophication (pre) [verified]
- 01 (Name the process): How does a root hair cell take in mineral ions?
    ✔ by active transport — Active transport (active uptake). Summer 2024 examiners said only the better answers made this link.
    ✘ by osmosis [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — Osmosis is how water enters. Mineral ions need active transport.
    ✘ by diffusion down the gradient [sci.active-transport.gradient-direction] — Diffusion would move ions out of the cell, because the concentration inside is higher.
    ✘ by photosynthesis [sci.nitrogen.nitrate-use-wrong] — Photosynthesis makes glucose; it does not move ions. Nitrate is what proteins are made from.
- 02 (The direction of the gradient): Active transport moves mineral ions
    ✔ from a low concentration to a high concentration — Against the gradient — uphill, which is why it costs energy.
    ✘ from a high concentration to a low concentration [sci.active-transport.gradient-direction] — That is diffusion, and it is free. Summer 2025 examiners reported many errors on this direction.
    ✘ in whichever direction the water is moving [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — Ions and water move independently, and by different processes.
    ✘ only when the concentrations are equal [sci.active-transport.gradient-direction] — Active transport works precisely when the concentrations are unequal, and it works against the difference.
- 03 (Osmosis, not active transport): How does water enter a root hair cell?
    ✔ by osmosis, needing no energy — Osmosis, down the water's own gradient, with no energy needed.
    ✘ by active transport, using energy from respiration [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — Examiners have reported this in two separate series. Only the mineral ions use active transport.
    ✘ by diffusion of water molecules through the cell wall only [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — The movement of water across a partially permeable membrane has its own name: osmosis.
    ✘ it is pulled in by the nitrate ions [sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport] — The ions do change the water potential, but the process that moves the water is still osmosis.
- 04 (It is the extension): Which feature identifies a root hair cell in a drawing?
    ✔ a long narrow extension from one side — The extension. Summer 2025's mark scheme wanted exactly 'it has no extension' for the cell that was not one.
    ✘ it looks like a hair on a person's head [sci.root-hair.adaptation-not-named] — Summer 2025 examiners stated that descriptions in terms of body parts were not accepted.
    ✘ it contains chloroplasts [sci.root-hair.adaptation-not-named] — Root hair cells have no chloroplasts — they are underground, with no light.
    ✘ it has no nucleus [sci.root-hair.adaptation-not-named] — It has a nucleus like almost every plant cell. What makes it special is its shape.
- 05 (Nitrate is for protein): What do plants use absorbed nitrate for?
    ✔ to make amino acids and proteins — Proteins, built from amino acids. That is what the specification's bullet says: absorption of nitrates for proteins.
    ✘ to make chlorophyll [sci.nitrogen.nitrate-use-wrong] — Chlorophyll is what magnesium is for in other specifications — and magnesium is not on CCEA's B1 at all. Nitrate is for protein.
    ✘ as a source of energy [sci.nitrogen.nitrate-use-wrong] — Energy comes from glucose by respiration. Nitrate supplies nitrogen atoms.
    ✘ to make starch for storage [sci.nitrogen.nitrate-use-wrong] — Starch is made from glucose and contains no nitrogen.
- 06 (The decomposers, after the algae die): In eutrophication, what uses up the dissolved oxygen?
    ✔ aerobic bacteria respiring as they decompose the dead plants and algae — The decomposers. Summer 2025 examiners reported the last three stages as the ones left wrong or blank.
    ✘ the living algae, as they grow [sci.eutrophication.algae-use-up-oxygen-directly] — Living algae photosynthesise and add oxygen to the water. The crisis comes after they die.
    ✘ the mat of algae stops oxygen dissolving from the air [sci.eutrophication.algae-use-up-oxygen-directly] — A surface mat does reduce the exchange a little, but the marking point is the bacteria respiring.
    ✘ the nitrate reacts with the oxygen in the water [sci.eutrophication.algae-use-up-oxygen-directly] — No chemical reaction is involved. Living organisms take the oxygen out by respiring.
- 07 (Name the ion): A question asks what is washed off a field into a river to start eutrophication. Which answer scores?
    ✔ nitrate from fertiliser — The ion, named. Phosphate is also accepted, and so is the source: fertiliser, slurry or sewage.
    ✘ nutrients [sci.eutrophication.vague-nutrients] — Summer 2024 examiners reported that 'nutrients' instead of nitrate or phosphate earned nothing for that point.
    ✘ minerals [sci.eutrophication.vague-nutrients] — Too vague in the same way. Which mineral? The mark is for naming it.
    ✘ chemicals [sci.eutrophication.vague-nutrients] — Everything is a chemical. Write nitrate or phosphate.

## FIND THE MISTAKE
### ftm.science.b1.b1-minerals-eutrophication.01 [verified] sci.active-transport.water-by-active-transport · earned P3 · ccea-cer:science:2025-summer:B1H:Q7
STEM: Aoife is asked: 'Describe how a root hair cell takes in water and mineral ions. [4]'
Her answer:
    1. The root hair cell has a hair like the ones on your head, which reaches into the soil.
  ✘ 2. It takes in water and mineral ions by active transport.
    3. They move down the concentration gradient into the cell.
    4. This uses energy.
  WRONG: **Water does not enter by active transport** — it enters by **osmosis**, and osmosis needs no energy. Line 3 then has the second error: active transport moves ions **against** the concentration gradient, from **low to high**, not down it. Line 1 is a third: Summer 2025's mark scheme states that descriptions of the root hair cell **in terms of body parts are not acceptable** — the word is **extension**.
  FIX: The root hair cell has a long narrow extension, which gives it a large surface area. / Water enters by osmosis, which needs no energy. / Mineral ions enter by active transport, against the concentration gradient, from the low concentration in the soil to the higher concentration in the cell. / Active transport requires energy, released by respiration.
  FEEDBACK: One mark from four, for 'this uses energy'. Every other line contains something the mark scheme explicitly rejects, which is worth knowing because none of it is a hard idea. Build the answer as two columns in your head before you write: **water — osmosis — no energy**, and **mineral ions — active transport — against the gradient — energy from respiration**. Then describe the shape as an **extension**, never as a hair.

### ftm.science.b1.b1-minerals-eutrophication.02 [verified] sci.eutrophication.algae-use-up-oxygen-directly · earned P2,P5 · ccea-cer:science:2024-summer:B1H:Q8
STEM: Niall is asked: 'Explain how fertiliser run-off can kill the fish in a lake. [5]'
His answer:
    1. Nutrients from the field are washed into the lake.
    2. This makes the algae grow really fast and cover the surface.
  ✘ 3. The algae use up all the oxygen in the water.
    4. So the fish cannot breathe and they die.
  WRONG: **The living algae do not use up the oxygen** — while they are alive they photosynthesise and add it. The oxygen falls only **after** the algae die: **aerobic decomposing bacteria** multiply on the dead material and **their** respiration strips the dissolved oxygen out. Line 1 has a second problem: **'nutrients' earns nothing**, because Summer 2024's examiners wanted the ion **named** — nitrate or phosphate. Two whole stages are also missing: the **shading and nitrate depletion**, and the **death of the plants and algae**.
  FIX: Nitrate from the fertiliser is washed into the lake. / The nitrate makes the algae grow and reproduce rapidly, forming a bloom at the surface. / The bloom shades the plants below and the nitrate is used up, so the algae and plants die. / Aerobic decomposing bacteria feed on the dead material, multiply, and use up the dissolved oxygen as they respire. / The fish cannot respire without dissolved oxygen, so they die.
  FEEDBACK: Two marks from five. The shape of the story was right, which is the encouraging part; what was missing is the middle of the chain and one named ion. Two habits close the gap.
