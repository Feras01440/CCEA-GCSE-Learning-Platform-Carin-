## WORKED EXAMPLES
### we.science.b1.b1-fieldwork-sampling.01 [verified] paper 7 calc
STEM: A field is 40 m long and 25 m wide. A student placed a quadrat of side 0.5 m at 10 random positions and counted the daisies in each: 7, 4, 9, 6, 3, 8, 5, 6, 4, 8.
Estimate the number of daisies in the whole field. [3]
  FIG: svg — A field drawn as a grid with five quadrat positions shaded at random co-ordinates.
  1. Mean per quadrat = (7 + 4 + 9 + 6 + 3 + 8 + 5 + 6 + 4 + 8) ÷ 10 = 60 ÷ 10 = 6   — Always start with the mean per quadrat. Put the whole sum in brackets on the calculator, or the division happens before the addition.  (P1)
  2. Number of quadrat areas in the field = (40 × 25) ÷ 0.25 = 1000 ÷ 0.25 = 4000   — The quadrat is 0.5 m square, so its area is 0.25 m², not 1 m². Dividing the field area by the quadrat area is the step people skip.  (P2)
  3. Estimated number = 6 × 4000 = 24000 daisies   — Multiply the mean by the number of quadrat areas. Check the size of the answer: tens of thousands is right for a small plant in a field this size.  (P3)
  FINAL: The mean is 6 daisies per quadrat. The field is 1000 m² and each quadrat covers 0.25 m², so 4000 quadrats would fit. The estimate is 6 × 4000 = 24000 daisies.
  TWIN: A lawn measures 20 m by 10 m. Five quadrats of side 0.5 m gave dandelion counts of 3, 5, 2, 4 and 6. Estimate the number of dandelions on the lawn.  → numeric 3200 dandelions tol={"type":"exact"} forms=decimal

### we.science.b1.b1-fieldwork-sampling.02 [verified] paper 7 calc
STEM: Describe how you would estimate the number of daisy plants growing in a school field. [6]
  FIG: svg — A dichotomous key with three numbered questions leading to clover, ribwort plantain, buttercup and daisy.
  1. Lay two tape measures along two edges of the field at right angles and use pairs of random numbers as co-ordinates.   — Say how the randomness is produced. 'Randomly' on its own is a claim; random numbers used as co-ordinates is a method.  (P1)
  2. Place a quadrat of known area at the first pair of co-ordinates.   — Name the apparatus. Summer 2025 examiners reported candidates who had done the practical but could not name the quadrat.  (P2)
  3. Use an identification key to work out which plants inside the quadrat are daisies.   — This is the point almost nobody wrote. A key is how you know which species you are counting.  (P3)
  4. Count the daisies inside the quadrat and record the number in a results table.   — Counting and recording are separate indicative points in the scheme.  (P4)
  5. Repeat at several more random positions.   — Repeats are what make the estimate reliable rather than a single lucky square.  (P5)
  6. Calculate the mean number per quadrat, then multiply by the number of quadrat areas that fit in the field.   — Finish with the arithmetic that turns your samples into an estimate for the whole field.  (P6)
  FINAL: Lay two tape measures along two edges of the field at right angles and generate pairs of random numbers to use as co-ordinates. Place a quadrat of known area at the first pair of co-ordinates. Use an identification key to decide which plants inside the frame are daisies, count them, and record the number in a results table. Repeat at several more random positions, calculate the mean number of daisies per quadrat, and multiply that mean by the number of quadrat areas that fit into the field to estimate the total.
  TWIN: Describe how you would find out whether buttercups are more common at the edge of a wood than in the middle of it.  → text accepted=["lay a belt transect from the edge of the wood into the middle, place a quadrat at fixed intervals along the line, use a key to identify the buttercups, count them in each quadrat, record the results and compare the mean number at the edge with the mean number in the middle"] keyWords=[{"any":["transect","line"],"marks":1},{"any":["quadrat"],"marks":1},{"any":["key","identification key"],"marks":1},{"any":["mean","average"],"marks":1}]

## DIAGNOSTICS
### dx.science.b1.b1-fieldwork-sampling (pre) [verified]
- 01 (Tell the six ecological terms apart): All the different species of plants and animals living in a rock pool, taken together, are called
    ✔ a community — A community: every population in that habitat. Add the water, rock and light and you have the ecosystem.
    ✘ a population [sci.ecology.terms-confused] — A population is one species only. All the species together is the community.
    ✘ a habitat [sci.ecology.terms-confused] — The habitat is the rock pool itself — the place. The organisms in it form the community.
    ✘ an ecosystem [sci.ecology.terms-confused] — Very close. An ecosystem is the community plus the non-living surroundings it interacts with.
- 02 (Separate biotic from abiotic): Which of these is an abiotic factor?
    ✔ the light intensity — Light is one of the five abiotic factors the specification names, along with wind speed, water, pH and temperature.
    ✘ the number of plant species [sci.abiotic.biotic-abiotic-confused] — That is biodiversity, which is a biotic factor — it is about living things.
    ✘ the number of rabbits [sci.abiotic.biotic-abiotic-confused] — Rabbits are alive, so this is biotic. Abiotic factors are the non-living conditions.
    ✘ the amount of grazing by sheep [sci.abiotic.biotic-abiotic-confused] — Grazing is done by living animals, so it is biotic.
- 03 (Recognise genuine random sampling): Which method gives a fair estimate of how many daisies are in a whole field?
    ✔ quadrats placed at co-ordinates taken from random numbers — Random numbers as co-ordinates give every part of the field the same chance of being sampled.
    ✘ quadrats placed wherever there are plenty of daisies [sci.sampling.placement-not-random] — That guarantees an overestimate. The whole point of random sampling is to take the choice away from you.
    ✘ quadrats placed every five metres along one line [sci.sampling.transect-called-random] — That is a belt transect. It shows how a species changes across a habitat, but it does not sample the whole field fairly.
    ✘ one quadrat placed in the middle of the field [sci.sampling.placement-not-random] — One quadrat gives no mean and no way of spotting an unusual patch. Several positions are needed.
- 04 (Remember the key): In a six-mark answer on estimating plant abundance, which step do most candidates leave out?
    ✔ using an identification key to name the species — Exactly this. Summer 2025 examiners reported that few candidates mentioned a key, even though they had done the practical.
    ✘ counting the plants inside the quadrat [sci.practical.quadrat-and-key-not-named] — Almost everybody writes the counting. The step that disappears is identifying the species with a key.
    ✘ recording the results [sci.practical.quadrat-and-key-not-named] — Recording is usually there. Identifying with a key is the one that is missing.
    ✘ repeating the measurement [sci.practical.quadrat-and-key-not-named] — Repeats are usually there too. Try again: what tells you which plant is which?
- 05 (Scale up using the quadrat area): A quadrat of side 0.5 m gives a mean of 6 daisies. How do you scale that up to a field of 1000 m²?
    ✔ multiply by 1000 ÷ 0.25 — The field holds 4000 quadrat areas, so the estimate is 24000.
    ✘ multiply by 1000 [sci.sampling.scaling-quadrat-area-ignored] — That would be right only if the quadrat covered 1 m². It covers 0.25 m², so this answer is four times too small.
    ✘ divide 1000 by the mean [sci.sampling.scaling-quadrat-area-ignored] — That gives an area per daisy, not a number of daisies. Multiply the mean by how many quadrats would fit.
    ✘ multiply by 0.25 [sci.sampling.scaling-quadrat-area-ignored] — Multiplying by a number smaller than one makes the estimate smaller than a single quadrat's count, which cannot be right.
- 06 (Reliability, not accuracy): A student repeats the quadrat count at ten positions instead of one. What does this improve?
    ✔ the reliability of the estimate — Repeats let you take a mean and spot an odd result, which is reliability. Accuracy is a different idea.
    ✘ the accuracy of each count [sci.practical.reliability-accuracy-confused] — Each count is just as accurate as before. Repeating makes the overall estimate more reliable.
    ✘ the validity of the method [sci.practical.reliability-accuracy-confused] — Validity is about whether the method answers the question asked. Repeating improves reliability.
    ✘ the resolution of the measurement [sci.practical.reliability-accuracy-confused] — Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can show. Counting whole plants does not change with repeats.

### ftm.science.b1.b1-fieldwork-sampling.01 [verified] sci.sampling.placement-not-random · earned QWC1 · ccea-cer:science:2025-summer:U7BBioH:Q1
STEM: Ciara is asked: 'Describe how you would estimate the number of daisies in a field. [6]'
Her answer:
  ✘ 1. I would walk around the field and find a good patch of daisies.
    2. I would put the square frame down there and count the daisies inside it.
    3. I would do that three more times and add up all the numbers.
    4. Then I would multiply the total by the area of the field in square metres.
  WRONG: Choosing a **good patch** is the opposite of random sampling: it guarantees an overestimate, and the mark for random placement is lost at the first line. Three more problems follow. The frame is never named as a **quadrat**, no **key** is used to identify the plants, and the last line scales the **total** rather than the **mean**, and multiplies by the field area rather than by the number of **quadrat areas** that fit into it.
  FIX: I would lay two tape measures along two edges of the field and use pairs of random numbers as co-ordinates. / I would place a quadrat at the first pair of co-ordinates, use a key to identify the daisies, count them and record the number. / I would repeat at several more random positions and calculate the mean number per quadrat. / Then I would multiply the mean by the field area divided by the area of the quadrat.
  FEEDBACK: About two of six as written: counting and repeating are there, but the method is not a sampling method. Two habits rescue this answer. First, never let yourself choose the spot — write 'random numbers used as co-ordinates' every time. Second, name the apparatus and the key in your first two sentences, because examiners report both as the points that go missing.
