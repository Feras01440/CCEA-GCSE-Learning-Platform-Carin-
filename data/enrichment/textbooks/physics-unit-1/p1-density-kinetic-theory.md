The whole of the book's section 1.3 (six pages) maps to this one topic, and it is solid: three measurement methods (liquid, regular solid, irregular solid), the mass–volume graph's gradient as density, nine calculations including both unit systems and a unit conversion, and a kinetic-theory table that explains the density order. ## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Density is mass per unit volume, in kg/m³ or g/cm³ (p. 32).
2. A worked pair: a mass from a density and a volume, then a volume from a density and a mass (p. 32).
3. To measure a density you need a mass and a volume (p. 33).
4. A liquid: weigh the empty cylinder, add the liquid and read the volume where the meniscus bottoms out, weigh again, subtract, divide, repeat and average (p. 33).
5. A regular solid: balance for the mass, ruler or callipers for the sides, a volume formula (cuboid, cylinder) (p. 33).
6. An irregular solid: lower it on a thread into a measuring cylinder; the rise in level is its volume (p. 34).
7. On a mass–volume graph the gradient is the density: steeper means denser (p. 34).
8. Six calculations, in both unit systems (p. 35).
9. Kinetic theory: a three-column table (spacing, motion, forces, shape and volume, density) for solids, liquids and gases, with particle drawings; the density order explained by spacing; ice as the exception (p. 36).
10. Practice: describing the measuring-cylinder method for a bracelet, identifying a metal from its density, checking a slab against a minimum density, and converting g/cm³ to kg/m³ (pp. 36–37).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **Density from spacing** (p. 36). The density order (solid, then liquid, then gas) follows from how far apart the particles are, which is exactly the specification's 1.3.5 reasoning. CCEA's 2018 November Higher scheme (Q2(b)(ii)) credits "particles closer" for the denser material; the Summer 2024 report (Higher Q8(b)(iii)) says candidates described the change from water to steam in imprecise words.
- **The ice exception** (p. 36). Ice floats because it is less dense than water. Not on the specification, but a natural "why does it matter" moment; keep it as an aside.
- **Identify the material from its density** (pp. 36–37). Measure, calculate, compare with a table. A good closing task because the answer is a word, not a number.
- **Convert by thinking about 1 m³** (p. 37). A cubic metre holds a million cubic centimetres, so 1 g/cm³ is 1000 kg/m³. The book does it in three steps; the specification names both units (1.3.4).
- **Volume first, then density** (p. 37, concrete slab; p. 35, room of air). Several CCEA items need a volume from dimensions before the density equation: the Summer 2023 Higher Q5(a) works backwards from a density to a volume to a missing height (`docs/sources/papers/science/2023-Summer/P1-H-Physics-MS-29631.txt`).

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Mass and volume from a density** (pp. 32, 35): forward and backward use of D = m/V in g/cm³.
- **Density from mass and volume** (p. 35): a direct division.
- **Air in a room** (p. 35): a volume from three dimensions, then a mass in kg from a density in kg/m³.
- **A stone by displacement** (p. 35): the rise in a measuring cylinder, then the density.
- **Petrol in a tank** (p. 35): mass from volume and density in SI units.
- **Density of air** (p. 35): weigh a container empty and full of air; the small difference over its volume, converted to kg/m³.
- **Bracelet and metal table** (pp. 36–37): method, density, identification.
- **Concrete slab** (p. 37): a volume from dimensions, a density, a comparison with a minimum.
- **Unit conversion** (p. 37): g/cm³ to kg/m³.

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Describe** how to measure a density, as a six-mark extended answer: a liquid (2019 Summer Higher Q1: balance, measuring cylinder, empty mass, full mass, density = m/V, and a precaution such as no spills or reading the meniscus at eye level; the scheme says "not repeat" for that point) or an irregular solid (2018 November Higher Q2: balance, measuring cylinder, density = m/V, avoid spills, repeat or average). `docs/sources/papers/science/2019-Summer/P1-H-Physics-MS-9998.txt`; `docs/sources/papers/science/2018-November/P1-H-Physics-MS-10067.txt`.
- **Calculate** a density, a mass or a volume (2–4; 2019 November Higher Q3(a): a volume from a mass and a density).
- **Volume from dimensions** inside a density problem (4; Summer 2023 Higher Q5(a): a missing height).
- **Explain** a density difference by particle spacing (1–2; 2018 November Higher Q2(b)(ii), Summer 2024 Higher Q8(b)(iii); Summer 2026 Higher Q2 is a six-mark extended answer linking each state's density to its particle spacing).
- **Read** a mass–volume graph: steepest line, largest density (2; 2025 November Higher Q8(a): the same material is both "largest density" and "largest mass-to-volume ratio"), then a density to one decimal place with its unit (Q8(b): 0.9 g/cm³).
- **Unit 7**: the displacement method (Summer 2025 report: missed by many) and density written as mass × volume (a common error).

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Density** (p. 32): mass per unit volume, the same as the specification's equation. The Summer 2023 Foundation report notes incorrect density formulas; the deck's "mass divided by volume" card is the right guard.
- **"Molecules"** (p. 36): the book's table says molecules; the specification says particles. Use "particles", which covers atoms and ions as well.
- **Reliability** (p. 33): the book closes the liquid method with "repeat and average for reliability". Right as science, but the 2019 Summer scheme did not accept "repeat" as the precaution point; teach a measuring precaution as well (eyes level with the meniscus, read its lowest point, no splashing).
- **Eureka can**: named in the specification (1.3.3) and in the book's learning-outcome list (p. 32), but not taught in the body.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Take the volume reading from the lowest point of the meniscus (p. 33).
- For an irregular solid the volume is the rise in level, not the final reading (p. 35).
- Keep units consistent: g with cm³, kg with m³ (p. 35).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **The three measurement methods**, each as a short explain-and-show (liquid by difference; regular solid by formula; irregular solid by displacement, with both the measuring cylinder and the eureka can), then a six-mark Your turn scaffolded like the 2019 and 2018 schemes.
2. **D = m/V in both unit systems**, three arrangements, with a non-example of m × V (cite the reports).
3. **Volume from dimensions inside a density problem**, forwards and backwards (a missing side from a density).
4. **Kinetic theory for density**: spacing first; particle boxes drawn correctly; a two-mark "explain why a gas is less dense" item.
5. **The 1.3.1–1.3.2 investigation**: vary the volume of a liquid (or use several blocks of one material), plot mass against volume, a straight line through the origin means mass is proportional to volume, and the gradient is the density.
6. **Unit conversion** g/cm³ ↔ kg/m³ as a short gate.
