## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 1.4: learning outcomes p. 38; the resources column of the forms table p. 39; "Renewable and Non-Renewable Energy Resources" p. 40 (definitions, examples, an exam tip, environmental effects). The nuclear-power debate is on p. 67 (see `p1-nuclear-fission`).

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Example resources sit beside the forms they supply: coal, oil, gas, peat, wood and food for chemical energy; a hydroelectric reservoir for gravitational potential; wind, waves and tides for kinetic; uranium and plutonium for nuclear (p. 39).
2. Renewable resources will never run out: solar, wind, tidal, hydroelectric, wood, geothermal (p. 40).
3. Why the specification also allows "replenished within a human lifetime": strictly, even the Sun will run out in a few billion years (p. 40).
4. Non-renewable resources are finite: fossil fuels, and uranium because its ore will not last (p. 40).
5. Exam tip: defining a renewable as one you can keep reusing earns nothing, because energy once used cannot be used a second time (p. 40).
6. Environmental effects: wind farms as visual pollution, dams and reservoirs destroying habitats, fossil fuels warming the climate through carbon dioxide, nuclear power leaving long-lived radioactive waste (p. 40).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **Why "never runs out" needs a second wording** (p. 40). Pointing out that the Sun itself has a lifetime explains why CCEA accepts "replaced within a human lifetime"; a student who understands that will not write "renewed" or "reused". The 2021 November Higher scheme (Q2, a six-mark answer marked by bands) credits either idea for the definition point: it will not run out, or it is replaced within a lifetime, which the scheme glosses as about a century (`docs/sources/papers/science/2021-November/P1-H-Physics-MS-19359.txt`).
- **"Used again" is wrong, and why** (p. 40). Energy that has been used has spread out and cannot be used a second time; it is the resource that is replaced. The Summer 2024 and 2025 and March 2026 Foundation reports all list "renewed", "reused" or "used again" as the commonest wrong definition.
- **Resources linked to forms** (p. 39). Wind, waves and tides as kinetic energy, a reservoir as gravitational potential energy: a bridge from `p1-energy-forms-conservation-efficiency` that makes the lists less arbitrary.

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Define** a renewable resource (1–2; almost every Foundation series; 2021 November Higher Q2 as part of a six-mark answer).
- **Classify** resources (1–2; the March 2026 Foundation report: wood called non-renewable and nuclear renewable; Summer 2023 Foundation: nuclear or natural gas called renewable; 2024 November Higher Q9: is fission energy renewable?).
- **Give** an environmental effect of a renewable or a non-renewable resource (1 each; the specification's examples are habitat destruction, visual pollution, acid rain and global warming; Summer 2024 Foundation report: nuclear wrongly chosen as the biggest contributor to global warming).
- **Six-mark account** (2021 November Higher Q2: a definition, a renewable example, two non-renewables, fossil fuels as non-renewable, and one environmental effect such as acid rain).

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Renewable** (p. 40): the book's two versions are the specification's (1.4.5) and the glossary's.
- **Non-renewable** (p. 40): finite supply, will run out; fossil fuels cannot be replaced within a human lifetime; uranium ore is finite. Matches 1.4.8–1.4.9.
- **Solar** (book) against **sunlight** (specification 1.4.6): either name is fine.
- **Acid rain** (1.4.10): in the book's learning-outcome list (p. 38) only; the body gives global warming as the fossil-fuel effect. The 2021 November scheme credits either.
- **"Evaluate"** (1.4.6): the book lists renewables but weighs none of them (reliability, cost, location, output). This is the main gap.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Never define renewable as something that can be used again (p. 40).
- Uranium, and so nuclear fission, is non-renewable (p. 40).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **The two definitions**, with the "reused" non-example and its reason.
2. **Classification**, with wood and nuclear as the named traps (sort interaction).
3. **Evaluating the specification's renewables**: for sunlight, wind, hydroelectricity, tidal, waves, wood and geothermal, one strength and one weakness each (for example wind is variable; tidal is predictable but suits few sites), so "evaluate" has substance.
4. **Environmental effects**: habitat destruction and visual pollution (renewables), acid rain and global warming (fossil fuels), long-lived waste (nuclear), each with its cause.
5. **A six-mark account** on the 2021 November pattern, with a new context.
