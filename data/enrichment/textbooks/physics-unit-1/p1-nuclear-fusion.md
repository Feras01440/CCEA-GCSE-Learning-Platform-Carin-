# p1-nuclear-fusion: textbook dossier

**Book.** CCEA's Higher Tier eGuide to Physics Unit 1 of Double Award Science, © CCEA 2023 (git-ignored under `docs/sources/textbooks/`). Page numbers are the book's printed numbers; the PDF page is the printed number plus 4.
**Ours.** **No note and no bundle yet.** The slug exists in our taxonomy (difficulty 5) and the P1 deck (`data/decks/science/P1.json`) has 12 flashcards for it; `packs/science/content/p1/` has no folder. This dossier is a briefing for the first author.
**Spec.** P1 1.5.20, 1.5.21, 1.5.22, 1.5.23 (all Foundation).
**Verdict.** **Thinner** (nothing authored). The book explains *why* fusion needs enormous temperatures (positive nuclei repel, so only very fast ones get close enough), draws the deuterium–tritium reaction, and lists the advantages in the specification's words. But its body omits the number CCEA asks for most often (fusion releases about four million times more energy per kilogram than burning a fuel: it is only in the book's learning-outcome list), omits the specification's cost and timescale points, and says the Sun fuses deuterium with tritium, which is not how the Sun mainly works.

## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 1.5: learning outcomes pp. 51–52 (the only place the four-million and fifty-year figures appear); "Nuclear Fusion" p. 66 (the process in stars, the deuterium–tritium reaction with a diagram and equation, attempts on Earth); "Difficulties with Fusion" p. 67 (containment, fuel from seawater, helium); the energy-per-kilogram comparison with fission in the debate list p. 67.

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Fusion powers stars like the Sun, whose core is at about fifteen million degrees (p. 66).
2. At that temperature atoms lose their electrons and the bare nuclei move very fast; they repel because both are positive, but fast enough ones get close and join (p. 66).
3. Deuterium and tritium join to form helium-4 and a neutron, releasing energy, some of which reaches Earth as electromagnetic radiation; a diagram and an equation (p. 66).
4. Controlled fusion on Earth: a European reactor's record output in 1997, progress in 2022, a working reactor still decades away (p. 66).
5. The main difficulty: holding the hot plasma long enough for the reaction to happen (p. 67).
6. Advantages: deuterium and tritium from seawater, nearly inexhaustible; no carbon dioxide; helium, inert and non-toxic, as the main by-product (p. 67).
7. Fusion releases about four times as much energy per kilogram as fission (p. 67).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **Why so hot** (p. 66). Nuclei are positive and push each other away; only nuclei moving very fast can get close enough to join, and speed means temperature. This turns the specification's "very high temperatures" (1.5.23) from a fact into a reason, and explains why containment is hard and costly.
- **Name what joins, what forms, what is released** (p. 66). Two light nuclei (isotopes of hydrogen) join, a helium nucleus forms, energy is released. CCEA's Summer 2025 Higher six-mark scheme (Q3, marked by bands) lists six points: nuclei joining, energy being given out, where it happens naturally (stars or the Sun), the two hydrogen isotopes as two separate points, and the helium formed (`docs/sources/papers/science/2025-Summer/P1-H-Physics-MS-67250.txt`); the report says many confused fusion with fission, wrote "atoms", or gave energy as the by-product.
- **The by-product is helium, and energy is not a by-product** (p. 67). The book names helium as the major by-product; the deck's trap card adds why "energy" is wrong.
- **Two different "times more" numbers** (pp. 52, 67). Four times fission per kilogram; four million times burning a fossil fuel per kilogram. CCEA asks for both, sometimes in one question; our note must keep them apart.

## 7. Definitions and wording, and a book error

- **Fusion**: the glossary's definition (two light nuclei such as hydrogen joined to give a heavier, more stable nucleus such as helium, giving out energy) is the one to learn; the book has no one-sentence definition.
- **Book error: deuterium and tritium in the Sun** (p. 66). The Sun releases its energy mainly by fusing ordinary hydrogen nuclei (protons) into helium in a chain of steps; deuterium–tritium fusion is the reaction planned for reactors on Earth. CCEA's Summer 2025 scheme lists stars or the Sun, deuterium, tritium and helium as separate points, so an answer naming both earns the marks; but our note must not state that the Sun fuses deuterium with tritium. Say: stars fuse hydrogen into helium; reactors would use deuterium and tritium.
- **Seawater**: the specification (1.5.21) says deuterium and tritium are widely available in seawater, and the 2023 November scheme credits "seawater". That is the marked answer. (Strictly, deuterium is plentiful in seawater; tritium is very rare in nature and would be made in the reactor. Do not add this to the answer; do not contradict the specification either.)
- **Four million times** (1.5.22): only in the book's learning outcomes (p. 52). **Fifty years** and **expensive to build and to contain** (1.5.23): the book says only "decades away" and that containment is the main problem. The schemes (2023 November Higher Q7(iv), 2 marks) credit any two of: building cost, the extreme temperature needed, and keeping the reaction contained.
- **Fusion and fission per kilogram**: the book's "about four times" (p. 67) matches 1.5.22.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Fusion joins light nuclei; fission splits a heavy one (pp. 65–66).
- Helium is the main by-product (p. 67).
- Very high temperature is needed because nuclei repel (p. 66).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **The fusion account in six points** (joining of light nuclei; deuterium and tritium; helium formed; energy released; very high temperature; in the Sun and stars), with "atoms" and "energy as by-product" as non-examples; a six-mark Your turn with headings, as CCEA sets it.
2. **The two numbers**: four million times burning a fuel; four times fission; a short gate on each and a twin calculation (given one energy, find the other).
3. **Why so hot, and therefore why difficult and expensive**: repulsion, temperature, containment, cost, about fifty years.
4. **Advantages**: fuel from seawater (the specification's wording), no carbon dioxide, helium inert and non-toxic.
5. **Fission against fusion** side by side (link to `p1-nuclear-fission`).
