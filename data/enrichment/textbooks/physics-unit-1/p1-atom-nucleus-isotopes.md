# p1-atom-nucleus-isotopes: textbook dossier

**Book.** CCEA's Higher Tier eGuide to Physics Unit 1 of Double Award Science, © CCEA 2023 (git-ignored under `docs/sources/textbooks/`). Page numbers are the book's printed numbers; the PDF page is the printed number plus 4.
**Ours.** **No note and no bundle yet.** The slug exists in our taxonomy (prerequisite `c1-atomic-structure`) and the P1 deck (`data/decks/science/P1.json`) has 9 flashcards for it; `packs/science/content/p1/` has no folder. This dossier is a briefing for the first author.
**Spec.** P1 1.5.1, 1.5.2, 1.5.3, 1.5.4 (all Foundation).
**Verdict.** **Thinner** (nothing authored). The book gives the particle table CCEA marks (location, relative mass, relative charge), nuclide notation with a worked uranium example, a good isotope definition and a short self-test. Half of its four pages, though, is the history of the atomic model (plum pudding, Rutherford's gold foil, Chadwick), which the Double Award specification does not ask for and no P1 paper we hold (2018–2026) has examined. One of its examples asks for the electrons "in the nucleus" and answers zero, which could confuse a student who expects the neutral atom.

## 1. Book chapter and pages

The book's section 1.5 opens with its learning outcomes on pp. 50–52, then: "Structure of Atoms" pp. 52–53 (electrons from a hot wire, the plum-pudding model, Rutherford's scattering, his conclusions, a scattering diagram, the neutron, the particle table); "Isotopes" p. 54 (mass and atomic numbers, nuclide notation, the isotope definition, an oxygen example); Test Yourself 1.5.1 p. 55. Answers p. 72.

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Atoms have structure; electrons were found first, from heated wires (p. 52).
2. The plum-pudding model: negative electrons dotted through a spread of positive charge (p. 52).
3. Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering and what it showed: most passed straight through, a few were turned sharply, a tiny fraction bounced back (p. 52).
4. His conclusions: a small, positive, massive nucleus; the rest of the atom mostly empty (p. 53).
5. The neutron's discovery about twenty years later (p. 53).
6. The particle table: proton and neutron in the nucleus with relative mass 1, electron orbiting in shells with relative mass 1/1840; charges +1, 0, −1 (p. 53).
7. Mass number and atomic number; the notation with A above Z before the symbol; uranium-235 worked out from its protons and neutrons (p. 54).
8. Isotopes: same element, same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers; an oxygen isotope to unpack (p. 54).
9. Self-test: which particles are where, uncharged, negative, nucleons, lightest; write the symbol for a sodium isotope from its neutrons and electrons; how isotopes are alike and how they differ (p. 55).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **The particle table as one fixed grid** (p. 53). Particle, where it is, relative mass, relative charge. CCEA marks exactly these cells (March 2026 Higher Q1(a), seven marks; Summer 2023 and Summer 2024 Higher six-mark accounts). Making the table the centre of the note matches the exam.
- **Build the notation from the counts** (p. 54). Start from the numbers of protons and neutrons, add them for the mass number, and the proton number is the atomic number; then the reverse (Test Yourself Q2, from neutrons and electrons to the symbol). Both directions are examined.
- **"Nucleons"** (p. 55). The self-test uses the word for protons and neutrons together; it helps her say "mass number = number of nucleons".
- **Why the atom is mostly empty** (p. 53). The scattering story is a good "how do we know" aside for a curious student, but it is not on the specification; if used, keep it short and clearly optional.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Uranium-235** (p. 54): mass number from protons plus neutrons; the symbol written out.
- **Oxygen-18** (p. 54): protons, neutrons and electrons in the **nucleus** (answer: electrons zero). Correct for the nucleus, but our items should ask about the neutral atom, where electrons equal protons, and say which they mean.
- **Sodium** (p. 55): write the nucleus's symbol from 12 neutrons and 11 electrons (so 11 protons, mass number 23).

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Complete** the particle table (3–7; March 2026 Higher Q1(a): the neutron's mass given as zero was the commonest error; the 2021 Summer Higher scheme insists the proton's charge carries + and the electron's −).
- **Six-mark account** of the particles (Summer 2023 Higher Q1: the electron's relative mass given as −1, confusing mass with charge; Summer 2024 Higher Q3: the proton's charge written without its plus sign, which lost the mark). The Summer 2023 scheme accepts any electron mass between 1/1833 and 1/2000 (`docs/sources/papers/science/2023-Summer/P1-H-Physics-MS-29631.txt`).
- **Read** a nuclide symbol: protons, neutrons, electrons, mass number (1 each; March 2026 Higher Q1(b), beryllium; the Foundation report says many gave 4 neutrons; Summer 2026 Higher Q4, vanadium).
- **Define** isotopes (2; Summer 2024 Higher Q7(a): a first mark, which gates the second, for the protons being equal in number, then one for the neutrons differing; the report says some answered with atomic mass).
- **Which letter differs** between isotopes (1; 2019 March Higher: the mass number).

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Mass number and atomic number** (p. 54): match the glossary (protons and neutrons; protons).
- **Isotope** (p. 54): the book's definition matches the glossary and the 2024 scheme.
- **Relative mass of the electron** (p. 53): 1/1840, as in the glossary; the 2023 scheme's range shows nearby values are accepted.
- **Shells** (p. 53): the electron's location as "orbiting the nucleus in shells" matches the schemes ("shells/orbit").
- **Not on the specification**: the plum-pudding model, alpha scattering, Chadwick and electrons from hot wires (pp. 52–53). None appears in 1.5.1–1.5.4 or in any P1 paper we hold.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Protons and neutrons are nucleons; electrons are outside the nucleus (p. 55).
- Isotopes differ only in neutrons, so only the mass number changes (p. 55).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **The particle table** with signed charges and the electron's tiny mass; a non-example that writes "−1" for the electron's mass (cite the 2023 report).
2. **Reading and writing nuclide symbols**: protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z, electrons = Z for a neutral atom; See it, then Your turn on new nuclides (not uranium-235, oxygen-18 or sodium-23).
3. **Isotopes defined** with the two mark points, and a pair of isotopes to compare.
4. **Link** to `c1-atomic-structure` (the same particles from the chemistry side) and forward to `p1-radioactive-decay` (Z and A changing).
5. **Optional aside**: why we think the atom is mostly empty (scattering), marked as beyond the specification.
