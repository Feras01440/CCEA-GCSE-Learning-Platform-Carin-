**Ours.** **No note and no bundle yet.** The slug exists in our taxonomy and the P1 deck (`data/decks/science/P1.json`) has 8 flashcards for it; `packs/science/content/p1/` has no folder. This dossier is a briefing for the first author.

**Verdict.** **Thinner** (nothing authored). The book's two pages are clear and correct: mass against weight as scalar against vector, W = mg on Earth, the Moon and Mars, and a one-line derivation that every mass falls with the same acceleration, with the coin-and-paper tube. It never says the Foundation form of 1.2.10 (speed rises by 10 m/s each second) in its body, and it does not address the gram-to-kilogram conversion that costs the most marks on this topic.

## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 1.2: learning outcomes p. 14; "Mass and Weight" p. 21 (definitions, g as field strength and as free-fall acceleration, deep space, the Moon and planets, a worked example); "Falling Objects" p. 22 (a = g for every mass, the coin and the paper, the vacuum tube).

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. People use mass and weight as if they were the same; they are not (p. 21).
2. Mass: the amount of matter, in kilograms, a scalar (p. 21).
3. Weight: the gravitational pull of the Earth on the object, a force, a vector pointing down towards the Earth's centre (p. 21).
4. Near the Earth each kilogram weighs 10 N, so g = 10 N/kg, and W = m × g; the same g, read as 10 m/s², is also the acceleration of any freely falling object (p. 21).
5. Far from any planet g is almost zero; on the Moon or another planet the mass is unchanged but the weight differs (p. 21).
6. Worked example on three worlds (p. 21).
7. Combining F = ma with W = mg: the mass cancels, so every object falls with acceleration g (p. 22).
8. Why a sheet of paper still falls slower than a coin in air, and what happens when the air is pumped out (p. 22).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **The mass cancels** (p. 22). Weight is mg and the acceleration is the force over the mass, so mg ÷ m leaves g: a heavy object is pulled harder but is harder to accelerate by exactly the same factor. This is the reason behind 1.2.10's first bullet, and it is short enough for a Foundation note.
- **Air resistance as a fraction of the weight** (p. 22). The coin and the paper meet similar air friction, but for the paper it is a large share of a small weight, so its resultant is much smaller. A sharper explanation than "the paper is lighter".
- **Same mass everywhere, different weight** (p. 21). Three worlds, one mass, three weights; the third part asks for the mass on the third world, and the answer is the unchanged mass. A good trap item.
- **Mass scalar, weight vector** (p. 21). Ties this topic to the Higher vectors topic for free.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **One person on three worlds** (p. 21): weight on Earth (g = 10 N/kg), weight on the Moon (g = 1.6 N/kg), and the mass on Mars (unchanged). CCEA's P1 papers we hold set g = 10 N/kg on Earth and have not used other worlds, but 1.2.9 allows a given g.
- **No calculation for free fall** in the book; the Foundation statement "speed rises by 10 m/s every second" is never turned into numbers.

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Define** mass (1–2; March 2026 Foundation Q4(a): many could not, and some answered "volume").
- **Identify** weight as a force (1; same item).
- **Calculate** a weight from a mass in grams (3; Summer 2024 Higher Q1(c), a tyre of 8200 g; the report says the commonest mistake was not converting to kilograms).
- **Calculate** a mass from a weight (3; 2024 November Higher Q2(iii)).
- **Complete** the free-fall sentence: 10 m/s², the acceleration of free fall, the symbol g (3; 2020 March Higher Q8(a)).
- **State** the speed after a few seconds of free fall from rest, then the average speed over that time (1 + 3; 2022 Summer Higher Q2(b), an object released from rest with no air resistance), which links to `p1-speed-equations`.

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Mass** (p. 21): the amount of matter, in kilograms. Same as the specification (1.2.8) and CCEA's glossary.
- **Weight** (p. 21): how strongly gravity pulls on the object; the specification and glossary define it as a force, caused by gravity pulling on the object. Either earns the mark; teach the glossary's.
- **g** (p. 21): both 10 N/kg (gravitational field strength) and 10 m/s² (acceleration of free fall). The book links the two; the specification gives the N/kg form for W = mg (1.2.9) and the m/s² form as Higher (1.2.11).
- **"Friction"** is the book's and the specification's word for air resistance here (1.2.10: "air resistance (friction)"). Either word is accepted.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

1.2.11 (an object falling freely from rest accelerates at 10 m/s², called the acceleration of free fall, g) is Higher in the specification. The book states it on pp. 21–22 without marking it.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Mass does not change with place; weight does (p. 21).
- Without air, a coin and a sheet of paper land together (p. 22).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **Mass against weight**, defined in CCEA's words, with the unit of each and a Your turn on the Moon trap (new numbers, for example g = 1.6 N/kg with a 45 kg mass).
2. **W = mg with grams first converted to kilograms.** See it with the conversion as its own step (cite Summer 2024 Higher Q1(c)); twin where the weight is given and the mass asked.
3. **Speed rises by 10 m/s every second in free fall** (Foundation), then the Higher statement (acceleration of free fall g = 10 m/s²), with a short table of speed against time from rest and the average speed over that time.
4. **Why all masses fall together**: the cancelling argument and the coin-and-paper tube.
5. **Link forward** to `p1-newtons-laws` (weight first, then the upward force) and to `p1-kinetic-and-potential-energy` (g in mgh).
