**Book.** CCEA's Higher Tier eGuide to Physics Unit 1 of Double Award Science, © CCEA 2023 (git-ignored under `docs/sources/textbooks/`). Page numbers are the book's printed numbers; the PDF page is the printed number plus 4.
**Ours.** **No note and no bundle yet.** The slug exists in our taxonomy (difficulty 4) and the P1 deck (`data/decks/science/P1.json`) has 13 flashcards for it; `packs/science/content/p1/` has no folder. This dossier is a briefing for the first author.
**Spec.** P1 1.4.17, 1.4.18, 1.4.19 (all Foundation).
**Verdict.** **Thinner** (nothing authored). This is one of the book's best pages: eight worked examples that climb from a direct mgh to a falling object whose speed comes from energy conservation (solved two ways) and a rising ball that has both kinds of energy at once. That last type is exactly what CCEA set in Summer 2024 (a golf ball at its highest point still moving) and the report says it caught many. The book never puts an energy loss to friction into a calculation, which March 2026 and 2019 both did.

## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 1.4: learning outcomes p. 39; "Gravitational Potential Energy" pp. 45–46 (meaning, equation, four worked examples); "Kinetic Energy" pp. 46–47 (meaning, equation, four worked examples, a note on the most challenging type).

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Lifting something does work against gravity; more mass or more height means more work, stored as gravitational potential energy (p. 45).
2. Ep = m × g × h; when the object falls the store becomes kinetic energy, and on landing sound and heat (p. 45).
3. Four potential-energy examples: grams and centimetres converted; the heat and sound on landing equal the energy at the start; a reservoir in standard form; a height from a landing kinetic energy (pp. 45–46).
4. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion; Ek = ½mv²; the squared speed makes it harder (p. 46).
5. Four kinetic-energy examples: a speed in km/h converted to m/s; a mass from the energy in MJ; a dropped object's landing speed by two methods; a rising ball part-way up (pp. 46–47).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **Two routes to v** (p. 47). Either set ½mv² equal to the energy and solve for v², then take the square root; or learn v = √(2Ek/m) and substitute. The book admits the second adds one more formula to memorise, in exchange for easier arithmetic. For her, the first route (from the equation she must recall) is safer; show the second as a check.
- **Energy shared between two stores** (p. 47). A ball rising after a kick: the total stays the same, so at any height Ep + Ek equals the starting kinetic energy. The book flags it as the hardest type. CCEA's Summer 2024 Higher Q9 is this shape in reverse (a golf ball at its highest point with both 11.25 J of potential and 28.75 J of kinetic energy; total 40 J; landing speed 42.2 m/s to one decimal place: `docs/sources/papers/science/2024-Summer/P1-H-Physics-MS-47741.txt`). The report says the commonest error was not adding the two to get the total.
- **The landing energy goes somewhere** (p. 45). The heat and sound produced on landing equal the starting potential energy. A clear conservation link.
- **Say the assumption** (p. 47). The falling-object answer notes that air resistance would make the real speed smaller. Questions often say "assume no energy losses"; she should know what that removes.
- **Change units first** (pp. 45–46). Grams to kilograms, centimetres to metres, km/h to m/s (dividing by 3.6, shown as 1000 m over 3600 s). The Summer 2025 Higher report (Q8) names mass left in grams as the commonest kinetic-energy error.

## 4. Diagrams and photographs

None for this topic. Our note should draw a falling or rising object with energy bars (potential falling, kinetic rising, total constant), and a version with a loss bar for friction.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Ep of a small mass** (p. 45): grams and centimetres converted first.
- **Heat and sound on landing** (p. 45): equal to the starting Ep.
- **Reservoir** (p. 45): a very large Ep in standard form.
- **Height from a landing energy** (p. 46): Ep = Ek, solved for h.
- **Car** (p. 46): a speed in km/h converted, then a "show that" for Ek in kJ.
- **Satellite** (p. 46): a mass from Ek in MJ and a speed.
- **Dropped from a tall tower** (p. 47): landing speed from mgh = ½mv², both routes (v² = 5600, v ≈ 75 m/s).
- **Kicked ball** (p. 47): Ep + Ek constant; at a stated Ek, find the height and the speed.

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Calculate** Ek, with the mass in grams (3–4; Summer 2025 Higher Q8(i)–(ii); Summer 2025 Foundation Q5: a "surprising lack" of recall of the equation).
- **Calculate** a height or a mass from Ep, often to one decimal place (3–4; Summer 2024 Higher Q9(i); Summer 2026 Higher Q9(i), a diver's mass; Summer 2024 Foundation Q5: most could not recall mgh; Summer 2024 Higher report: g left out).
- **Energy with two stores** (5; Summer 2024 Higher Q9(ii), above).
- **Energy with a loss** (8; March 2026 Higher Q8: a dropped 8 kg object lands at 5 m/s having lost 10 J to friction; the starting Ep is the landing Ek plus the loss, 110 J, and the height is 1.4 m to one decimal place; the report says many ignored or subtracted the 10 J. 2019 Summer Higher Q7(a): a child on a slide, the height from the kinetic energy at the bottom plus 500 J lost to friction).
- **Final velocity, then Ek** (Summer 2026 Higher Q9(ii): goggles dropped from rest, final velocity from the average velocity, then the gain in kinetic energy), joining this topic to `p1-speed-equations`.

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Kinetic energy** (p. 46): the energy an object has because it moves; the glossary says "due to its motion". Same meaning.
- **Gravitational potential energy** (p. 45): stored by lifting against gravity; the specification says "because of its position above the ground" and the glossary "due to its height above the earth's surface". Teach the glossary's phrase.
- **g** is 10 N/kg here (1.4.19), as the book uses throughout.
- **"Vertical height"**: the specification says h is the vertical height; the book does not stress it. Our note should, because slopes and stairs are common contexts.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

None (all Foundation). The multi-store and loss problems appear on the Higher paper.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Convert grams, centimetres and km/h before substituting (pp. 45–46).
- Square the speed (p. 46).
- When neither energy is zero, use the total, which stays the same (p. 47).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **Ek = ½mv² with the mass converted from grams**, See it then Your turn; a non-example with the mass left in grams (cite the 2025 report).
2. **Ep = mgh with g included and h vertical**, to one decimal place when asked.
3. **Energy conservation for a falling object**: mgh at the top equals ½mv² at the bottom; solve for v by squaring and rooting.
4. **Losses**: the starting Ep equals the final Ek plus the energy lost to friction (March 2026 and 2019 Summer shapes), on our own numbers.
5. **Two stores at once**: total energy at the top is Ep + Ek (Summer 2024 shape), on our own numbers.
6. **Why v² matters**: double the speed, four times the energy (one line and a gate), which the book never says.

## 11. Anything in our material the book suggests is wrong or misleading

One deck trap card misreads its citation: it describes a ball that "already has 10 J of kinetic energy at the top", citing the March 2026 Higher report, but the March 2026 question (Q8) is about 10 J **lost to friction** during the fall, which must be added to the landing kinetic energy to find the starting potential energy (`docs/sources/papers/science/2026-March/P1-H-Physics-MS-68446.txt`). The card's arithmetic habit (do not drop the 10 J) is right; its physics story should match the question, or it should cite the Summer 2024 golf-ball item, which really does have kinetic energy at the top.
