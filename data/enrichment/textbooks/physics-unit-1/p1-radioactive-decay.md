# p1-radioactive-decay: textbook dossier

**Book.** CCEA's Higher Tier eGuide to Physics Unit 1 of Double Award Science, © CCEA 2023 (git-ignored under `docs/sources/textbooks/`). Page numbers are the book's printed numbers; the PDF page is the printed number plus 4.
**Ours.** **No note and no bundle yet.** The slug exists in our taxonomy (difficulty 4) and the P1 deck (`data/decks/science/P1.json`) has 13 flashcards for it; `packs/science/content/p1/` has no folder. This dossier is a briefing for the first author.
**Spec.** P1 1.5.5, 1.5.6, 1.5.8 (Foundation) and 1.5.7 (Higher: decay equations).
**Verdict.** **Thinner** (nothing authored). The book defines random and spontaneous, names the three radiations, works one equation of each kind, explains beta decay as a neutron becoming a proton, and ends with a summary table of all three radiations' properties. But this is its least reliable section: its gamma equation changes the mass number, it gives the beta particle two different relative masses, it puts beta at about a tenth of light speed, and it says gamma emission changes nothing in the nucleus while also explaining it as the loss of excess energy. Authors should take every fact here from the specification and the schemes, using the book only for its structure.

## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 1.5: learning outcomes pp. 50–51; "Radioactivity" pp. 55–57 (random and spontaneous; alpha, beta and gamma described; an equation for each; what happens in beta decay; two worked examples; a summary table of properties).

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Some nuclei are unstable and break up randomly and spontaneously; the two words defined (p. 55).
2. The three emissions: alpha (two protons and two neutrons, relative mass 4, charge +2, with its symbol), beta (a fast electron, charge −1, with its symbol), gamma (an electromagnetic wave with no charge or mass) (p. 55).
3. Alpha decay as an equation, parent to daughter, the atomic number down 2 and the mass number down 4 (p. 55).
4. Beta decay as an equation, the atomic number up 1 and the mass number unchanged (pp. 55–56).
5. Gamma emission after alpha or beta decay leaves a nucleus with excess energy; no change in either number (p. 56).
6. In every equation the top numbers balance and the bottom numbers balance (p. 56).
7. Why a nucleus can emit an electron: a neutron becomes a proton and an electron (p. 56).
8. Two worked examples: identify the particle from its charge, then fill in the missing numbers (pp. 56–57).
9. A table of nature, relative charge, relative mass, speed, ionising effect and penetration for all three (p. 57).

## 3. Explanations and devices worth recreating (our own words)

- **Random and spontaneous, each with a meaning** (p. 55). Random: no way to say which nucleus goes next or when. Spontaneous: nothing done to the sample starts or stops it. (The book's own wording for spontaneous, "with or without human intervention", is loose; the deck's version is clearer.)
- **Identify the particle from its charge first** (pp. 56–57). Negative means beta, +2 means alpha; then the missing numbers follow by balancing. A clean two-step method CCEA rewards.
- **Why beta raises the atomic number** (p. 56). A neutron turns into a proton and an electron; the electron leaves, the extra proton stays, so Z goes up by 1 and A (the nucleon count) is unchanged. Not required by the specification, but it makes the beta rule a reason rather than a rule, which is what CCEA's Summer 2025 report says candidates got wrong (Higher Q6).
- **Gamma as a nucleus shedding energy** (p. 56). After alpha or beta emission the nucleus can be left with spare energy and releases it as gamma, so neither number changes.
- **One table for all three** (p. 57). Laid out properly (with correct entries, section 7), the comparison table is the best single revision object for 1.5.6 and 1.5.8.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Molybdenum to technetium** (p. 56): a negatively charged particle, so beta; the daughter's numbers by balancing.
- **Thorium to radium** (p. 57): a particle with charge +2, so alpha; the missing atomic and mass numbers.

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Why** do some nuclei disintegrate? (1; 2018 March and 2018 November Higher: they are unstable; 2023 March Higher Q2(b)).
- **Name** the three emissions; **identify** each from its nature (1–3; 2018 Summer and 2018 November Higher; Summer 2025 Foundation Q7(a)).
- **Penetration**: which is stopped by paper, which passes through thick lead (1–2; 2022 November Higher; March 2026 Higher Q3(b)); the 2022 March Higher six-mark account wanted gamma, lead, alpha and a few centimetres of air (`docs/sources/papers/science/2022-March/P1-H-Physics-MS-19606.txt`).
- **Complete** decay equations (Higher, 2–4; Summer 2025 Higher Q6: carbon-14 to nitrogen-14 by beta, polonium-212 to lead-208 by alpha; the report says beta was the hardest part). The deck's beta example (carbon-14) is the same nuclide CCEA used, so our note's practice should use others.

## 7. Definitions and wording, and book errors

- **Alpha, beta, gamma** (p. 55): match the specification's 1.5.6 and the glossary (helium nucleus of two protons and two neutrons; fast electron; high-energy electromagnetic wave).
- **Book error: the gamma equation** (p. 56). It shows uranium-233 becoming uranium-235 with a gamma ray; the mass number must stay the same, as the sentence below it says. Correctly, the same nuclide appears on both sides (the specification's form: X to X plus γ).
- **Book contradiction: the beta particle's mass** (pp. 55, 57). The text gives its relative mass as 0, the table as 1/1840. In decay equations its mass number is written 0 (specification 1.5.7); as a particle its relative mass is about 1/1840 (the electron's). Teach both, saying which is which.
- **Book error: beta speed** (p. 57). The table puts beta at roughly one tenth of light speed, the same as alpha. Beta particles are typically far faster (a large fraction of light speed). Speed is not on the specification; drop the row.
- **Book contradiction: gamma and the nucleus** (pp. 55–56). The text says gamma emission causes no change within the nucleus, then explains it as the nucleus losing excess energy. The second is right: the nucleus loses energy, though A and Z do not change.
- **Penetration wording** (p. 57): the book says a few mm of aluminium stop beta and that gamma is never stopped completely. The specification (1.5.8) says beta is stopped by a thin sheet of aluminium (or several metres of air) and gamma can be blocked by lead; the 2022 March scheme credits "lead" for gamma. For marking, use the specification's words; "thick lead greatly reduces gamma" is a fair extension, but she should not write that nothing stops it.
- **Ionising order** (p. 57): alpha most, gamma least; correct, and needed for `p1-background-radiation-dangers-safety`.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

Decay equations (1.5.7) are Higher in the specification. The book teaches them to everyone without marking the tier.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Top numbers balance, bottom numbers balance (p. 56).
- Alpha: A down 4, Z down 2; beta: A unchanged, Z up 1; gamma: neither changes (pp. 55–56).
- Decide the particle from its charge before filling in numbers (pp. 56–57).

## 10. GAPS: what our first note must teach, ranked by exam weight

1. **The three radiations' nature and penetration** in the specification's words, with an absorber figure (paper, thin aluminium, thick lead; and the distances in air).
2. **Random and spontaneous**, unstable nuclei, and the glossary's definition of radioactive.
3. **Decay equations** (Higher): explain balancing; See it on a new alpha and a new beta decay; Your turn with the particle unnamed (identify it from the numbers); a gamma example with identical nuclides each side.
4. **Why beta raises Z** (a neutron becomes a proton), as the reason behind the rule.
5. **Ionising strength** as the link to the dangers topic.
