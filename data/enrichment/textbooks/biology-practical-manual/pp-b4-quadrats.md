## 2. The book's method, in order (our words)

1. Counting every organism is rarely possible, so an area is sampled: quadrats are laid, each species inside is counted or its cover estimated, and the counts are then set beside measurements of physical or living factors to look for links (p. 15).
2. Two ways to place quadrats (p. 15). Where conditions **change across** the site (shore from the water's edge inland; wood into grassland), lay a tape across the change and put quadrats at intervals along it: a belt transect. Where the site is **the same throughout**, decide its size first, lay two tapes at right angles as axes and place each quadrat at a random coordinate, read like a point on a graph (for example 1 m along and 4 m up).
3. The worked example is a belt transect from the edge of a wood into grassland (pp. 15–16): tape from the wood's edge outwards; quadrat at 0 m; count the daisies; in the same quadrat, measure light, soil moisture and soil pH with probes or a light meter; move along to 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 m and repeat.
4. Light is recorded on a three-step scale (low, medium, high) if the teacher says so (p. 16).
5. Questions: describe the trend in daisies; give two reasons for it from the table; give the term for the physical factors (p. 17).

## 3. Where the book is better than our note

- **Every count is paired with the conditions in the same quadrat** (p. 16, step 4). That is what lets a trend be explained from data rather than from general knowledge. Our transect example pairs plantain with light alone.
- **Choosing the reasons from the table.** The book's table is blank, but CCEA's 2021 paper filled the same table and its scheme credits two reasons, more light and more soil moisture, because those columns rise along with the daisies; in that paper the pH column barely changes (6.7 to 6.8), so it cannot be a reason. A distractor column that does not change is a teaching device our notes lack. CCEA's 2024 report makes the same point from the other side: a factor named without its direction ("pH" instead of "lower pH inland") was not credited.
- **The trend's shape in words.** The 2021 scheme's trend is "increases then levels off": two features of the line, not one. Our trend answers state a single direction.
- **The rule for choosing a method** is stated as a pair (changes across the site → transect; the same throughout → random), and the random coordinate is explained by the graph-plotting picture. Our note has both methods but frames the transect as "a different question" rather than as the answer to a site that changes.
- **Instruments for three abiotic factors at once**: a light meter or light probe, a pH probe, a soil moisture probe (p. 15). Our note names a pH meter, light meter, thermometer and anemometer; add the moisture probe.
- **A qualitative scale is acceptable data** (light as low, medium or high, p. 16): a useful link to Unit 7 tables (arbitrary units, categories).

## 4. Where the book is worse, and what it leaves out

- **No key, no identification.** The book counts "daisies" without saying how she knows which plants are daisies. CCEA's six-mark fieldwork schemes (2019 and 2025, both tiers) list "use a key" and "identify the plants" as indicative points, and the 2025 report says few candidates mentioned the key. Our note's key figure and gate are better.
- **One transect, no repeats.** CCEA 2022 Booklet B Q1(a)(iv) (both tiers) asks how to improve the reliability of a shore transect and credits "repeat". The book never repeats a line or takes a mean.
- **No population estimate.** The scaling-up calculation (mean per quadrat × field area ÷ quadrat area), the 2023 report's warning about candidates who tried an over-complicated calculation and percentage cover with a gridded quadrat are all ours, not the book's.
- **No quadrat size**, and a metre stick in the apparatus list that the method never uses.
- **Table headings disagree with the instruction**: light is headed in arbitrary units while step 4's note asks for low, medium or high (p. 16). In CCEA's 2021 paper the light column did hold low, medium and high.
- **No wind speed**, although CCEA's transects on shores and dunes use it (2022 and 2024 Booklet B; the 2024 report: many could not name what an anemometer measures).

## 8. Definitions and wording

- **Belt transect** (p. 15): quadrats at intervals along a line laid across a change. **Random sampling**: quadrats at random coordinates where the site is uniform. Same as 1.7.3 and our note.
- **Physical factors** is the book's phrase; the specification and every scheme use **abiotic**. Teach abiotic as the answer.
- **Book slips, not to copy:** "plants and or animals" in the heading (p. 15); a garbled stem in Question 2 ("for give", p. 17); "meters" for metres throughout.

## 10. Pitfalls and precautions the book builds in

- Measure the abiotic factors **in the same quadrat** as the count (p. 16).
- Decide the size of the site **before** fieldwork, for random coordinates (p. 15).
- Care in the habitat (p. 15), the only safety line.

## 11. GAPS in our note, ranked

1. **A multi-factor transect table with a distractor column.** Add a data Your turn on a wood-to-grass transect (our own numbers): daisies rising then levelling, light and moisture rising, pH flat. She picks the two factors that explain the trend, with an option note for pH ("it hardly changes, so it cannot explain a change"). Then a gate on wording: "more light further from the wood", not "light".
2. **Trend with two features.** In b1-fieldwork-sampling item 0005 and the note's transect paragraph, model a trend that rises then levels, and say both parts.
3. **The choice rule as a pair.** One callout: conditions change across the site, transect; the site is uniform, random sampling. Add the soil moisture probe to the instruments list.
4. **Light on a category scale.** One sentence in u7-analysing or here: low, medium and high is ordinal data and is recorded as words in the table.
