## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. New cells need a full set of chromosomes so they can make their own proteins (p. 41).
2. Mitosis: for growth, replacing worn-out cells and repairing tissue; two cells, genetically identical to the parent and to each other (p. 41).
3. Meiosis happens only in ovaries and testes and makes gametes (p. 41).
4. Mitosis in detail: the DNA is copied first so each new cell ends up with the normal number; a 46 → 46 + 46 summary picture (p. 41).
5. The copied chromosomes line up across the middle and are pulled apart, giving two daughter cells; a four-chromosome picture of the steps (p. 42).
6. Meiosis: two divisions, four gametes, each with half the parent's genetic material, all different (p. 44).
7. A summary picture of meiosis in the testes ending in four sperm of 23 chromosomes, then a detailed four-chromosome version (p. 44).
8. (Bold) Each pair lines up independently of every other pair, so the gametes get different mixtures: independent assortment (p. 45).
9. A comparison table and two self-tests that ask her to **draw** (pp. 45–46).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **Copy, line up, separate** (pp. 41–42). The book pictures mitosis as three moves: the DNA is copied, the copies line up across the middle, and they are pulled apart into two cells. Our note says the chromosomes are "duplicated exactly" and the cell divides once, but has no picture of the line-up, which is what makes "identical" visible.
- **Why cells need chromosomes at all** (p. 41): without them a new cell could not make its proteins. A one-line motivation our note lacks.
- **Follow the style you are given** (pp. 42, 45 tips). Diagrams are drawn in different ways; copy the question's style of chromosome when completing one. Useful advice for a drawing task.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Stages 1–3** (p. 43): describe two changes between the first and second pictures (DNA copied; chromosomes lined up across the middle); compare the final cells with the parent and with each other (identical to both).
- **Test Yourself 2.4.3** (p. 46): name the chemical of chromosomes (DNA); draw the cells mitosis makes from the pictured cell.
- **Test Yourself 2.4.4** (p. 46): one place meiosis happens; how many haploid cells one cell makes (four); draw the parent cell of a given gamete.
- The comparison table (p. 45) is a ready-made "complete the table" shape: number of cells, chromosomes in each (human), diploid or haploid, identical or different, whether it happens in testes and ovaries, number of divisions.
- CCEA 2023 Higher Q9 set the drawing shapes for seven marks (`docs/sources/papers/science/2023-Summer/B2-H-Biology-MS-29143.txt`): (a) products of mitosis [3] for two cells, four chromosomes in each, the parent's combination in each; (b)(i) products of meiosis [4] for four cells, two chromosomes in each, one bent and one straight in each, and the shaded and clear chromosomes shared out between the four; (b)(ii) the role of independent assortment [1]: variation.

## 6. Practice question types

- **Compare** the products with the parent and each other (1–2).

## 7. Definitions and wording, and a book error

- **Mitosis** and **meiosis**: same meanings as ours; the book adds **diploid** (cells with pairs) and **haploid** (one of each) in its table. Our note uses haploid but never diploid in this topic.
- **Independent assortment** (p. 45, bold): each pair lines up independently of the others, giving variation among gametes. Same as ours; our note's "Higher tier" label matches the specification's bold clause.
- **Book error: the chromosome count after the first division.** The p. 44 summary labels each of the two cells after the first meiotic division "46 chromosomes". After the first division each cell holds one member of every pair, 23 chromosomes (each still copied). Our note never gives an intermediate count, which is right: the specification excludes the stages, and she only needs 46 in and 23 out.
- **A nuance to leave alone.** The specification (and the book) says the four cells from one meiosis are all genetically different. Strictly, with no crossing over, one cell's four products come as two matching pairs, and crossing over (which CCEA excludes) is what makes all four differ. Our note follows the specification's statement; that is correct for the exam. Authors should not add further claims about which process makes all four differ.

## 11. Anything in our note the book suggests is wrong or misleading

Nothing that the book contradicts. One internal inconsistency noticed while reading (not from the book): section 5's examiner callout is titled "Pick the right four, not just four", but the section's own six nuclei contain only **three** that meiosis could make ((i), (iii), (vi)). With a long pair and a short pair there are exactly four possible haploid combinations, and CCEA 2025 Higher Q9 asked for four. Either redraw the six nuclei so that all four possible combinations appear, or retitle the callout.
