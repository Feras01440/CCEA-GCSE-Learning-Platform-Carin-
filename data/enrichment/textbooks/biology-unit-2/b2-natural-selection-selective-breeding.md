## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Individuals differ in phenotype; when the environment changes, they compete for resources; those whose phenotype suits the change survive and the rest die; the survivors are more likely to breed and pass on their genes: natural selection (p. 70).
2. A worked example on a prey animal whose coat changes colour with the seasons, and a mutation that times the change differently as the climate warms (pp. 70–71).
3. (Bold) Antibiotic resistance as the second example: a mutated gene lets some bacteria survive an antibiotic; they pass it on; MRSA (p. 72).
4. (Bold) Put five statements about a bacterial population in order (p. 72).
5. (Bold) Evolution: selection continuing over many generations may produce a new species; Darwin's 1859 book and voyages; the Galapagos finches, whose beaks diversified as they exploited different foods (p. 73).
6. Selective breeding as five repeated steps; examples in wheat, cattle and sheep; the aims people breed for (p. 74).
7. A sorting task: which of five examples are natural selection, and how to tell (p. 74).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **A mismatch in timing, not a new predator** (pp. 70–71). An animal whose coat turns from white to brown in spring is camouflaged only while its coat matches the ground. If warming makes the snow go earlier, individuals carrying a mutation for an earlier change are the camouflaged ones, are eaten less, breed more and pass the gene on, so their share of the population rises. Every example in our note is a change in *what* the environment is (soot, poison, drought); none is a change in *when*. CCEA sets unfamiliar contexts every year (dog whelks 2023, garter snakes 2024), and this one exercises the same chain from a new angle.
- **A real evolution story** (p. 73). Finches that reached islands short of seeds; those with beaks able to use other foods (cactus, insects) survived; over many generations several species arose, each with a beak suited to its food. Our beak-depth curve teaches the same selection but is unnamed. The history (Darwin, 1859) is not on the specification.
- **Breeding can harm the animal** (p. 74). One of the book's sorting examples is dogs bred for very flat faces that then struggle to breathe. Our note presents selective breeding only as improvement; a non-example of "desirable for the animal" is worth one line.
- **Breeding for more offspring** (p. 74). The book's list of aims adds more offspring (sheep bred to have more lambs) to the specification's appearance, food quantity or quality, and disease resistance.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Coat colour and climate** (pp. 70–71): (1) suggest why a white winter coat helps (hidden from the predator on snow); (2) describe and explain how earlier snow melt changes the number carrying the early-brown mutation (it rises: camouflaged sooner, eaten less, more survive, reproduce, pass the gene on).
- **Ordering a bacterial population** (p. 72): variation; some have genes for resistance; the resistant survive to reproduce; they pass their genes on; resistant numbers rise over time.
- **Sorting five examples** (p. 74, Test Yourself 2.5.1): pick the natural-selection ones (the coat-colour animal and desert trees with fewer stomata); explain the difference (people choose in selective breeding; the environment decides in natural selection).
- No calculations or graphs. Ours includes dish counts, a milk-yield chart and a two-population graph; CCEA's maths statement for this section expects population data read against changing conditions, which ours serves and the book does not.

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Natural selection** (p. 70): individuals whose phenotype suits the conditions have a better chance of living to breed, so their genes reach the next generation more often. Same as ours.
- **Absolute against probable wording.** The book says the unsuited individuals "will die" and the suited "will survive" before softening to "more likely". Our note says "more likely" throughout, which is the better science; CCEA's 2024 Higher scheme accepts "the snakes that were not resistant died" (`docs/sources/papers/science/2024-Summer/B2-H-Biology-MS-47961.txt`), so either wording earns the mark. Keep ours.
- **Evolution** (p. 73, bold): continuing selection causing gradual change that may produce a new species. Same as ours.
- **Tier of antibiotic resistance.** The book prints its resistance passage in bold (Higher), but the specification names antibiotic resistance as an example inside the Foundation bullet of 2.5.3 (the superbug detail, 2.6.7, is the Higher part). Our note treats the resistance chain as open to both tiers, which matches the specification.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Selective breeding is done by **people**; natural selection by the **environment** (p. 74, answer p. 105).
- Resistance comes from a **mutation** that is **passed on** (p. 72).
