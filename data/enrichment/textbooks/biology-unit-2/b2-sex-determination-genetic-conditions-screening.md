## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. 23 pairs in most human cells; one pair decides sex; the X is longer than the Y; XX female, XY male (p. 54).
2. A photograph of one baby's chromosomes sorted into pairs, identified as male by its X and Y (p. 55).
3. Some conditions come from particular alleles, recessive or dominant; families are drawn as pedigree diagrams (p. 55).
4. Cystic fibrosis: what it does (thick mucus in the lungs, harder breathing, digestion affected), then a carriers' pedigree and a square (pp. 55–56).
5. Huntington's disease: dominant, slowly damages the nervous system; then a dominant pedigree (polydactyly) worked with five questions (pp. 57–58).
6. (Higher) Haemophilia: the Y is shorter, so some X alleles have no partner; a recessive allele on the X makes a sex-linked condition, commoner in males, with carrier females; notation with superscripts; a pedigree and a square (pp. 58–60).
7. Down's syndrome: not an allele but an extra chromosome, from a meiosis error giving a gamete of 24; the zygote has 47 and every cell copies it; three copies of chromosome 21 (p. 60).
8. Screening: a genetic counsellor advises families with a history; for Down's, the risk rises with the mother's age; a blood test first, then amniocentesis if needed, with its small miscarriage risk; the dilemma that follows a positive result; insurance (pp. 61–62).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **What the conditions do** (pp. 55, 57). Cystic fibrosis: more mucus in the lungs, so breathing is hard, and digestion can suffer. Huntington's disease: gradual damage to the nervous system. Our note names each condition's cause and genotypes but never says what it does to a person, which leaves an "explain the effect" question unanswerable.
- **Reasoning from a dominant pedigree** (pp. 57–58). Two affected parents who have an unaffected child must both be heterozygous; an unaffected couple cannot have an affected child. Our note's only pedigree is recessive (cystic fibrosis). The same gap is logged in `b2-monohybrid-genetics`; one section can serve both notes.
- **Why Down's syndrome happens and who is tested** (pp. 60, 62). Something goes wrong in meiosis, so one gamete carries an extra chromosome; the chance rises as the mother gets older, which is why older mothers are offered tests. Our note has the 24 + 23 = 47 arithmetic but not the meiosis error or the age link.
- **The order of testing** (p. 62). The blood test is offered first because it carries no risk; amniocentesis follows only if needed, because it carries a small risk of miscarriage. Our note compares the two tests well but does not give the order or the reason for it.
- **The genetic counsellor** (p. 61). A family with a history of a condition can be tested for the allele and advised of the chances. Our note does not name the counsellor's role, which sits naturally under "who decides who is tested".
- **Why a son escapes his father's haemophilia, from the son's side** (p. 60, answer p. 104). A son's X comes from his mother, so an unaffected mother gives an unaffected son. Our note states the father-to-son rule; the mother-to-son rule is the other half.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Cystic fibrosis** (p. 56): draw the square for two carrier parents and ring the affected genotype.
- **Polydactyly** (pp. 57–58): five parts: an unaffected child's genotype and term; the possible genotypes of an affected sibling; the parents' square; the chance for an unaffected couple (zero).
- **Haemophilia** (pp. 59–60, Higher): parents' genotypes from the pedigree; their square; then Test Yourself 2.4.7: why the named son is unaffected, his square with a carrier partner, and the percentage chance of an affected child (25 %).
- **Down's syndrome** (p. 61): fill the gamete and zygote numbers (24 and 47). CCEA 2023 Foundation Q3(b) set exactly this for two marks (`docs/sources/papers/science/2023-Summer/B2-F-Biology-MS-29142.txt`), and Q3(c) gave three marks for describing amniocentesis (needle or syringe; fluid removed; foetal cells in it, or care over where the needle goes).

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Sex chromosomes** (p. 54): the one pair that decides sex. Same as ours.
- **Carrier** (p. 56 key): one copy of the recessive allele, no symptoms. Same as ours.
- **Sex-linked** (p. 58, Higher): caused by an allele on the X with no partner on the Y, so males are more often affected and females can be carriers. Same as ours.
- **Colour blindness** (p. 59) is given as a second sex-linked example; it is not on the specification. Leave it out or mark it as not on spec.
- **Screening accuracy.** The book calls the maternal blood test "not very accurate"; our note says it gives "a chance, not a certain answer". Compatible; ours is the better wording.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

Printed in bold: haemophilia and sex linkage, the notation, the pedigree, its answers and Test Yourself 2.4.7 (pp. 58–60). Our note marks haemophilia Higher.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Use the letters the question gives; books differ (p. 56).
- Both parents of an affected child with a dominant condition must carry at least one dominant allele (p. 58, hint).
- Write sex-linked genotypes with the X and a superscript allele; the Y carries none (p. 59).

## 10. GAPS in our note, ranked

1. **No effects of the conditions.** Add one line each to the three-conditions section (cystic fibrosis: sticky mucus in the lungs and digestive system, so breathing and digestion suffer; Huntington's disease: gradual damage to the nervous system; haemophilia: blood does not clot properly) and a Your turn "describe one effect of cystic fibrosis".
2. **No dominant pedigree.** Add a `variant` section with a See it on our own Huntington's pedigree: two affected parents with an unaffected child are both heterozygous; an unaffected couple has a zero chance; Your turn on a new family. (Shared with `b2-monohybrid-genetics`.)
3. **Down's syndrome lacks its cause and its risk factor.** Add: an error in meiosis leaves one gamete with an extra chromosome (its partner gamete has one too few); the risk rises with the mother's age, which is why older mothers are offered screening.
4. **Screening lacks its order and the counsellor.** Add: blood test first (no risk, gives a chance), amniocentesis only if needed (definite, small miscarriage risk); a genetic counsellor explains the chances to families with a history.
5. **The mother-to-son rule for haemophilia.** Add a Higher Your turn: why is the son of an unaffected mother unaffected, whatever his father has?
