## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. A pair of chromosomes, one from each parent, carrying the same gene (eye colour) at the same place, one allele dominant (B) and one recessive (b) (p. 47).
2. With one of each, the dominant one shows; genotype is the pair of letters; phenotype is the appearance (p. 47).
3. Homozygous and heterozygous; the three genotypes and their names in a table (pp. 47–48).
4. Gametes carry one allele; fertilisation gives the zygote two, one from each parent; mitosis copies both into every body cell; diploid against haploid (p. 48).
5. The Punnett square: one parent's gametes down the side, the other's across the top, one allele per outer box; the four inner boxes are the possible offspring (pp. 48–49).
6. Three crosses in turn (both homozygous dominant; homozygous dominant with heterozygous; both heterozygous), ending in the 3 : 1 (pp. 50–51).
7. Results as ratios, probabilities, percentages or genotype frequencies (p. 51).
8. A plant worked example with a 1 : 1 answer (pp. 51–52).
9. (Higher) The test cross: cross the unknown with a homozygous recessive; draw **both** possible squares; the offspring decide (pp. 52–53).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **Like noughts and crosses** (p. 48). The book likens filling a Punnett square to the grid game, with one letter per outer box. A light touch, but it fixes the layout: letters outside, pairs inside.
- **Draw both squares whenever a dominant-looking parent is unknown** (pp. 52–53). Not only in a test cross: the p. 52 self-test crosses a hornless (dominant) bull of unknown genotype with a horned cow and asks for **two** squares, one for each possible bull. Our note draws the two outcomes only inside the test-cross section.
- **Every body cell carries both alleles** (p. 48). The zygote's two alleles are copied into every cell by mitosis. Our note says a gamete carries one allele; it never closes the loop back to body cells.
- **A pedigree built on a dominant allele** (pp. 57–58, taught under genetic conditions with polydactyly). Two affected parents with an unaffected child must both be heterozygous; two unaffected parents cannot have an affected child (probability zero). Our pedigree section teaches only the recessive case ("start with the shaded symbols: every one of them is bb"), which gives the wrong first step when the shaded phenotype is the dominant one.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Three eye-colour crosses** (pp. 50–51): all offspring brown; all brown but two genotypes; 3 : 1.
- **Pea height** (pp. 51–52): genotype of a dwarf plant; heterozygous tall × dwarf in a square; phenotypes 1 : 1.
- **Hornless cattle** (p. 52): the genotypes a hornless animal could have; a hornless bull × a horned cow drawn as two squares (all offspring hornless, or half and half).
- **Smooth and wrinkled peas** (p. 53, Higher): the test cross drawn both ways.
- **Purple and white flowers** (p. 54, Higher): show the test cross; one white offspring appears, so the purple parent was heterozygous.
- **Polydactyly** (pp. 57–58): genotype and term for an unaffected daughter of two affected parents; the possible genotypes of an affected sibling (homozygous dominant or heterozygous); the square for the affected parents; the chance of an affected child for two unaffected parents (none).

## 6. Practice question types

- **Give** the genotype (1); **give the term** for it (1).
- **Use a Punnett square** for the offspring genotypes (2–3).
- **Give** the phenotypes and their ratio (1–2).
- **Draw two Punnett squares**, one per possible parent genotype (Higher, 2–4).
- **What is the probability** that a child has the condition (1).

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Genotype** (p. 47): the letters for the alleles a person carries for one gene. **Phenotype**: the outward appearance for that characteristic. Same as ours.
- **Homozygous / heterozygous** (p. 47): two alleles the same / different. Same as ours; the book's table gives homozygous dominant, heterozygous, homozygous recessive, the terms CCEA 2025 Foundation found few candidates could write (our note's examiner evidence).
- **Diploid / haploid** (p. 48): pairs of chromosomes / one of each. Not in our note for this topic.
- **Test cross** (p. 52): the unknown crossed with a homozygous recessive. Same as ours.
- Letters differ between books and papers; use the letters the question gives (p. 56 tip).

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

Printed in bold: the test (back) cross and its exam tip (pp. 52–54) and the introduction of pedigree diagrams (p. 54). The pedigree worked examples on pp. 56–58 are not in bold even though the specification marks pedigrees Higher.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- One allele only in each outer box of the square (p. 48).
- For a test cross, always draw **two** squares, both with the homozygous recessive partner (p. 53).
- You cannot decide the unknown genotype without being told what offspring appeared (p. 53).
- Use the letters given in the question (p. 56).

## 10. GAPS in our note, ranked

1. **Pedigrees are taught for a recessive allele only.** Add a `variant` section: a dominant-allele pedigree, with the rule "if the shaded phenotype is dominant, start with the **clear** symbols (homozygous recessive) and with shaded parents who have a clear child (both heterozygous)"; See it on our own three-generation diagram; Your turn on the probability for two unaffected parents (zero).
2. **"Draw both squares" outside the test cross.** Add a twin in the cross section: a dominant-phenotype parent of unknown genotype crossed with a recessive one; she gives both possible squares and their outcomes.
3. **No plant example.** CCEA sets pea plants (2022 Foundation Q5, purple and white flowers). Add one plant cross as a twin.
4. **Body cells carry both alleles.** One line after the gamete step: the zygote gets one allele from each parent and mitosis copies both into every body cell.
5. **Diploid and haploid.** One line tying the square to chromosome numbers.

## 11. Anything in our note the book suggests is wrong or misleading

Nothing is wrong. One risk: the pedigree rule "Start with the shaded symbols: every one of them is bb" is right only when shading marks the recessive phenotype; a dominant-condition pedigree (as the book sets on p. 57) would mislead her. State the condition on the rule.
