## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 2.1, second half: "Transpiration" p. 7; washing-line method and worked example p. 8; potometer, Test Yourself 2.1.2, weight potometer and "Uses of water" p. 9. Answers p. 99. The "Mathematical content" box on p. 1 lists the calculations: rate of uptake from a change in mass or from a bubble potometer, and the rate at which leaves lose water when conditions are changed.

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. The path of water: taken in at the roots, carried up the stem, used in the leaf for support and for photosynthesis in the palisade and spongy layers (p. 7).
2. Transpiration defined as two linked steps: evaporation from mesophyll cell surfaces into the air spaces, then diffusion out through the stomata (p. 7).
3. The five factors listed; four of them speed it up and one (humidity) slows it (p. 7).
4. One reason for each factor, in turn: wind, temperature, light, leaf area, humidity (p. 7).
5. Measuring **loss**: leaves hung on a line and weighed (B6a), with a worked example on graph shapes and a leaf greased on its lower surface (p. 8).
6. Measuring **uptake**: the bubble potometer (B6b), rate as distance moved per unit time, and its use to compare conditions or leaf areas (p. 9).
7. The weight potometer (B6c) as a second way to measure uptake (p. 9).
8. What the plant does with its water: support, transport (including minerals from the root hairs), transpiration and photosynthesis (p. 9).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **Surface area has a reason** (p. 7). Bigger leaves carry more stomata, so more routes out for vapour. Our note lists surface area as a factor and the bundle asks for it, but nothing in the note explains it.
- **Light, by a second route** (p. 7). The book's reason is that brighter light drives more photosynthesis, so the plant draws up more water and the surplus is lost from the leaves. Our note gives the other creditworthy route (stomata open wider in light). CCEA's 2019 Higher scheme (Q8(a)(ii), `docs/sources/papers/science/2019-Summer/B2-H-Biology-MS-9992.txt`) accepts **three** routes, each of which must begin with "more water uptake": more photosynthesis needing more water; stomata open more or wider; more heat so more evaporation. Our note should say that any of the three earns the marks and that the answer opens with the increase in uptake.
- **Wind renews the gradient** (p. 7). The book frames wind as sweeping away vapour so the concentration difference is constantly restored. Our note's "wind sweeps the vapour away, so the difference grows" is the same idea, equally good.
- **Loss and uptake are measured differently** (pp. 8–9). The book pairs each apparatus with what it measures: the washing line measures water **lost** from detached leaves; the potometer measures water **taken up** by a shoot. Our note uses both but never contrasts them in one sentence.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Graph shapes** (p. 8): given one factor's line, complete the other two. Direction only, no numbers.
- **Greased lower surface** (p. 8): leaves coated underneath with a waterproof jelly lose water more slowly, because the jelly blocks the stomata, which are mostly on that surface, so vapour cannot diffuse out. CCEA used this very set-up on the 2022 Unit 7 Higher Booklet A (Q2(b): the leaf with both surfaces covered loses least; marks for water loss, evaporation or transpiration, and all or more stomata covered; `docs/sources/papers/science/2022-Summer/U7-H-Biology-BkA-MS-49194.txt`). The same paper's Q1 hung leaves of three sizes on a line and asked for the relationship between leaf surface area and loss in mass.
- **Rate from a potometer** (p. 9, the first Test Yourself item there): a distance over a time of a few hours gives a rate per hour (single division). Our worked examples do the same with a start reading that is not zero, which is harder and better.
- **Fewer leaves** (p. 9): predict and explain the effect on uptake of a shoot with fewer leaves (less area, fewer stomata, lower rate).

## 6. Practice question types

- **Complete** a sketch graph for a named factor (1–2 marks, shape only).
- **Describe and explain** the effect of greasing one or both leaf surfaces (2–3 marks).
- **Calculate** a rate per hour from a bubble distance and a time (1–2 marks).
- **Predict and explain** the effect of leaf number or area on uptake (2 marks).

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Transpiration** (p. 7): water turning to vapour at the surfaces of the mesophyll cells, then that vapour diffusing through the air spaces and out at the stomata. Same as ours.
- **Humidity** (p. 7): how much water vapour the air already holds. Our note uses the idea ("humid air is already full of vapour") but never states the definition; one line would help.
- **Scheme note on light.** See section 3: three routes accepted, each with "more water uptake" first.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

None (no bold text on pp. 7–9).

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Humidity is the one factor that works the **other way** (p. 7).
- The potometer measures **uptake**; the washing line measures **loss** (pp. 8–9, implicit in the headings).
- A rate is a distance (or mass) **per unit time**; divide, and say per what (p. 9, answer p. 99).

## 10. GAPS in our note, ranked

1. **Surface area is unexplained, and the grease experiment is missing.** Add a `variant` section: explain "more leaf area, more stomata, more routes out", then a See it on a washing-line table of four leaves (no grease, upper surface greased, lower surface greased, both greased) worked to "both greased loses least, then lower greased, because most stomata are underneath", then a Your turn choosing the leaf that loses most. This is CCEA's 2022 Unit 7 shape.
2. **Only one of three creditworthy explanations of light is taught.** Add a `why` callout: any of the three routes earns the marks, and the answer must open with more water being taken up.
3. **No check on graph shapes.** Add a Your turn showing four sketch shapes and asking which one is humidity (or wind); CCEA has asked her to draw these (2019 and 2025 Higher).
4. **Uptake versus loss.** One sentence and a two-column mini-table: potometer, water taken up by a shoot; washing line, water lost by cut leaves.
5. **"Transport" use lacks content.** Add that the water carries minerals up from the root hairs (a link to B1).

## 11. Anything in our note the book suggests is wrong or misleading

Nothing. (Our graphs with plateaus are more accurate than the book's straight lines from the origin.)
