## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Definition: the genome of an organism, such as a bacterium, is altered so that it carries a gene for a wanted protein from another organism (p. 62).
2. In a fermenter with suitable conditions the altered bacterium multiplies and makes that protein, often a human one such as insulin for diabetes (p. 62).
3. Three advantages: no animals are needed (insulin used to come from pig pancreases); large amounts can be made; the product is human insulin, whereas animal insulin differs slightly in structure (p. 62).
4. A second human protein made the same way: erythropoietin, which stimulates red cell production, for people who do not make enough (p. 62).
5. A seven-step diagram and an eight-row table: cut the gene out with restriction enzymes; take the plasmid out of a bacterium; cut the plasmid open with the same enzymes; join the gene into it; put the modified plasmid into a bacterium; let the bacterium and its plasmid replicate in a fermenter; many more replications and large amounts of product; final processing (pp. 63–64).
6. Sticky ends: the enzymes cut in a staggered way, so two pieces cut by the same enzyme join easily (p. 65).
7. Downstreaming: extraction, purification, packaging; needed because the fermenter also holds the bacteria's own proteins and everything else (p. 66).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **A second product** (p. 62). Erythropoietin, the hormone that stimulates red cell production, made by the same method for people who make too little. The specification asks for the advantages of producing insulin *and other products*; our note mentions only insulin.
- **The bacteria's own proteins are the reason for purification** (pp. 63, 66). The book says the fermenter ends up with human insulin *and* bacterial proteins, so extraction and purification must separate them. Our note says purification removes "everything that is not insulin", which is right, but naming the bacteria's own proteins gives her the specific reason an examiner looks for.

Everything else the book says about this topic, our note says more fully and more precisely.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Genetic engineering** (p. 62): changing an organism's genome to include a gene for a wanted protein from another organism. The specification's framing is "to introduce desirable characteristics"; our note uses the specification's framing and explains the protein link. Both are fine.
- **Sticky ends** (p. 65): the single-stranded overhangs left by a staggered cut. Same as ours.
- **Downstreaming** (p. 66): extraction, purification, packaging. Same as ours.
- **Advantages.** The book gives three (no animals, large quantities, human in structure). Our note adds "cheaper"; nothing in the book or the schemes we hold contradicts it, but it is the least secure of the four for a mark.
- **Tier.** The book prints the whole section in bold, including the opening definition, whereas the specification marks only the bullets Higher. Our note treats the definition as open to both tiers (bundle items tagged "both"), which follows the specification.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Use the **same** restriction enzyme on the gene and on the plasmid, so the sticky ends match (p. 65).
- The fermenter makes the human protein **and** bacterial proteins, so the product must be separated and purified (p. 66).

## 10. GAPS in our note, ranked

1. **Only one product.** Add a short `variant` paragraph and a Your turn on erythropoietin: people who make too little are treated with the hormone made by modified bacteria, and it stimulates red cell production (links to `b2-blood-and-vessels`). This is the specification's "other products".
2. **Name the bacteria's own proteins** in the downstreaming section as what purification removes.
3. **Rank the advantages.** Put "identical to human insulin", "large quantities" and "no animals used" first; keep "cheaper" as a fourth.
