## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Organs get oxygenated blood through arteries and send deoxygenated blood back in veins; the blood then goes to the lungs and returns to the heart before going out again, so it passes the heart twice per circuit (p. 14).
2. A table naming, for liver, kidneys, intestines, heart and lungs, the vessel in and the vessel out (p. 14).
3. A memory hook for arteries and "away", then the lung vessels as the exceptions, noting that each lung has its own pulmonary artery (p. 15).
4. The heart's own supply (coronary arteries) and the link forward to heart attacks (p. 15).
5. Aorta, vena cava and hepatic portal vein, each with its job (p. 15).
6. The heart as a muscular pump: right side to the lungs, left side to the body; atria receive, ventricles pump; the left wall is thicker because it drives blood round the whole body, the right only to nearby lungs; the septum keeps the sides apart (p. 15).
7. A labelled section with flow arrows, then the valves: between each atrium and ventricle, and at the base of the two arteries leaving the heart, all shutting when the ventricles relax (p. 16).
8. A two-column flow chart (the deoxygenated route, the oxygenated route), then box-chain completion tasks (pp. 17–19).
9. Exercise: muscles contract more, need more glucose and oxygen for respiration; breathing and heart rate rise; pulse as the measure; regular training strengthens the heart so it pumps more per beat, giving a lower resting rate, a lower rate in the same exercise and a quicker return to rest (p. 19).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **The septum** (p. 15). A wall down the middle so blood cannot pass straight from one side to the other. Our note says "two pumps in one case" but never names or labels the septum.
- **When the valves shut** (p. 16). Atrioventricular valves close when the ventricles relax, and so do the valves where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart; the book calls these arteries unusual for having valves at all. Our note says valves "shut against a backward flow" without saying when.
- **Two pulmonary arteries** (p. 15), one to each lung. A small, examinable detail our note omits.
- **Fitness in three measurable signs** (p. 19). A trained heart pushes more blood per beat, so the fit person shows a lower resting pulse, a lower pulse in the same exercise and a faster recovery. Our note has the training outcomes in the specification's words but not these three observable signs.
- **Why the heart rate rises** (p. 19). The same chain as ours (more respiration, more oxygen and glucose needed); the book adds that breathing also rises but the oxygen still has to be delivered faster.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Name the circulation; fill the chain** (pp. 17–18): name the type of circulation (double), then fill a vertical chain from the lungs through two chambers to the body.
- **Loop completion** (Test Yourself 2.2.2 on p. 19): a closed loop of eight boxes with some given; then name the upper chambers, the thickest-walled chamber, and the structures that stop blood falling back into the heart when it pumps to the body (the valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery).
- **Pulse table** (p. 20, Test Yourself 2.2.3): three exercises, pulse before and after and the increase, one cell missing. Pattern: find the missing "before" value by subtraction (after minus increase); identify the gentlest exercise from the smallest increase; explain the larger increase for the most intense exercise (more contraction, more respiration, more oxygen and glucose needed).
- **Who is fittest** (p. 20, exam tip): with three people's pulse data, say who has the lowest (or highest) rate *compared with the other two*, not just "low". CCEA 2019 B2 (Foundation Q10(b), Higher Q3(b)) gave three marks for exactly this: lowest before exercise, lowest during, quickest back to normal, each against the other boys or with data (`docs/sources/papers/science/2019-Summer/B2-F-Biology-MS-9964.txt`).

## 6. Practice question types

- **Name** chambers and vessels on a diagram (1 each).
- **Complete** a flow chain or loop (2–4).
- **Explain** the difference in ventricle wall thickness (2).
- **Calculate** a missing pulse value (1); **explain** how a table shows which exercise was gentlest (1–2); **explain** the difference in pulse rise between two exercises (2–3).
- **Use data** to decide who is fittest (3, with comparisons).
- In practical questions, **describe** how to measure a pulse (p. 20 tip).

## 7. Definitions and wording, and a book error

- **Double circulation** (p. 15): on one full circuit of the body the blood goes through the heart two times. Same as ours.
- **Scheme wording on training.** The specification says regular exercise strengthens the heart muscle and increases cardiac output at rest. CCEA's 2019 scheme (Foundation Q10(c), Higher Q3(c)) accepts *increased cardiac output* **or** *pumps more blood per beat* **or** *increased stroke volume*. The book words it as more blood per beat. Our note uses the specification's phrase (see section 11 for the one line that over-reaches).
- **Book error: the lungs row of the p. 14 table is reversed.** Under the heading for vessels bringing *oxygenated* blood to each organ, the book lists the pulmonary artery for the lungs, and it lists the pulmonary vein under blood returning *deoxygenated* to the heart. Both are wrong for the lungs: the pulmonary artery brings deoxygenated blood to the lungs and the pulmonary vein returns oxygenated blood to the heart, as the book itself says on p. 15. Authors must not copy that row; our note and bundle have it right.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Link "artery" with "away" (p. 15).
- The pulmonary artery carries **deoxygenated** blood (p. 15).
- On a diagram the heart's **right** side is on the page's **left** (p. 16).
- With three people, compare each with **both** others (p. 20).
- Some questions look the same as Unit 1 breathing-rate questions; answer about the rate actually asked (p. 20).

5. **Box-chain completion.** Add one Your turn in CCEA's box-loop format.

## 11. Anything in our note the book suggests is wrong or misleading

- **The justification in gate "After months of training…" over-reaches.** Its explanation says the resting pulse falls "but the stroke volume rises by more", and worked example `we.…heart-double-circulation.01` builds a resting cardiac output that rises by 7.1 %. The book says only that a trained heart pumps more per beat and so beats more slowly; CCEA's scheme accepts "more blood per beat" as fully equivalent to the specification's phrase. Keep the specification's wording as the answer, but do not argue a numerical rise in resting output as a physiological fact; teach "more blood per beat, so a slower resting pulse" as the mechanism.
