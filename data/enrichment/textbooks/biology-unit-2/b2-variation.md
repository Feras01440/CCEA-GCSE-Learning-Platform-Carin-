## 1. Book chapter and pages

Section 2.5 (differences between individuals, and selection): learning outcomes and "Mathematical content" p. 67 (draw and interpret graphs of the two kinds of variation; read population data as conditions change); "Types of Variation" pp. 68–70 (histogram p. 68, curve and discontinuous variation p. 69, more examples p. 70); the causes of variation p. 70.

## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Variation named: individuals in a population differ; two types (p. 68).
2. Continuous variation: a range of values, shown as a histogram whose bars touch; an example of pupils' heights in six classes (p. 68).
3. Join the mid-points of the bar tops to get a curve, the usual way continuous variation is drawn; most people cluster near the centre (pp. 68–69).
4. Finger length as a second continuous example (p. 69).
5. Discontinuous variation: a few separate categories; tongue rolling as two bars that do not touch (p. 69).
6. More discontinuous examples: hand dominance (two bars) and blood groups (four bars) (p. 70).
7. Causes: genetic (mutation; sexual reproduction through (bold) independent assortment in meiosis and through the joining of two different gametes at fertilisation) and environmental (food supply affecting height) (p. 70).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **From bars to a curve** (pp. 68–69). Join the centre points of the bar tops and the histogram becomes a smooth hump; that hump is how continuous variation is often drawn. Our note never draws or names this curve, yet `b2-natural-selection-selective-breeding` asks her to read and extend exactly such curves (beak depth). A two-line bridge here would prepare that.
- **Most individuals are in the middle** (p. 69). The hump's peak is the commonest value; the extremes are rare. Our note reads counts off bars but never states this shape.
- **Two sources inside sexual reproduction** (p. 70). Each gamete gets its own mixture of alleles because the chromosome pairs line up independently in meiosis (bold, Higher), and then two different gametes join at fertilisation. Our note says sexual reproduction "gives every child a new combination" without either mechanism.

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Continuous variation.** The book describes it as having "many different categories" with a range of values. That wording is loose: continuous data has no categories at all until we choose class intervals. Our note's "any value within a range" is the better definition; keep it.
- **Discontinuous variation** (p. 69): a few separate categories. Same as ours.
- **Mutation** (p. 70): a random change in chromosome number or in a gene's structure. Same as ours (the specification's definition).
- **Environmental example** (p. 70): the book says the food available affects height. Ours says diet. Same idea.

## 8. Higher-tier-only content in the book

Printed in bold: "due to independent assortment" as the reason each gamete has a unique mixture of alleles (p. 70).

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Continuous: bars **touch**. Discontinuous: bars **do not touch** (pp. 68–69).
- A graph of blood groups has **four** bars; of hand dominance, **two** (p. 70).
