## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. The first line of defence keeps microorganisms out: skin, mucous membranes, blood clotting (p. 82).
2. Mucous membranes in detail: mucus traps dust and microorganisms, cilia sweep it to the throat, it is swallowed and stomach acid breaks the microorganisms down (p. 82).
3. Clotting as a chain of six boxes, from platelets releasing a chemical, through soluble fibrinogen becoming insoluble fibrin, a mesh trapping cells, to a clot and a scab (pp. 82–83).
4. Past the barriers, lymphocytes recognise microorganisms as foreign and make antibodies, each complementary to the antigens (surface proteins) of one kind of microorganism (p. 83).
5. Antibodies join microorganisms together (clumping), which stops them spreading and reproducing and reduces symptoms (p. 83).
6. Making antibodies takes days, and meanwhile the infection can take hold; levels must rise high enough to protect (p. 84).
7. Phagocytes then engulf and digest the clumps (p. 84).
8. A seven-step summary of the whole response (p. 85).
9. Active immunity (your own antibodies, after infection or vaccination) with memory lymphocytes making the second response faster, larger and longer; passive immunity (antibodies received ready-made), which a baby gets through the placenta and later in its mother's milk, protecting the baby before its own system is ready (p. 85).

## 3. Explanations and analogies the book uses that our note lacks

- **Cilia, swallowing and stomach acid** (p. 82). The mucous membrane is not just sticky: cilia carry the trapped mucus up to the throat, it is swallowed, and the stomach's acid destroys the microorganisms. Our note stops at "trap them in sticky mucus".
- **Passive immunity across the placenta** (p. 85). The book gives two natural routes, placenta and breast milk, and the reason passive immunity matters for a newborn: its own immune system is not yet fully working. Our note gives milk and injection only, and no reason.
- **Soluble to insoluble is the point of clotting** (p. 83). The book's chain turns on the change from a dissolved protein to a solid one; our note says "insoluble fibrin threads" and "fibrinogen, a plasma protein", which is the same fact, but not framed as the reason the blood sets.
- **The exposed days** (p. 84). While the first antibodies are being made the body is vulnerable, which is why a first infection makes you ill. Our note says you "may feel ill meanwhile", which is the same idea; fine.

## 6. Practice question types

- **Name** the barriers (1–3).
- **Describe** clotting (2–4).
- **Explain** how antibodies and phagocytes destroy a microorganism (3–5, with the words complementary, clump, engulf, digest).
- **Explain** why a second infection causes no illness (3).
- **Distinguish** active from passive immunity (2), with an example of each.

## 7. Definitions and wording

- **Antigens** (p. 83): proteins on a microorganism's surface. **Antibodies**: made by lymphocytes, complementary to one antigen. Same as ours.
- **Clumping** (p. 83): antibodies joining microorganisms together. Same as ours.
- **Active / passive** (p. 85): whether the body makes the antibodies itself. Same as ours ("did your own lymphocytes make them?").
- **Memory lymphocytes.** The book says they keep a memory of the antibodies' structure; ours says they recognise the antigen. Ours is the better wording and matches CCEA 2021 Higher ("recognise (tetanus) antigen").
- A slip in the book (p. 85): it attributes breast-feeding to the foetus; it is the baby after birth that feeds. Do not copy.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- Antibodies are **specific**: one antibody, one antigen (p. 83).
- The first response is **slow**; memory makes the second fast (pp. 84–85).
