## 2. The book's teaching sequence

1. Blood brings oxygen and glucose to cells for respiration; if a coronary artery (the heart's own supply) is blocked, a heart attack can follow (p. 94).
2. The chain in nine boxes: fatty deposits such as cholesterol collect in the artery wall; the lumen narrows; a clot may form and block it; blood stops; the muscle cells beyond get no glucose or oxygen, cannot respire, and die (p. 94).
3. The same chain in an artery supplying the brain gives reduced brain function: a stroke (p. 94).
4. Treatments: angioplasty (a balloon-like device widens the narrowed artery) followed by a stent (a mesh tube left inside to hold it open), so more blood flows (p. 95).
5. Statins: daily tablets that lower blood cholesterol, helping prevent heart attacks and strokes (p. 95).
6. Aspirin: makes platelets less sticky, so the blood clots less readily (p. 95).
7. A drug trial of statins to explain and draw conclusions from (p. 96).
8. Lifestyle factors that raise risk (too much fat, too little exercise, smoking, stress) and the changes that lower it (p. 97).

## 3. Explanations and analogies worth recreating (our own words)

- **A chain, one link per mark** (p. 94). The strength of the book's box diagram is that each step causes the next. CCEA's 2025 Foundation scheme gives four marks for the links (narrowing or blockage; reduced flow or a clot; less oxygen; less glucose; less respiration or energy; death of heart muscle cells: `docs/sources/papers/science/2025-Summer/B2-F-Biology-MS-67230.txt`, Q3(a)(ii)). Our note should teach it as a numbered chain with the mark each link earns.
- **Same chain, different organ** (p. 94). Heart attack and stroke differ only in which artery is blocked; one figure with two branches (coronary artery, brain artery) makes that obvious. CCEA 2025 Foundation Q3(c)(i) asked which organ a stroke affects.
- **Treatment matched to the link it breaks** (pp. 94–95). Angioplasty and a stent reopen the narrowed lumen; statins slow the build-up at the start of the chain; aspirin acts at the clot step. Matching each treatment to its link mirrors the device our contraception note uses so well.

## 5. Worked examples, calculations and data tasks

- **Explain how high cholesterol leads to a heart attack** (p. 96): the chain, six lines.
- **Statin trial** (p. 96): a large group with high cholesterol, half given statins, everyone on the same healthy-eating advice, six months, a two-line graph; conclusions (cholesterol falls with statins; by how much; that it also falls a little without them; that statins lower it more and faster). The book's answer quotes figures read from the graph; CCEA rewards quoted data.
- CCEA's own data shapes in this topic, from the schemes: a percentage of a total from a table (2024 Higher Q7(b)(i), 72.4 % of 5762 → 4172); what a blood-pressure trial does and does not show, and why a treated person could still have a heart attack (other risk factors: obesity, smoking, stress, high cholesterol, a narrowed artery, genes) (2024 Higher Q7(b)(ii)–(iv)); an aspirin-dose trial read for the smaller effective dose (2022 Higher); reading two patients' arteries and explaining the difference by statins (2021 Higher Q6(c)); identifying a stent and angioplasty from a diagram (2019, 2024).

## 6. Practice question types, with CCEA evidence

- **Name** the substance that builds up (cholesterol) (2025 Foundation).
- **Explain** how a blockage leads to death of heart muscle (4, 2025 Foundation; 5 with glucose and oxygen, 2024 Higher).
- **Name** the organ affected in a stroke (2025 Foundation).
- **Name** two drugs that reduce risk (aspirin, statins) and **explain** how aspirin helps (2019; 2022 Higher: less likely to clot, platelets less sticky).
- **Identify** a stent and angioplasty (2019; 2024 Higher).
- **Give** lifestyle changes that lower risk (stop smoking, more exercise) and risk factors (lack of exercise; too much fat, salt or sugar; obesity; genetics) (2021 Higher Q6(a); 2025 Foundation Q3(c)(ii)).
- **Interpret** trial data and **calculate** a percentage (2022, 2024 Higher).

## 7. Definitions and wording, and statements not to copy

- **Heart attack** (p. 94): death of heart muscle cells when a coronary artery is blocked. **Stroke**: death of brain cells, with reduced brain function, when an artery to the brain is blocked. Same as the specification.
- **Not to copy: "the heart stops beating"** (p. 94 chain; p. 96 answer). A heart attack is the death of part of the heart muscle; the heart often keeps beating. CCEA's scheme ends the chain at "(heart muscle) cells die". End ours there.
- **"Aspirin thins the blood."** The book says so (p. 95) and CCEA's 2019 scheme accepts it, but the mechanism is that platelets become less sticky, so clots form less easily (also accepted). Teach the mechanism and accept the phrase.
- **Not to copy: the p. 97 claim that a fitter person's stronger heart means less cholesterol is deposited.** Heart-muscle strength does not govern deposition; exercise lowers risk by helping with weight, blood fats and blood pressure. Keep exercise as a risk-lowering factor without that causal link.
- **Stress** (p. 97): raises blood pressure, which adds to the other risks. Same as the specification's list.

## 9. Pitfalls and "remember" notes in the book

- The same processes in a **brain** artery give a stroke (p. 94).
- The coronary arteries supply the **heart muscle itself** (p. 15, cross-referenced here).
