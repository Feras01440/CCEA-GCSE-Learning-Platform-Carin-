# Enrichment dossier — p2-lens-ray-diagrams

**CCEA** Double Award Science, P2 §2.2 Light · outcomes 2.2.9, 2.2.10 (F), **2.2.11 (H)** · tier mixed · **difficulty 5 — the hardest topic in P2 by our own scoring** · no prescribed practical on this topic (focal-length measurement sits on `p2-lenses-focal-length`)
**Prerequisite in our taxonomy:** `p2-lenses-focal-length`
**Compiled** 19 September 2026 · 50 minutes
**Headline** Lenses are **absent from AQA Combined Science: Trilogy and from Edexcel Combined Science entirely** — they exist only in the separate Physics specifications. So the whole of the free, board-aligned material she might reach was written for Triple Science students. The one borrowing that matters is a construction habit, not content: AQA's separate-physics treatment and every good teacher's version insist you **mark and label F and 2F on both sides before drawing a single ray**, because the ray rules are defined by those points. Our own Higher examiner finding — "focal points not labelled; arrows missing on rays" — says candidates are losing marks on the scaffolding, not the physics. Make the scaffolding the lesson.

## 2. Teaching angles worth recreating (in our own words)

1. **Scaffolding first: axis, lens, F and 2F on both sides, object — then rays.** *Implicit in AQA §4.6.2.5, which prints the standard lens symbols in the specification itself, and explicit in every good construction guide (Save My Exams' AQA "Convex and concave ray diagrams" notes open with exactly this order).* Our Summer 2025 Higher finding is that focal points went unlabelled and arrows were missing — that is a **procedure** failure, not a physics failure. So the method steps are: (1) draw the principal axis; (2) draw the lens as a vertical line with outward arrowheads at both ends (the standard convex symbol); (3) mark F and 2F **on both sides**, equally spaced; (4) draw the object as an upright arrow standing on the axis; (5) only now draw rays, **each with an arrowhead showing direction**; (6) mark the image where the rays cross, as an arrow; (7) write the three words. Every step carries its reason, and step 3's reason is that two of the three ray rules are *defined by F*.

2. **Three rules, two needed, one to check.** *Standard construction pedagogy; Save My Exams and Physics & Maths Tutor both present the three rays and note that two determine the image.* The rules: a ray parallel to the axis refracts through F on the far side; a ray through the centre of the lens goes straight on; a ray through F on the near side refracts parallel to the axis. Any two locate the image; the third should land on the same point, so it is a free self-check. Telling her that the third ray is a **check** converts a memory burden into a tool — and it is how she catches her own error before the examiner does.

3. **Describe every image with the same three words, in the same order.** *A discipline that Save My Exams, PMT and MME all impose in their AQA notes, and which CCEA's mark schemes reward.* **Real or virtual · upright or inverted · magnified or diminished.** Always those three, always that order. It makes the answer a fill-in-the-blanks rather than a recall, and it makes a partially-correct answer partially creditable.

4. **Real versus virtual, defined by a screen.** *The operational definition used across the board.* A **real** image is where light actually arrives, so it can be caught on a screen — a camera sensor, a cinema screen. A **virtual** image is where the light only *appears* to come from; put a screen there and there is nothing on it. That test — "could you catch it on a screen?" — is quicker and more reliable at GCSE than "do the rays actually cross or only extend backwards?", and it explains why the magnifying-glass image is drawn with **dashed** construction lines.

5. **The magnifying glass does not always magnify.** *This angle is available as a photograph (see §7): `File:Convex lens (magnifying glass) and upside-down image.jpg` shows a hand lens with a distant scene through it, upside-down and smaller.* The same lens gives the camera case when the object is far away and the magnifying-glass case when the object is close. One object, one lens, two completely different images, and the only thing that changed is **u**. That is the whole topic in one photograph, and it is why the three cases must be taught as one sliding parameter rather than three separate facts.

6. **Why the image is upside down.** *Constructed, because it is the "why" that most resources skip.* The rays from the top of the object end up below the axis, and the rays from the bottom end up above, because they **cross** at the lens. Say it once with the figure in front of her; it removes the need to memorise "inverted" for the two real cases.

## 3. The representation to rebuild as our own SVG

**Hero figure: one parameterised ray diagram.** This should be a real generator, because the whole topic is one diagram with one parameter changed, and because hand-drawn versions are where geometry errors creep in.

`viewBox` about `0 0 1000 480`, origin at the lens centre, principal axis horizontal through the middle.

- **Principal axis:** a horizontal line across the full width, labelled at the right end.
- **Lens:** a vertical line at the centre with outward-pointing arrowheads top and bottom (the standard converging-lens symbol, which is what CCEA prints).
- **F and 2F marked on both sides**, as small crosses on the axis with labels `F`, `2F` on the left and `F`, `2F` on the right, at exactly `f` and `2f` from the lens. These are the four marks our examiner says candidates omit, so they are drawn first and never optional.
- **Object:** an upright arrow standing on the axis at distance `u` to the left, height `h`.
- **Three rays, each with an arrowhead mid-segment:**
  - ray 1 from the object tip, parallel to the axis, refracting at the lens line and passing through the far-side `F`;
  - ray 2 from the object tip straight through the lens centre, undeviated;
  - ray 3 from the object tip through the near-side `F`, refracting at the lens line to run parallel to the axis.
- **Image:** an arrow at the crossing point, drawn **inverted below the axis** for the two real cases. For the virtual case (`u < f`) the rays diverge after the lens, so the three rays are extended **backwards as dashed construction lines** to their apparent crossing point on the same side as the object, and the image arrow is drawn upright and larger there.
- **Label block** at the bottom right, printing the three words for the current case and the value of `u` in terms of `f`.

**Generator parameters — all geometry computed from these, nothing typed:**
- `u` (object distance, expressed as a multiple of `f`) — the single parameter that produces every case: `u = 3f` (camera), `u = 1.5f` (projector), `u = 0.5f` (magnifying glass). The image distance follows from `1/v = 1/f − 1/u`; **compute with it, never print it**, because the lens equation is beyond CCEA.
- `rays: (1|2|3)[]` — so a gate can show two rays and ask her to place the third, or show one and ask where the image is.
- `showConstructionLines: boolean` — the dashed backward extensions, on only for the virtual case.
- `labels: "full" | "no-focal-points" | "blank"` — the `"no-focal-points"` variant is a find-the-mistake item aimed straight at the recorded finding.
- `showArrowheads: boolean` — likewise, since missing arrowheads is a named loss of marks.
- Assert in the generator that no emitted coordinate is `NaN` when `u` approaches `f`.

**Second figure: the sliding-object strip.** `viewBox` about `0 0 1000 620`. Four small versions of the hero diagram stacked vertically, sharing the same lens position, `f` and scale, with `u` decreasing down the strip: `u = 3f`, `u = 2f`, `u = 1.5f`, `u = 0.5f`. Beside each, the three words and the everyday device: **camera · (the boundary case) · projector · magnifying glass**. The point of drawing them at the same scale on the same axis is that the image can be seen sliding outwards and growing as the object approaches F, then flipping to the other side when it passes F. Nothing else makes that continuity visible.

**Third, small: the screen test.** Two panels side by side, identical lens and object. Left: real image, a screen drawn at the image position with the image on it. Right: virtual image, a screen drawn at the apparent image position with **nothing on it** and the caption "the light never got here". Ten lines of SVG and it settles real-versus-virtual permanently.

## 4. Practicals CCEA also examines

No prescribed practical is tagged to this topic — CCEA's lens practical work sits on `p2-lenses-focal-length`. Variants worth knowing because CCEA could set a written question about them:

| Variant | Where it comes from | What is measured | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Illuminated object, lens on an optical bench, screen** | The standard method on every board | move the screen until the image is sharp; record `u` and `v`, and the image's nature | This is how the three cases are *produced*. A Booklet B question could give a table of `u` and the image nature and ask her to complete it |
| **Distant-object method for focal length** | AQA 8463 (investigate the magnification produced by a range of convex lenses); CCEA `p2-lenses-focal-length` | focus a distant window on a wall; the lens-to-image distance is `f` | The bridge between the two topics: `f` is what the ray diagram's F marks depend on |
| **Measuring magnification** | AQA §4.6.2.5 explicitly (image height ÷ object height) | image and object heights in mm | **Beyond CCEA as a calculation**, but the *idea* that the image can be measured supports the "magnified or diminished" word |
| **A lens over printed text, moved slowly away** | Everyday, and the cheapest demonstration in the topic | qualitative: upright and magnified, then blurred, then inverted | It is the sliding-object strip, live, with a lens from a pound shop. Worth naming in the lesson as something she can actually do |

The transferable practical-skills demands CCEA does examine: recognising when an image is sharp (and that "sharpest" is a judgement, so repeat it), and the fact that a real image can be caught on a screen while a virtual one cannot — an operational distinction, which is exactly the kind of thing Booklet B asks about.

## 5. Question types the other boards use that CCEA also rewards

1. **"Complete the ray diagram to find the image" [3].** The universal shape, and the one our Foundation candidates could not start. The mark-scheme habit across boards: **one mark per correctly-constructed ray, one for the image drawn in the right place with the right orientation** — and marks are awarded for the *construction*, so a ray drawn without an arrowhead or without passing through F earns nothing even if the image ends up in the right place. Build this as a `graph`-style drawing part where the expected image vertices are marked within tolerance, and author the feedback to name which ray is wrong.
2. **"Describe the image formed" [2–3].** Three words, and boards mark them as separate points. Encode as a `text` part with one key-word group per word — real/virtual, upright/inverted, magnified/diminished — so a two-out-of-three answer scores two. Include the rejects: "bigger" alone, "smaller" alone, "the right way up" is acceptable but "normal" is not.
3. **"A camera and a projector both use a converging lens. Explain why the object is placed in a different position in each" [3].** A comparison item that AQA sets on the separate paper and that CCEA's three named devices make inevitable. Marks: beyond 2F for the camera so the image is diminished; between F and 2F for the projector so it is magnified; both real so both can fall on a sensor or a screen.
4. **Find-the-mistake.** Present a diagram with exactly one error — F unlabelled, an arrowhead missing, the parallel ray drawn through the lens centre, or the image drawn upright — and ask what is wrong. Our `find-the-mistake` item type is the strongest interaction in the product and this topic's examiner evidence names four candidate errors, so there are four items here without inventing anything.
5. **"Why can the image from a magnifying glass not be caught on a screen?" [2].** Virtual; the light does not actually come from there, it only appears to. Targets the hardest Higher idea with a one-mark-per-clause scheme.

## 6. Misconception framings worth borrowing

| Misconception | The framing that fixes it | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Cannot start the diagram at all | Give the seven-step procedure and make steps 1–4 (axis, lens, F and 2F both sides, object) a gate of their own, before any ray | our Summer 2025 P2 Foundation finding: "many candidates could not construct any correct ray" |
| F and 2F not marked | Two of the three ray rules are *defined by F*; without F on the paper there are no rules to follow | our Summer 2025 P2 Higher finding |
| Arrows missing from rays | A ray without an arrowhead is a line, and a line does not say which way the light went. Marks are for the construction | our Summer 2025 P2 Higher finding |
| A magnifying glass always magnifies | The photograph in §7 shows the same lens giving a small, upside-down image of a distant scene. What changed is `u`, not the lens | `File:Convex lens (magnifying glass) and upside-down image.jpg` |
| Virtual means "not really there" | Virtual means *the light did not come from there*. It is perfectly visible; it just cannot be caught on a screen. The two-panel screen test settles it | the operational definition, standard on all boards |
| The image is always inverted | It is inverted for the two real cases and upright for the virtual one. The sliding strip shows the flip happening at F | follows from the representation |
| Any two rays will do, so the third is optional | Two locate it; the third **checks** it. Draw it and catch your own mistake | standard construction pedagogy |
| The lens "makes" the image bigger | The lens changes where the rays go; the image's size follows from where they cross. Move the object and the same lens gives a different size | follows from the sliding strip |

## 8. Scope note

**Beyond CCEA — `notonspec` if used:**
- **magnification = image height ÷ object height** (AQA 8463 §4.6.2.5, on their equation sheet)
- **the lens equation `1/f = 1/u + 1/v`** — use it inside the generator, never show it
- **ray diagrams for concave (diverging) lenses** (AQA requires them; CCEA names diverging lenses only for focal length)
- power of a lens, dioptres
- the eye, accommodation, long and short sight and their correction
- chromatic and spherical aberration
- real-image formation in the eye or a camera at the level of the sensor

**CCEA-only or CCEA-specific:**
- the three named applications — **camera, projector, magnifying glass** — as the way the three cases are labelled and examined
- carrying this content at **Double Award** at all, when AQA and Edexcel confine it to separate Physics
- the tier split: the two **real** cases at Foundation, the **virtual** magnifying-glass case (2.2.11) at **Higher only**
