# Enrichment dossier — c2-carboxylic-acids

**CCEA** Double Award Science, **C2** §2.5 Organic Chemistry · outcomes **2.5.23 (H)**, 2.5.24, 2.5.25 (both partly Higher) · tier mixed · **difficulty 5 — the hardest topic in C2 by our own scoring** · no prescribed practical
**CCEA must-recall:** **Higher** — methanoic HCOOH, ethanoic CH₃COOH, propanoic C₂H₅COOH, butanoic C₃H₇COOH (liquids); functional group **–COOH**. Carboxylic acids are **weak acids: only partially ionised in solution**. Reactions: with **carbonates** → salt (ethanoate) + water + CO₂; with **hydroxides** → salt + water; with **metals** → salt + hydrogen, e.g. `2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂`.
**Prerequisites in our taxonomy:** `c2-alcohols-fermentation`, `c1-acids-alkalis-strength`, `c1-reactions-of-acids`
**Compiled** 20 September 2026 · 32 minutes
**Headline** **This row was `ccea-only` and is now corrected to `partial`, because the half that actually loses marks is matched.** AQA Trilogy **§5.4.2.5** (Higher only) defines a weak acid as "only partially ionised in aqueous solution", names **ethanoic acid** as its example, and then adds the exact clause our Summer 2025 finding turns on — *"for a given concentration of aqueous solutions, the stronger an acid, the lower the pH"*. That sentence is the distinction candidates conflate. The organic half — the –COOH group and the four named acids — is in **AQA Chemistry 8462 §4.7.2.4**, whose list is identical to CCEA's, and in no Edexcel 1SC0 statement.

## 2. Teaching angles worth recreating (in our own words)

1. **Weak is about how much it ionises, not how low the pH goes.** *AQA §5.4.2.5 is the source and it is worth following exactly, because it separates the two ideas and then relates them.* **Weak** means only *some* of the molecules split into ions in solution. **Concentrated** means a lot of acid per litre. They are independent: a concentrated weak acid and a dilute strong acid can have the same pH. AQA's own added clause — *for a given concentration*, the stronger acid has the lower pH — is the bridge, and the words "for a given concentration" are the whole of it. Our Summer 2025 finding is precisely this conflation, so this angle carries the topic.

2. **Draw the equilibrium arrow and make it mean something.** *Standard in the AQA treatment of §5.4.2.5.* `CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺` with a **double arrow**, against `HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻` with a single one. The double arrow says most of the molecules are still whole at any moment. That single visual difference is the definition, and it is far more durable than the phrase "partially ionised".

3. **The –COOH group is the acid, and everything before it is just a tail.** *AQA 8462 §4.7.2.4's framing, and the reason the family behaves alike.* Methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic and butanoic differ only in the length of the carbon chain; the acidic behaviour comes from the same –COOH in every one. So a reaction learnt for ethanoic acid transfers to all four, which is a real economy at Higher.

4. **Naming the salt is a rule: drop "-ic acid", add "-ate".** *CCEA Summer 2025 C2 Higher: naming **copper(II) ethanoate** was a recorded difficulty.* ethanoic acid → ethano**ate**; methanoic → methano**ate**. Then the metal's name goes first, with its Roman numeral where needed. Set it as a drill in both directions, because the salt name is a mark on its own and it is a convention rather than chemistry.

5. **The three reactions are the C1 reactions of acids, with a new acid.** *Directly from CCEA's `c1-reactions-of-acids`, and the cheapest possible teaching economy.* Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Acid + hydroxide → salt + water. Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen. She has met all three; the only new thing is that the salt is an ethanoate. Presenting the topic as "the same three reactions, a different acid" removes most of its apparent difficulty.

6. **Balance the metal equation carefully — two acid molecules for one magnesium.** *CCEA's own example, `2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂`, and CCEA Summer 2024 records carboxylic acid + base equations as weak.* The bracket-and-subscript notation for `(CH₃COO)₂Mg` is where it goes wrong: the whole ethanoate ion is taken twice, so the bracket must enclose it. Draw the bracket explicitly and say why it is there.

7. **Weak does not mean unreactive.** *A common conflation worth pre-empting.* Ethanoic acid still fizzes with a carbonate and still neutralises a hydroxide; it just does so more slowly at the same concentration, because fewer H⁺ ions are present at any moment. That connects to `c2-collision-theory-catalysts` and to `c1-indicators-and-ph`.

## 3. The representation to rebuild as our own SVG

**Hero figure: strong against weak, as a particle picture.** `viewBox` about `0 0 960 420`. Two boxes side by side, each containing the **same number** of acid particles at the **same concentration**.

- **Left — hydrochloric acid (strong).** Every particle drawn as a **separated pair**: an H⁺ and a Cl⁻, with none left whole. Equation beneath with a **single arrow**.
- **Right — ethanoic acid (weak).** Most particles drawn **whole** (a single CH₃COOH shape), with only two or three split into H⁺ and CH₃COO⁻. Equation beneath with a **double arrow**.
- **A counter under each box**: `H⁺ ions present: 12` and `H⁺ ions present: 3`, computed from the drawn particles.
- **A caption band across both:** *same concentration, different degree of ionisation — so different pH. Change the concentration and this comparison changes too.*
- **Generator parameters:** `particles: number`, `ionisedFractionStrong: 1`, `ionisedFractionWeak: number`, `showCounters: boolean`, `showEquations: boolean`, `acids: [string, string]`. Counters computed from the drawn particles; assert the strong box has no whole molecules.

**Second figure: the family ladder with the group circled.** Four rows — methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic, butanoic — each with the displayed formula drawn out, the **–COOH circled identically in every row**, and the chain length highlighted as the only difference. A column on the right gives the **salt name** for each (methanoate, ethanoate, propanoate, butanoate), which sets up angle 4. **Generator parameters:** `members: number`, `blankColumn: "name" | "formula" | "salt" | null`, `circleFunctionalGroup: boolean`.

**Third figure: the three reactions strip.** Three rows, each with a word equation and a symbol equation beneath it:
- `ethanoic acid + sodium carbonate → sodium ethanoate + water + carbon dioxide`
- `ethanoic acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium ethanoate + water`
- `ethanoic acid + magnesium → magnesium ethanoate + hydrogen`, with `2CH₃COOH + Mg → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂` and **the bracket around the ethanoate ion picked out** with a leader labelled *the whole ion is taken twice*.
Generator: `metal`, `base`, `carbonate` as parameters so twins are computed rather than typed; assert each symbol equation balances.

## 4. Practical variants CCEA also examines

No prescribed practical on this outcome, but the topic's observations are easy and CCEA examines them as written questions:

- **Fizz test with a carbonate**, and the **limewater** confirmation — links to `c2-carbon-dioxide-preparation`, which is Prescribed Practical C6, so the apparatus is already learnt.
- **Comparing ethanoic and hydrochloric acid at the same concentration** — pH meter or universal indicator, and rate of fizzing with magnesium. This is the practical that makes "weak" observable, and it is an ideal Booklet B stem: same concentration, different pH, explain.
- **AQA's own suggestion** at §5.4.2.5 is "an opportunity to measure the pH of different acids at different concentrations", which is exactly the comparison above and is a legitimate model for the method.
- The transferable Unit 7 demand: **controlling concentration** so the comparison is fair, and explaining **why** a fair test needs it here.

## 5. Question types the other boards use that CCEA also rewards

1. **"Explain what is meant by a weak acid" [2].** Partially ionised in solution — and *not* "has a high pH". The recorded failure. Author the rejects to include "low concentration" and "high pH", with feedback naming the difference.
2. **"Both solutions have the same concentration. Explain why the ethanoic acid has a higher pH" [3].** AQA §5.4.2.5's own clause, turned into an item. Marks: fewer H⁺ ions; because only partially ionised; so higher pH.
3. **"Name the salt formed when ethanoic acid reacts with copper(II) oxide" [1].** Copper(II) ethanoate — the recorded naming difficulty.
4. **"Write a balanced symbol equation for ethanoic acid and magnesium" [2].** The bracket-and-subscript item.
5. **"Complete the word equation" [2].** Carbonate route, with all three products.
6. **"Draw the structure of propanoic acid" [1–2].** Higher; the –COOH must be drawn out, not abbreviated.

## 6. Misconception framings worth borrowing

| Misconception | The framing that fixes it | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Weak means low pH, or dilute | Weak = **partially ionised**. Dilute = little acid per litre. Two independent ideas; the particle figure shows both | **CCEA S2025 C2 Higher**; AQA §5.4.2.5 separates them explicitly |
| Weak means unreactive | It still reacts, just more slowly at the same concentration, because fewer H⁺ are present | links to `c2-collision-theory-catalysts` |
| Salt names guessed | Drop "-ic acid", add "-ate". Metal first, with its Roman numeral | CCEA S2025: copper(II) ethanoate |
| `(CH₃COO)₂Mg` written without brackets | The whole ethanoate ion is taken twice, so the bracket encloses it | CCEA's own example; S2024 records weak equations |
| A single arrow used for a weak acid | The double arrow **is** the definition | AQA §5.4.2.5's treatment |
| –COOH abbreviated in a displayed formula | At Higher the group must be drawn out, with the C=O and the O–H visible | CCEA S2024: drawing full structural formulae is the biggest difficulty |
| The tail changes the chemistry | All four behave alike because the –COOH is the same. Learn one, transfer to four | AQA 8462 §4.7.2.4's homologous-series framing |
