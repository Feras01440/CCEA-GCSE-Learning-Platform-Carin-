# Enrichment dossier — p2-refraction-and-dispersion

**CCEA** Double Award Science, P2 §2.2 Light · outcomes 2.2.5, 2.2.6 (spec labels: Refraction of light; Dispersion of white light) · tier F · difficulty 4 · **Prescribed Practical P5**
**Prerequisites in our taxonomy:** `p2-reflection`, `p2-wave-types-and-properties`
**Compiled** 19 September 2026 · 50 minutes
**Headline** Two findings that change how this lesson should be built. First, **dispersion by a prism appears in no AQA or Edexcel GCSE specification** — not Trilogy, not separate Physics, not Combined. It is CCEA-only, and half of our examiner evidence is about it. Second, the Institute of Physics' *marching model* is the analogy that makes "change of speed causes change of direction" inevitable rather than asserted — and "change of speed" is the exact phrase our Unit 7 examiner demanded in place of "optical density". Build the lesson on the marching model and draw dispersion ourselves, because nobody else will have drawn it for her board.

## 2. Teaching angles worth recreating (in our own words)

1. **The marching model.** *Institute of Physics, IOPSpark, "Marching model of refraction" (`spark.iop.org/marching-model-refraction`), written for 14–16.* A row of people with linked arms marches at an angle across a line between tarmac and grass. Each person shortens their stride the instant they cross. Because one end of the row crosses first, the whole row swings round. Nobody decided to turn; the turn is a consequence of one end slowing before the other. **This is the only GCSE-level explanation that makes refraction feel inevitable instead of arbitrary**, and it is exactly why "change of speed" is the correct answer and "optical density" is not — optical density names a property, the model shows a mechanism. **Where it breaks:** the model explains the *direction* of the bend and why the bend happens at all, but nothing in it says which way is "towards the normal" — that still has to be tied to the diagram.

2. **Change of speed, in those words, everywhere.** *Edexcel CP4.10 words the statement as "the change of direction **and speed**"; AQA's HT statement is "explain refraction in terms of the change of speed"; our own Summer 2025 Unit 7 Booklet A finding says the explanation must be a change of speed, not "optical density".* Three independent sources agreeing is a strong signal. Make "the light slows down, so it changes direction" the sentence she writes in a `blank` gate, and add "optical density" to the rejects with feedback that explains why it is not enough.

3. **Ripples in a ripple tank first, light second.** *IOPSpark, "Refraction of ripples entering shallow water".* Waves slowing over a submerged plate bend visibly, and you can see the wavefronts do it. CCEA's spec says refraction happens to waves in general and examines it for light; showing the wave version first means the light version is a special case rather than a new rule. This is also the honest reading of AQA's HT wave-front statement.

4. **Draw the normal before you draw anything else.** *A habit, not a fact, and it comes straight from our own Summer 2025 P2 Higher finding: prism diagrams were "drawn without normals and bent the wrong way".* Make it a numbered method step with its reason: the normal is the line all angles are measured from, so a diagram without it cannot be marked. Every figure we draw must show the normal as a dashed line, and every drawing gate must require it.

5. **Dispersion is refraction happening seven times over.** *Constructed here, because no board teaches it — the closest published treatments are the classic Newton demonstrations.* The prism does not add colour. White light already contains every colour; each colour travels at a slightly different speed in glass, so each is refracted by a slightly different amount, and they arrive separated. Red changes speed least, so it bends least; violet changes speed most, so it bends most. Framing dispersion as **the same rule applied seven times** links it to the previous section instead of leaving it as an isolated fact — which is precisely the confusion our Foundation examiner recorded.

6. **Two faces, two refractions.** *Follows from the CCEA Higher finding about the inverted prism.* A prism question is not one bend, it is two: once on the way in and once on the way out, and the two bends **add** because of the prism's shape (unlike a rectangular block, where they cancel and the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray). Teach the block first so the cancelling is understood, then the prism so the adding is a contrast. That contrast is the single hardest idea on this topic and it is worth its own section.

## 3. The representation to rebuild as our own SVG

**Hero figure: the rectangular block, both boundaries.** `viewBox` about `0 0 900 460`.

- A rectangle of glass across the middle of the frame, drawn with a light `fill-opacity` tint and labelled "glass".
- **An incident ray** arriving at the top face at about 45°, with an arrowhead mid-ray.
- **A dashed normal** at the entry point, perpendicular to the surface, extending on both sides of the boundary. Angle `i` marked between the incident ray and the normal with a small arc; angle `r` marked between the refracted ray and the normal, visibly **smaller**.
- **The refracted ray** inside the glass, bent towards the normal.
- **A second dashed normal** at the exit point on the bottom face, with the exit angles marked and the **emergent ray bent away from the normal**.
- **A faint dotted continuation** of the original incident ray straight through the block, so the **lateral displacement** between it and the emergent ray is visible, with a small double-headed arrow labelling that gap. The emergent ray must be drawn genuinely parallel to the incident ray — compute it, do not eyeball it.
- **Speed annotations:** a short label on the ray in air reading "faster", on the ray in glass reading "slower", on the emergent ray "faster again". These three words carry the whole explanation.
- **Generator parameters:** `incidentAngle` (so the same generator can emit 20°, 40°, 60° versions, and the angles are computed rather than drawn by hand), `showNormals: boolean`, `showAngleLabels: "both" | "i-only" | "none"`, `showDisplacement: boolean`, `medium: "glass" | "water" | "perspex"` changing the computed bend and the label. Use a fixed refractive index internally to compute the geometry, but **never print it** — it is beyond CCEA.
- Accessibility: normals dashed, rays solid with arrowheads, tint by `fill-opacity`; `<title>` describing entry, exit and the parallel emergent ray.

**Second figure: the prism and the spectrum.** `viewBox` about `0 0 940 420`.

- An equilateral triangular prism, apex up, tinted.
- A single white ray entering the left face at an angle, with a **dashed normal at that face** and the angles marked.
- Inside the prism, the ray already fanned into a narrow band.
- At the right face, a **second dashed normal**, and the band leaving and spreading further, reaching a screen at the right edge.
- **Seven rays drawn separately**, labelled at the screen end in order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet, with **red deviated least (nearest the straight-through direction) and violet most**. The order on the screen is itself an examiner finding, so it must be labelled, not merely coloured — and because colour cannot be the only signal, each band is labelled in text.
- A caption band under the screen reading "spectrum" with a small note: the prism has not added anything; it has separated what was already there.
- **Generator parameters:** `rays: number` (7 or 2 — a two-ray red/violet version is much clearer for the first teaching pass), `showNormals`, `label: "names" | "letters" | "blank"`, `apexUp: boolean` so the **inverted prism** from the Summer 2025 Higher question can be generated and the learner has met it before the paper does.

**Third figure, and it may be the most valuable: the P5 results graph.** `viewBox` about `0 0 700 520`.

- Axes: **angle of incidence / degrees** on the x-axis, 0 to 80, and **angle of refraction / degrees** on the y-axis, 0 to 50. Both labelled with the quantity and the unit, gridlines every 10, five small squares per labelled step.
- **Plotted points** at the angles a sensible P5 range would use — 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 — with a **smooth curve** through them that passes through the origin and bends over.
- **A dashed straight line through the origin** drawn on the same axes, clearly diverging from the curve at high angles, labelled "what proportional would look like".
- A caption: *as the angle of incidence increases the angle of refraction increases, but not in proportion — the graph is a curve, not a straight line through the origin.*
- **Generator parameters:** `points: [i, r][]` from real computed values, `showProportionalLine: boolean`, `showCurve: boolean` (off, so she draws it), `missingAngle: number | null` — set this to 25 to reproduce the Booklet B situation where a candidate omitted 25° from their range, and ask what is wrong with the set of readings.

This third figure answers two separate recorded examiner findings at once and there is no equivalent anywhere on the open web, because no other board's practical asks for this plot.

## 4. Practicals CCEA also examines

**Prescribed Practical P5** is the core: ray box (or laser and single slit) and a rectangular glass block on paper, marking the ray in and out, removing the block, joining the marks, drawing the normal and measuring both angles with a protractor for a range of incident angles.

| Variant | Where it comes from | What changes | Why CCEA could set it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vary the **substance**, not the angle | AQA 8463 **Required practical activity 9** ("the refraction of light by different substances") | one fixed angle of incidence, blocks of glass, perspex and a water cell | "Suggest how you would find out which material refracts light most" is a natural Booklet B part, and it isolates the change-of-speed idea |
| **Optical pins** instead of a ray box | Traditional UK method, still standard in CCEA centres | the ray is located by lining up two pins either side | A "why use four pins rather than two?" question tests the same precision reasoning as "why repeat readings?" |
| **Semicircular block** | Edexcel and AQA separate-physics practicals | ray enters along a radius so it is undeviated at the curved face | Beyond CCEA as a required method, but it is the cleanest way to show the flat-face refraction alone. Worth one aside, not a section |
| **Ripple tank over a submerged plate** | IOPSpark | water waves, not light | Makes wavefronts visible; CCEA examines refraction as a wave property, so this is legitimately on-spec background |
| **Rainbow from a hose or a glass of water** | Everyday | dispersion plus internal reflection | Good hook, but the internal reflection part is beyond CCEA — keep it to a sentence |

The transferable **practical-skills** demands CCEA does examine, all of which our items should carry: choosing a sensible range and interval of angles (and noticing a missing value), measuring from the normal rather than from the surface, repeating and taking a mean, and drawing a **smooth curve** rather than joining points with straight segments.

## 5. Question types the other boards use that CCEA also rewards

1. **"Complete the path of the ray" [2–3], on a printed diagram.** Universal across boards. The mark-scheme habit: **one mark for the correct bend at the first boundary, one for the correct bend at the second, one for the emergent ray parallel to the incident ray.** Our `graph`-style drawing parts are the closest fit; failing that, use a `choice` over four computed diagram options where the distractors are (a) bent away at entry, (b) not bent at all, (c) bent towards at both boundaries. Every distractor is then a real candidate error.
2. **"Explain why the ray changes direction as it enters the glass" [2].** One mark for the speed change, one for the consequence. Author the rejects to include "optical density", "the glass is denser" alone and "the light reflects", and make the feedback say which half is missing. This is the item that carries the Unit 7 finding.
3. **"Describe the relationship shown by the graph" [2–3].** The mark scheme wants a direction *and* a statement about proportionality: as `i` increases, `r` increases; the increase gets smaller / the graph is a curve / they are not proportional. AQA and Edexcel run the same shape on every practical. A bald "they both go up" is one mark.
4. **"Name the process / explain why the white light splits" [2].** Different colours travel at different speeds in the glass, so they are refracted by different amounts. The one-word answer is **spectrum**, and "rainbow" is the recorded wrong answer — so one gate must ask for the word and reject the other.
5. **Range-and-interval planning [1–2].** "A student measured the angle of refraction at 10°, 20°, 30°, 40° and 50°. Suggest one improvement to the set of readings." Edexcel and AQA both set planning questions of this shape on Core/Required practicals; CCEA examines it directly in Booklet B.

## 6. Misconception framings worth borrowing

| Misconception | The framing that fixes it | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Dispersion and refraction are the same thing | Dispersion **is** refraction — applied seven times, once per colour. One rule, seven answers | our Summer 2025 P2 Foundation finding |
| The answer is "rainbow" | The word on the answer line is **spectrum**. A rainbow is a thing that happens in the sky; a spectrum is what a prism produces | our Summer 2025 P2 Foundation finding |
| "Optical density" explains the bend | It names a property; it does not explain anything. The explanation is that the light **changes speed** | our Summer 2025 Unit 7 Booklet A finding; Edexcel CP4.10 and AQA both word it as speed |
| Angles are measured from the surface | Every angle in optics is measured from the **normal**. Draw the normal first, always | our Summer 2025 P2 Higher finding (normals missing) |
| The ray bends away from the normal on entering glass | Slower → towards. Faster → away. Two words, and the marching model makes it a consequence rather than a rule to memorise | our Summer 2025 P2 Higher finding (bent the wrong way) |
| `i` and `r` are proportional | Plot it and lay a straight line through the origin beside the curve. They separate visibly by 60° | our Summer 2025 Unit 7 findings |
| The prism adds colour to white light | It separates what was already there. Newton's recombination experiment — a second prism putting the spectrum back together into white — is the one-sentence proof | classical; the Wellcome image in §7 shows exactly this |
| Violet is at the top of the spectrum | Order is by how much each colour is slowed: red least, violet most. The order is the answer, so label it, do not rely on colour | our Summer 2025 P2 Higher finding (order wrong) |
| Light slows down because the glass "pushes" it | Do not open this; at GCSE the change of speed is the given. Say "light travels more slowly in glass" as a fact and spend the time on the consequence | scope discipline |

## 8. Scope note

**Beyond CCEA — `notonspec` if used:**
- **Snell's law, refractive index and any calculation with n** (Edexcel 1PH0 and AQA separate physics; CCEA's `mustRecall` says explicitly that Snell's law is not required)
- **critical angle and total internal reflection**, optical fibres, endoscopes
- wave-front diagrams as an assessed skill (AQA Trilogy HT only)
- colour by absorption, transmission and filters (AQA 8463 §4.6.2.6) — a different idea from dispersion, and easy to import by accident
- the rainbow's full explanation (refraction **plus** internal reflection plus a second refraction)
- specular versus diffuse reflection as named terms
- why light travels more slowly in glass

**CCEA-only, so no borrowed resource will cover it:**
- **dispersion of white light by a prism** in its entirety, including "red refracted least, violet most"
- the P5 plot of angle of incidence against angle of refraction and the "related but not proportional" conclusion
- the inverted-prism diagram from the Summer 2025 Higher paper
