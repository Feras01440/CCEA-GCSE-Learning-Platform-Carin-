# Enrichment dossier — c2-electrolysis-molten-salts

**CCEA** Double Award Science, **C2** §2.7 Electrochemistry · outcomes 2.7.1, 2.7.2 (F), **2.7.3 (H)** · tier mixed · difficulty 4 · no prescribed practical on this outcome
**CCEA must-recall:** electrolysis is the **decomposition** of a molten or dissolved ionic compound by passing electricity through it; the electrolyte conducts because **ions move and carry charge**; **inert (graphite)** electrodes; **anode positive, cathode negative**. Molten **LiCl**: lithium (silvery liquid) at the cathode, chlorine (pale green gas) at the anode. Molten **PbBr₂**: lead (silvery bead) at the cathode, **bromine (red-brown vapour)** at the anode. **Higher:** half equations — `Li⁺ + e⁻ → Li`; `Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb`; `2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻`; `2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻`; and for aluminium `Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al`, `2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻`.
**Compiled** 20 September 2026 · 30 minutes
**Headline** The best-matched chemistry topic in this batch — AQA §5.4.3.1, §5.4.3.2 and §5.4.3.5; Edexcel CC3.22 to CC3.27 — so the borrowed material is abundant and correctly pitched. The marks CCEA records losing are three very specific ones, and none is conceptual: the definition must contain the word **"decomposed"**; bromine is a **red-brown vapour** at the **anode**; and the Unit 7 answer needs **"in a fume cupboard"** because the products are toxic. All three are vocabulary and safety, so the lesson's weight belongs on exact wording, not on the chemistry.

## 2. Teaching angles worth recreating (in our own words)

1. **"Decomposed" is the word the mark scheme wants.** *CCEA Summer 2025 C2 Foundation records exactly this: the definition must include "decomposed" (by electricity).* Not "split", not "broken down by electricity" — though those are the right idea, the marked word is **decomposition**. Give her the sentence once, in full, and make her type it in a `blank` gate: *electrolysis is the decomposition of a molten or dissolved ionic compound by passing electricity through it.*

2. **Molten is the whole trick: the ions have to be free to move.** *Both boards make this the opening idea (AQA §5.4.3.1; Edexcel CC3.22).* In a solid ionic lattice the ions are locked in place, so nothing can carry charge to the electrodes. Melt it and the ions are free, so the compound conducts — and can be taken apart. Teaching *why melting matters* means "molten or dissolved" is a consequence rather than a phrase, and it connects straight back to `c1-ionic-and-molecular-structures`.

3. **Opposites attract — that is the whole of "which electrode".** *The single most useful reasoning step, and it removes the need to memorise two lists.* The **cathode is negative**, so it attracts the **positive** ions — the metal. The **anode is positive**, so it attracts the **negative** ions — the non-metal. Every product prediction in the topic follows from that one sentence, including aluminium.

4. **A mnemonic is available, and it should be a check rather than the method.** *Common in both boards' teaching material.* **PANIC** — Positive is Anode, Negative Is Cathode. Worth giving, but only after angle 3, so she has a reason as well as a rhyme.

5. **Name the product's appearance, because CCEA asks for it.** *CCEA Summer 2025 C2 Higher records bromine needing to be described as a **red-brown gas or vapour** at the anode.* The appearances are examinable: lithium a silvery liquid, chlorine a pale green gas, lead a silvery bead, bromine a red-brown vapour. No other board asks for these, so they must be authored from CCEA's own wording and drilled.

6. **Half equations: balance the atoms, then balance the charge with electrons.** *Edexcel CC3.27 and AQA §5.4.3.5.* At the cathode, positive ions **gain** electrons (so electrons appear on the left); at the anode, negative ions **lose** them (so electrons appear on the right). The reliable method is: write the ion, write the product, balance the atoms, then add electrons to whichever side makes the total charge equal. Doing it in that fixed order means `2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻` is derived rather than recalled.

7. **The fume cupboard is a mark.** *CCEA Summer 2025 Unit 7 Booklet B: toxic-gas electrolysis must be done in a fume cupboard, and the anode must be labelled.* Chlorine and bromine are toxic. Safety here is not a general caution but a specific, markable answer tied to a specific product — so it belongs in the method, beside the product it protects against.

## 4. Practical variants CCEA also examines

No prescribed practical is tagged to this outcome, but electrolysis of molten lead bromide is a standard demonstration and CCEA examines its method points in Unit 7.

| Variant | Source | What is seen | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Molten lead(II) bromide** | the classic school demonstration | silvery bead at the cathode, red-brown vapour at the anode | CCEA's own named example, and the appearances are marked |
| **Molten lithium chloride** | CCEA's second named example | silvery liquid and pale green gas | Same reasoning, different products |
| **Electrolysis of copper sulfate solution** | **Edexcel Core Practical CC3.31**; AQA §5.4.3.4 | aqueous, so water is involved | **Beyond CCEA** — recognise it in borrowed material and cut it |
| **Testing whether the solid conducts, then the melt** | standard | no current, then current | The best possible demonstration of angle 2, and a good "explain the observation" item |

**The Unit 7 demands CCEA marks here:** labelling the **anode** correctly, stating that the apparatus must be in a **fume cupboard** because the gas is toxic, using **inert** electrodes and saying why, and writing the anode half equation.

## 5. Question types the other boards use that CCEA also rewards

1. **"Define electrolysis" [2].** The recorded failure; the word **decomposition** is a mark. Encode as a `text` part with a key-word group requiring it, and a reject for "splitting" alone.
2. **"Explain why molten lead bromide conducts electricity but solid lead bromide does not" [2].** The ions are free to move when molten.
3. **"Name the product at each electrode and describe its appearance" [4].** CCEA's own detail; two marks per electrode.
4. **"Write the half equation for the reaction at the anode" [2].** Higher. Marks for the correct species and for the electrons on the correct side, balanced.
5. **"Why must this electrolysis be carried out in a fume cupboard?" [1].** The product is toxic — name the gas.
6. **"Why are graphite electrodes used?" [1].** They are inert, so they do not react with the products.
7. **Find-the-mistake:** a labelled cell diagram with the electrodes swapped. Straight from the Unit 7 finding.

| Misconception | The framing that fixes it | Evidence |
|---|---|---|

| Bromine described as a liquid or as brown only | **Red-brown gas or vapour**, at the **anode** | **CCEA S2025 C2 Higher** |
| Anode and cathode mislabelled | The cathode is negative and attracts positive ions. Derive it, then check with PANIC | **CCEA S2025 Unit 7 Booklet B** |
| Fume cupboard omitted | Chlorine and bromine are toxic; the safety point is tied to the named product | **CCEA S2025 Unit 7 Booklet B** |
| Electrons drawn moving through the electrolyte | **Ions** carry the charge through the melt; electrons move only in the wires | AQA §5.4.3.1 and Edexcel CC3.24's framing |
| Half equations written without electrons | Balance the atoms, then add electrons until the charge balances. The charge column shows it | Edexcel CC3.27 |
| Electrons on the wrong side | Cathode = gain (electrons on the left); anode = loss (electrons on the right) | the two-step builder |
| Reaching for aqueous electrolysis | Beyond CCEA. Molten compounds only, plus aluminium in the next topic | AQA §5.4.3.4; Edexcel CC3.30–CC3.31 |
